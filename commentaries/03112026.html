Liturgy:
(1 Thessalonians I, 6-10)
1 Thessalonians I, 6 — 'ἐν θλίψει πολλῇ' — 'en thlipsei polle' — in much affliction; in great tribulation; under many afflictions. In what, in what situation, under what circumstances.

1 Thessalonians I, 7, 8 — 'ἐν τῇ Μακεδονίᾳ καὶ ἐν τῇ Ἀχαΐᾳ' — 'en te Makedonia kai en te Akhaia' — in Macedonia and Achaia. Where.

1 Thessalonians I, 8 — 'ἀφ’ ὑμῶν' — 'aph hymon' — from you. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates here that the Word of the Lord does not originate from the Thessalonians, yet they shared it generously with others as a gift. It echoes the recent Gospel reading for the Apostles: Freely you have received, freely give. 'ἐν παντὶ τόπῳ' - 'en panti topo' — in every place; everywhere. Where.

1 Thessalonians I, 9 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν εἰδώλων' — 'apo ton eidolon' — from idols. The preposition 'apo' emphasizes that the faithful completely, entirely, utterly turned away from idols and idol worship.

1 Thessalonians I, 10 — 'ἐκ τῶν οὐρανῶν' — 'ek ton ouranon' — from the heaven. The preposition 'ek' here indicates from where we await the Second Coming of Christ, that is, Himself. 'ἤγειρεν ἐκ τῶν νекρῶν' - 'egeiren ek ton nekron' — raised from the dead. The preposition 'ek' here indicates from where the transition of Christ occurred — from the dead unto life. 'ἐκ τῆς ὀργῆς τῆς ἐρχομένης' - 'ek tes orges tes erkhomenes' — from the wrath to come. The preposition 'ek' here instead of 'apo' indicates not merely deliverance itself, but the exit from a state that evokes God's wrath, pointing out that this wrath is deserved, whereas the exit from it is, therefore, God's saving gift.

The Apostle Paul recounts (reminds of) the history of the Thessalonians' conversion and their early times, as well as the ministry of the apostles among them (such as it was everywhere). He speaks of faith and comfort from it, and most of all — from the Holy Spirit, Who is glorified through faith.
The wrath to come anthropomorphically denotes God's Last Judgment — just as human wrath is directed toward overcoming obstacles, so God's judgment will separate the saved from the ungodly and unrepentant (this is the sense of the comparison; at the same time, it does not imply emotionality or passionateness in God's judgment — by no means!).
If the apostles had sought glory from men (here named in the sense of the primary motive of actions, the goal), they could not have sought God's things with all their heart, nor could they have so cared for salvation and possessed the necessary zeal, or worse — they could have drawn away from Christ to the point of opposing Him Himself. It is not a matter of diminishing the significance of glory among men, but of seeing its limitations in comparison with God's glory, as well as in the integrity of the apostles' ministry and faith, in fulfilling the commandment of love for God in fullness. It must also be remembered that among pagans especially, glory received from them may have sinful roots, which is why in reality it can even be a manifestation of sins, and not a cause of true joy, but of sorrow. In this latter meaning, the explanation of the words is self-evident.
The reading concludes with words of love and attachment. For a person, their soul is of supreme value, therefore to give one's soul out of love is spoken figuratively of extraordinary love.

(Luke XI, 1-10)
Luke XI, 1 — 'ἐν τῷ εἶναι' — 'en to einai' — as he was; while. That is, when; during. 'ἐν τόπῳ' - 'en topo' — in a certain place. Where.

Luke XI, 2 — The words our, Who is in heaven are absent in many manuscripts.

Luke XI, 4 — 'εἰς πειρασμόν' — 'eis peirasmon' — into temptation; into trial. In Ancient Greek, temptation and trial/testing are the exact same word. See in more detail regarding the Lord's Prayer and the meaning of its conclusion in particular at the links concerning the parallel passage in Matthew VI:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/21022026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/01032025.html

Luke XI, 5 — 'ἐξ ὑμῶν' — 'ex hymon' — which of you. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates here from where the choice is made — from among the group of the faithful.

Luke XI, 6 — 'ἐξ ὁδοῦ' — 'ex hodou' — out of the way. That is, whence?.

Luke XI, 7 — 'εἰς τὴν κοίτην' — 'eis ten koiten' — in(to) bed. Here 'eis' is used in the sense of a certain indefiniteness as to where precisely these children are right now, that is, it indicates the general time rather than the exact physical location (indeed, the friend does not ask where the host's children are currently situated). The host does not want to open not because the children are in bed, but because they are resting, and it is already a late hour. One can also understand it another way: as the merging and shortening of a complete phrase into a single preposition. The full phrase implies the children have already gone to bed (hence, prepared for sleep or fallen asleep, everything is proceeding in its own way according to its own logic) and are currently there. Then it concerns the fact that this man has other designs and plans regarding his friend's request.

Regarding prayer, in particular the Lord's Prayer: in Luke the version differs from Matthew's, and in some manuscripts a portion of the words is omitted.
The section concerning importunity is present only in Luke, yet it is quite eloquent. I would note that there were indeed certain relations (friendship) between the host and the one asking, but the words emphasize that they can be far from ideal, yet one must ask nonetheless.
