Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians I, 12-20)
2 Corinthians I, 12 — 'ἐν ἁγιότητι καὶ εἰλικρινίᾳ τοῦ θεοῦ, οὐκ ἐν σοφίᾳ σαρκικῇ ἀλλ’ ἐν χάριτι θεοῦ' — 'en hagioteti kai eilikrinia tou theou, ouk en sophia sarkike all en khariti theou' — in the holiness and sincerity of God, not in fleshly wisdom, but in the grace of God; by the holiness and purity of God, not by fleshly wisdom, but by the grace of God. Both translation options are possible; the first indicates what is the foundation of the life of the faithful, and the second — what is the driving force and incentive. 'ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ' - 'en to kosmo' — in the world. Where. See also at the link a fine dialogue with Google Gemini Flash-3.6 about the translation:
https://gemini.google.com/share/901c8daff8ce

2 Corinthians I, 14 — 'ἀπὸ μέρους' — 'apo merous' — in part; partially; partly. Set expression. 'ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ' - 'en te hemera tou kyriou hemon Iesou' — in the Day of our Lord Jesus. During the Second Glorious Coming of Christ. When.

2 Corinthians I, 16 — 'εἰς Μακεδονίαν, καὶ πάλιν ἀπὸ Μακεδονία' — 'eis Makedonian kai palin apo Makedoni' — into Macedonia and again from Macedonia. A typical pair of prepositions 'eis'-'apo', which here emphasizes a change of location. 'εἰς τὴν Ἰουδαίαν' - 'eis ten Ioudaian' — into Judea. Where to.

2 Corinthians I, 17 — 'ἐλαφρίᾳ' — 'elaphria' — frivolity, inconstancy (as a human characteristic). 'παρ’ ἐμοὶ τὸ ναὶ ναὶ καὶ τὸ οὒ οὔ;' - 'par emoi to nai nai kai to ou ou?' — with me Yea, Yea, and Nay, Nay? The preposition 'para' in its form before a following vowel here is with the dative case and indicates that it is a matter of deeply personal motives, plans, aspirations, and the like, which may differ from what the apostle preaches openly. In other words, it is about the sincerity and complete openness of the apostle regarding the preaching of Christ — he means precisely what he says, and speaks all he can, without harboring secret additional motives. Formally, here is a direct parallel with Christ's Sermon on the Mount, where there is the same phrase, but here another meaning is intended than that of which Christ speaks. Therefore Christ commands faithfulness and truthfulness in words, humility, while Paul speaks of openness and transparency. Also such a construction is used by the Apostle James in the same sense as Christ (see: James V, 12). See more about these places with another meaning at the links:

2 Corinthians I, 19 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'en hymin' — in you. Where, among whom. 'ἀλλὰ ναὶ ἐν αὐτῷ γέγονεν' - 'alla nai en auto gegonen' — but Yea was in Him. Here further the apostle draws a parallel with the words of Christ in the Sermon on the Mount and means that God was fully manifested in Christ, that this is undeceptive and true, desirable and life-giving.

2 Corinthians I, 20 — 'ἐν αὐτῷ' — 'en auto' — in Him. In Whom.

Paul calls a good event — arrival — grace. This shows that the word grace in Scripture does not always mean a purely spiritual divine action, but can also refer to God-given events or states (hence the ancient Christian wish, often forgotten now: I wish you God's graces!).

By asking about frivolity, Paul shows what fleshly plans are (a term he uses in various cases; here it is most clearly seen what he means). The flesh does not possess the fullness of gifts, in particular knowledge and wisdom, confidence, firmness, and the like, so when a person is uncertain, when their position can easily be changed, inconstant, unstable, changeable, then these are fleshly plans.

To show how spiritual thoughts differ from fleshly plans, Paul emphasizes this in the form of words about in Christ was Yea and Amen! God's promises are not like human fleshly plans; God is faithful and true. If a person unites with God, then they also cease to be fleshly in this sense, but become a spiritual person (according to the measure of power and depth, sincerity, integrity, and fullness of union).

(Matthew XXII, 23-33)
Matthew XXII, 23 — 'Ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ' — 'en ekeine te hemera' — on that day. When.

Matthew XXII, 25 — 'παρ’ ἡμῖν' — 'par hemin' — with us. The preposition 'para' in its form before a following 'he' here is with the dative case and indicates here that these brothers were (according to the Sadducees' words; many consider the story invented, although there is nothing fantastic and unreal in it) among those Sadducees.

Matthew XXII, 28, 30 — 'ἐν (γὰρ) τῇ ἀναστάσει' — 'en (gar) te anastasei' — (for) in the resurrection. That is, after the general resurrection of the dead. 'ὡς ἄγγελοι θεοῦ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ εἰσίν' — 'hos angeloi theou en to ourano eisin' — are as angels of God in the heaven; as it were angels of God in the heaven. That is, the state of people after the general resurrection of the dead is similar to the state of God's angels in the heaven. This applies to all people; it is about the state of nature. At the same time, this does not mean that people will feel the same as God's angels, since the feelings of God's angels depend very much on the grace of God. People will have a state of blessedness or judgment and in varying degrees depending on the union with God and its degree, form, and depth.

Matthew XXII, 31 — 'τὸ ῥηθὲν ὑμῖν ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'to rhethen hymin hypo tou theou' — that spoken to you by God. The preposition 'hypo' here is with the genitive case and indicates here the passive voice of the participle spoken.

After the resurrection there is no belonging of people to anyone except God, no marital relations, so the question whose wife shall she be cannot be an obstacle to faith in the resurrection of the dead. It must also be said that according to common faith, despite the absence of the mentioned state of the present age, spiritual closeness in relationships in a marriage that is saved will be preserved in a special way.

The dispute of the Sadducees with Jesus about the resurrection of the dead (the Sadducees were rationalists, did not believe in the general resurrection of the dead, unlike the Pharisees and Christians). It also has significance for understanding the Sacrament of Matrimony.

The example of the Sadducees (probably invented, although theoretically possible; it was intended by them to emphasize the problematic nature of their logic regarding the resurrection of the dead) relies on the Old Testament law of levirate marriage. This law provided the possibility for the next elder brother after the deceased to raise up seed unto his brother through marital relations with his childless widow. Such was not considered sinful, because it was necessary for the Israeli people to be preserved until the Coming of Christ. After His Coming, this law lost its force and is not in effect now (Christians do not recognize levirate marriage as permissible now).
