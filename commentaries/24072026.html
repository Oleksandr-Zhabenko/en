Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians XI, 8-22)
1 Corinthians XI, 8 — 'ἐκ γυναικός' — 'ek gynaikos' - from woman. 'ἐξ ἀνδρός' - 'ex andros' - from man. The preposition 'ek' ('ex' before a vowel) indicates origin or source.

1 Corinthians XI, 9 — 'διὰ τὴν γυναῖκα' — 'dia ten gynaika' - through the woman; for the sake of the woman; regarding the woman. 'διὰ τὸν ἄνδρα' - 'dia ton andra' - through the man; for the sake of the man; regarding the man. Detailed explanation below.

1 Corinthians XI, 10 — 'διὰ τοὺς ἀγγέλους' — 'dia tous angelous' - for the sake of the angels.

1 Corinthians XI, 11 — 'γυνὴ χωρὶς ἀνδρὸς' — 'gyne khoris andros' - woman apart from man; woman without man. 'ἀνὴρ χωρὶς γυναικὸς' - 'aner khoris gynaikos' - man apart from woman; man without woman. 'ἐν κυρίῳ' - 'en kyrio' - in the Lord. In both clauses, the first rendering (apart from) better conveys that God did not design the sexes to exist in isolation or alienated from one another. The only justification for separation would be spiritual struggle against temptation or sin.

1 Corinthians XI, 12 — 'ἡ γυνὴ ἐκ τοῦ ἀνδρός' — 'he gyne ek tou andros' - the woman [is] from the man. See below. 'ἀνὴρ διὰ τῆς γυναικός' - 'aner dia tes gynaikos' - the man [comes] through the woman. Here, in contrast to earlier examples, the preposition dia governs the genitive case, pointing to the instrumental mode of origin—man being born of woman. This bears two levels of meaning: first, the natural birth of men from women; second, by virtue of the spiritual design expounded below, it points simultaneously to three profound truths—Christ and the Church as His Body, the birth of Christ from the Virgin Mary, and the creation of Eve from Adam's body. 'ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ' - 'ek tou theou' - from God. Here the preposition 'ek' indicates ultimate origin at the deepest levels of nature, hypostasis, and personhood. See more below.

1 Corinthians XI, 13 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν αὐτοῖς' — 'en hymin autois' - among/within yourselves. Where.

1 Corinthians XI, 17 — 'εἰς τὸ κρεῖσσον' — 'eis to kreisson' - for the better; unto the greater. 'εἰς τὸ ἧσσον' - 'eis to hesson' - for the worse; unto the lesser. The preposition 'eis' here denotes the objective/outcome toward which their gatherings lead.

1 Corinthians XI, 18, 19 — 'ἐν ἐκκλησίᾳ' — 'en ekklesia' - in Church; in the assembly. 'ἐν ὑμῖν' - 'en hymin' - among you. Where.

1 Corinthians XI, 21 — 'ἐν τῷ φαγεῖν' — 'en to phagein' - in eating; during the meal. When.

1 Corinthians XI, 22 — 'εἰς τὸ ἐσθίειν' — 'eis to esthiein' - to eat in. 'ἐν τούτῳ' - 'en touto' - in this; for this. Paul speaks with pastoral gentleness — substituting I do not praise you for direct rebuke, and employing rhetorical questions to awaken conscience rather than issue raw assertions.

Paul continues addressing spiritual realities that present a challenge to modern interpreters. For example, his reference to angels in this context remains an enigmatic detail. Nevertheless, difficulty in comprehension is no grounds for dismissing canonical Scripture.

Paul affirms that sexual differentiation is divinely ordained and inherently good, as is all of God's creation. He stresses that neither sex exists independently of the other; they are mutually necessary. Building upon yesterday's reflection:

Why God created man first and woman afterwards remains a divine mystery. Yet Paul asserts that this sequence is not accidental or arbitrary, but foundational to God's providence for humanity. While one might recall that from the Father all fatherhood is named, it is vital to remember that all genuine good — including motherhood — originates from the Father: Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and comes down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow of turning (James I, 17). Since motherhood is a good gift and a perfect gift, it likewise descends from the Father. For further details, see:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/07012026.html

Care must be taken to avoid misinterpreting Paul's phraseology.

To address a common misconception: the statement that woman was created from man / through man does not imply ontological inferiority, lesser value, or subjugation. It references the historical reality of Eve being formed from Adam's rib — from his living body. God's plan for humanity unfolded in Eve through Adam, forming a clear archetype of Christ and the Church. Here is visible a prototype [prefiguration] of Christ and the Church as the Body of Christ. The creation of Eve from the body of Adam is a prototype [prefiguration] of the fact that the Church will become the Body of Christ, Who will give Himself up for her, for her salvation. If in the relationship of a man and a woman they are united with Christ and the Church, then the prototype [prefiguration] of Christ and the Church is revealed manifestly. Eve is not a secondary byproduct or mere extension of Adam; God creates her from his body as an prefiguration of the Church. Conversely, the Son of God is born of the Virgin, taking flesh by the Holy Ghost and of the Virgin Mary. Adam was formed from the dust of the ground, while Eve was formed from living human flesh. This reveals that humanity is both the crown of physical creation and called into Eucharistic union as the Body of Christ. While the text addresses order ('exousia'), it contains no notion of despotic rule. Indeed, Paul notes that woman covers her head because of the angels, grounding the practice in spiritual nature and cosmic order rather than earthly authority. See also:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/14072026.html

From this perspective, practical customs become coherent. If short hair naturally aligns with masculine modes of thought and long hair with feminine modes, such distinctions are fitting, though external norms may adapt without disrupting the underlying principle.

Created for the sake of / regarding ('dia' + accusative) does not imply any hierarchy or comparison of value between man and woman. The statement that woman was created for/because of/with a view to man likewise does not mean she is less important, less significant, or inferior. Rather, it concerns God’s original design for humanity and how its distinct parts function within a whole. Consider an architectural analogy — drawing from the theological concept of Divine Economy (oikonomia, God's building and ordering of creation): If you design a house and orient the kitchen windows toward the south (or north in the Southern Hemisphere) so that it receives ample light throughout the day, and then arrange the remaining rooms with respect to that orientation, this by no means implies that the kitchen is superior or more valuable than the rest of the house. While the kitchen holds primary functional importance for preparing meals, bedrooms take precedence when it comes to rest and sleep. Each room possesses its own distinct purpose within the unity of the whole structure.

Similarly, in God's divine economy, sequential creation and relational orientation reflect purposeful, complementary roles within a single human vocation, rather than a scale of ontological worth (This sentence is added by Google Gemini Flash-3.6 during translation, I left it here in full).

(Matthew XVII, 10-18 )
Matthew XVII, 11 — 'ἀποκαταστήσει' — 'apokatastesei' - will restore. This signifies that John the Baptist would awaken the people's hearts, rekindle spiritual expectation, and call them to repentance — re-establishing the foundational disposition required for true communion with God.

Matthew XVII, 12 — 'ἐν αὐτῷ' — 'en auto' - to him; with him. The preposition 'en' highlights what was committed against John. 'ὑπ’ αὐτῶν' - 'hyp auton' - by them. The preposition 'hypo' (elided before a vowel) governs the genitive case, emphasizing the Savior's deep humility and showing that while these men acted as instruments of sin, through the Incarnation they also became instruments of Christ's voluntary suffering.

Matthew XVII, 15 — 'εἰς τὸ πῦρ' — 'eis to pyr' - into the fire. 'εἰς τὸ ὕδωρ' - 'eis to hydor' - into the water.

Matthew XVII, 18 — 'ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'ap autou' - from him. The preposition 'apo' indicates the total, definitive departure of the evil spirit from the person. 'ἀπὸ τῆς ὥρας' - 'apo tes oras' - from that hour; from then on. A set phrase denoting an immediate and complete transformation.

This passage connects John the Baptist to the prophesied Elijah, foretells Christ's Passion, and accounts the healing of the demon-possessed youth. John the Baptist was not Elijah hypostatically (Elijah remained bodily in heaven during John's earthly ministry), but John operated in the spirit, power, and zeal of Elijah, fulfilling the prophecy of the First Coming. Scripture and Holy Tradition also affirm that Elijah the Tishbite will appear personally before the Second Coming to preach repentance. That John was not Elijah in person is further evidenced at the Transfiguration, where Elijah appeared from heaven alongside Moses (from Abraham's bosom), rather than John.

Regarding the healing, the father lacked full faith yet did not despair, bringing his son directly to Jesus. Christ's piercing words address both the father's spiritual condition and the disciples' lack of faith, which prevented them from working the cure. Christ's emotive response reflects His desire to halt the spread of unbelief and foster genuine faith. Out of abundant mercy and to establish faith, He heals the boy.
