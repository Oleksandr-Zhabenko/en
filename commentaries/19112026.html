Liturgy:
(2 Thessalonians II, 13-III, 5)
2 Thessalonians II, 13 — 'ἠγαπημένοι ὑπὸ κυρίου' — 'egapemenai hypo kyriou' — beloved of the Lord / loved by the Lord. The preposition 'hypo' here takes the genitive case and indicates the passive voice of the participle (be)loved (by Whom). 'ἀπ’ ἀρχῆς' - 'ap arkhes' — from the beginning. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates the starting point of time reckoning. 'εἰς σωτηρίαν ἐν ἁγιασμῷ πνεύματος καὶ πίστει ἀληθείας' - 'eis soterian en hagiasmo pneumatos kai pistei aletheias' — to salvation through sanctification of the Spirit and belief of the truth / for salvation in sanctification of the Spirit and true faith; for salvation by sanctification of the Spirit and true faith. All translation options are possible. The words regarding election can only be understood properly as a parallel to the calling and election of the apostles. It was not the apostles' decision that made them apostles, but God's decision, after which they were able to respond with consent, accepting this divine decision. Naturally, without the consent and action of each of them, none would have become an apostle; yet what is underlined here is that without God's decision, none of them would have become an apostle. As Christ says: You have not chosen Me, but I have chosen you, and ordained you. At the same time, a different Greek word is used for God's election concerning the apostles. Here, however, a rare word is employed that is close in meaning to to give preference / to take as firstfruits. One may think that the subsequent words, as well as the very fact of several letters being addressed to the Thessalonians, testify to the significance of their community in the activity of the Apostle Paul; therefore, the use of the idea of election by God is interwoven with the thought of the special role of the Church in Thessalonica regarding the matters about which the epistle is written. Overall, this signifies that the Thessalonians, through the Epistle and the labour of the Apostle Paul, took a special part—providentially prepared for them by God—in resolving the issues under discussion. More broadly, these words hold significance concerning certain particular events.

2 Thessalonians II, 14 — 'εἰς ὃ' — 'eis ho' — whereunto / to which. The preposition 'eis' here indicates by sense the direction of the action. 'εἰς περιποίησιν' - 'eis peripoiesin' — to the obtaining / for obtaining. Here the preposition 'eis' indicates the goal of the action.

2 Thessalonians II, 16 — 'ἐν χάριτι' — 'en khariti' — in grace; by grace. Both translation options are possible.

2 Thessalonians II, 17 — 'ἐν παντὶ ἔργῳ καὶ λόγῳ ἀγαθῷ' — 'en panti ergo kai logo agatho' — in every good word and work; by every good work and word. Both translation options are possible and complement one another.

2 Thessalonians III, 2 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ἀτόπων καὶ πονηρῶν ἀνθρώπων' — 'apo ton atopon kai poneron anthropon' — from unreasonable and wicked men / from people out of place and evil. The preposition 'apo' indicates complete and total deliverance.

2 Thessalonians III, 3 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ' — 'apo tou ponerou' — from evil / from the evil one. Meant here is evil in its results, in the final outcome, hence it may also be cunning / deceitful (deceptively good). Analogous to the previous.

2 Thessalonians III, 4 — 'ἐν κυρίῳ' — 'en kyrio' — in the Lord. That is, the Lord Himself is hope, yet He is also a safe Haven for hope, an Abode of peace and certainty.

2 Thessalonians III, 5 — 'εἰς τὴν ἀγάπην τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ εἰς τὴν ὑπομονὴν τοῦ Χριστοῦ' — 'eis ten agapen tou theou kai eis ten hypomonen tou CHristou' — into the love of God, and into the patient waiting [endurance] of Christ. The preposition 'eis' here indicates the direction of orientation.

The Apostle Paul wishes for the Thessalonians to be established and comforted by God, and also asks them for their prayers.
I will pause on verse III, 5 — The Lord direct your hearts — means He will act by grace upon human hearts so that they draw closer to God's (the Father's) love and Christ's patience. At the same time, human hearts remain free; God neither takes away nor diminishes human freedom, but prompts, gives, strengthens, fills, endows, enlightens, directs, and so forth.
Paul also gives thanks to God for the faith and faithfulness of the Thessalonians, praises them, and instructs them to hold fast to what was handed down from the apostles. That which was handed down from the apostles is called Apostolic Tradition.

(Luke XIII, 1-9)
Luke XIII, 1 — 'ἐν αὐτῷ τῷ καιρῷ' — 'en auto to kairo' — at that very season / at that same time. Set expression.

Luke XIII, 2, 4 — 'παρὰ πάντας' — 'para pantas' — above all / to the proximity with all. The preposition 'para' here takes the accusative case; in sense it denotes something not entirely clear-cut, encompassing both comparison and juxtaposition, and at the same time inclusion within the group of all Galileans or all inhabitants of Jerusalem. On the one hand, it aptly conveys the doubts of those asking the question, and on the other, possible avenues of reasoning which nevertheless remain problematic, as is clearly and thoroughly written below.

Luke XIII, 4 — 'ἐν τῷ Σιλωὰμ' — 'en to Siloam' — in Siloam. 'ἐν Ἱερουσαλήμ' - 'en Hierousalem' — in Jerusalem. Where.

Luke XIII, 6 — 'ἐν τῷ ἀμπελῶνι αὐτοῦ' — 'en to ampeloni autou' — in his vineyard. Where. 'ἐν αὐτῇ' - 'en aute' — thereon / on it.

Luke XIII, 7 — 'ἀφ’ οὗ' — 'aph hou' — these [three years] / since. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following 'hou' indicates the reckoning of time from the moment of which the master speaks. 'ἐν τῇ συκῇ ταύτῃ' - 'en te syke taute' — on [specifically] this fig tree.

Luke XIII, 9 — 'εἰς τὸ μέλλον' — 'eis to mellon' — in the future [time] / into that which is to come. Points to the future, that is, orientation toward it — fruit is expected in the future.
Many people commit the very errors against which Christ warns here. At first glance, it seems that Christ, while calling to repentance, frightens with punishment. Yet if one looks attentively at the original text, it becomes evident that the meaning is different.
1. …That they were sinners above... — in the original, the preposition ‘παρά’ (para) with the Accusative — which can mean both above / beyond (over and above) and to the proximity with, like, in comparison with. In the first case, Christ rejects the way of thinking that those people suffered bodily because of their excessive sinfulness, and in the second — that they suffered just like everyone else, because they are sinful, like all (we are all sinful, therefore they suffered). He decisively and unambiguously says, No!
Why? After all, both explanations seem entirely proper, just, and pious, as though reflecting the action of God's righteous retribution.
Yet…
2. Suppose a person reasons according to the first pattern:
Those people suffered because they are more sinful than… others. And what about us? As for us — we are less sinful, therefore... we have not suffered. Logical?
Yet read the parable of the Publican and the Pharisee (which appears further on in the text) several times, and you will see pure… Pharisaism. Instead of repentance, such a way of thinking leads to the growth of pride, self-satisfaction, Pharisaism, and condemnation of others. Moreover, it is problematic from a purely logical standpoint as well: for if, following this same logic, one further compares those who perished with other people who are not us, yet who likewise did not suffer, one arrives at the dubious assertion that the victims were themselves to blame, whereas others may in reality be far more sinful than those who perished...
3. Let us now consider the second option. Indeed, all people are sinful. Yet this cannot explain the perishing of particular individuals among them whilst others do not perish. For then we would have selective justice from God… and that is certainly not the case. God does not punish sinners selectively (I will punish these so that others may fear), because the unpunished would then be far more inclined to believe in their own impunity than in their future punishment, having already departed from a pure conscience and from God. Such reasoning leads to a sense of certainty in one's own chosenness, and in effect entrenches one in pride.
It becomes clear why the answer is No!
What then? A universal call to repentance. And the parable of the fig tree… in a vineyard. Yet the master planted it there. Is this not an image of how a person enters a situation that is not naturally favourable for them, yet the Lord begins to care for them in a special way — digging around it, applying fertiliser... So too with a person who distances themselves from God into natural conditions unfavourable to them: God cares for them so that they may return to Him, yet does not completely block the operation of those conditions. And what becomes of a person depends to a great extent on the person themselves. It is far better not to enter such conditions in the first place. Returning to those who perished in the Gospel — God took care that they should not perish, yet their conduct and circumstances proved naturally unfavourable, and they perished.
