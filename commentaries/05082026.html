(2 Corinthians I, 1-7)
The reading of the 2nd Epistle of the Apostle Paul to the Corinthians on weekdays begins. From the text of the letter it becomes clear that there were more epistles to the Corinthians; this is not actually the second in order, but only two have been preserved, of which this is the second. An Epistle rich in theology (factually, like all the others).

2 Corinthians I, 1 — 'τῇ ἐκκλησίᾳ τοῦ θεοῦ τῇ οὔσῃ ἐν Κορίνθῳ' — 'te ekklesia tou theou te ouse en Korintho' — to the Church of God which is in Corinth. Where. 'σὺν τοῖς ἁγίοις πᾶσιν τοῖς οὖσιν ἐν ὅλῃ τῇ Ἀχαΐᾳ' - 'syn tois hagiois pasin tois ousin en hole te Akhaia' — with all the saints who are in all Achaia. Where. The Epistle is addressed not only to the Corinthians themselves.

2 Corinthians I, 2 — 'ἀπὸ θεοῦ πατρὸς ἡμῶν καὶ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ' — 'apo theou patros hemon kai kyriou Iesou CHristou' — from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that grace and peace are God's gifts to people.

2 Corinthians I, 3 — 'οἰκτιρμῶν' — 'oiktirmon' — of mercies, of compassions (in the original in the plural).

2 Corinthians I, 3 — 'παρακλήσεως' — 'parakleseos' — of comfort, of encouragement, of consolation. This word in various forms is key in today's reading. Here in the singular.

2 Corinthians I, 4, 6, 7 — 'παρακαλῶν' — 'parakalon' — comforts, consoles, encourages. 'παρακαλεῖν' - 'parakalein' — to comfort, to be able to comfort, console. 'εἰς τὸ δύνασθαι' - 'eis to dynasthai' — so that [we] may be able. The preposition 'eis' indicates here the purpose of the action. 'τοὺς ἐν πάσῃ θλίψει' - 'tous en pase thlipsei' — those in any affliction (tribulation). Whom in what. 'παρακλήσεως' - 'parakleseos' — by comfort, by consolation, by encouragement. 'παρακαλούμεθα αὐτοὶ ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ' - 'parakaloumetha autoi hypo tou theou' — ourselves comforted, consoled by God. The preposition 'hypo' here is with the genitive case and indicates here the passive voice of the participle comforted, consoled.

2 Corinthians I, 5 — 'ὅτι καθὼς περισσεύει τὰ παθήματα τοῦ Χριστοῦ εἰς ἡμᾶς, οὕτως διὰ τοῦ Χριστοῦ περισσεύει καὶ ἡ παράκλησις ἡμῶν' — 'hoti kathos perisseuei ta pathemata tou CHristou eis hemas, houtos dia tou CHristou perisseuei kai he paraklesis hemon' — for as the sufferings of Christ abound in us, so through Christ our comfort (consolation) also abounds. Here it is not simply about an increase, but about overflowing, abounding, going beyond boundaries and limits. That is, it is not about every suffering, but about the endurance of the faithful thanks to Christ, that is, when the faithful in sufferings unite with Christ, then also their sufferings overflow with God's grace, the comfort of the Holy Spirit, the comfort of Christ.

2 Corinthians I, 6 — 'ἐν ὑπομονῇ τῶν αὐτῶν παθημάτων ὧν καὶ ἡμεῖς πάσχομεν' — 'en hypomone ton auton pathematon hon kai hemeis paskhomen' — in the patient enduring of the same sufferings which we also suffer; by the patient enduring of the same sufferings which we also suffer. The sufferings of Christ here are in another sense — these are those sufferings of the faithful for the sake of faithfulness to Christ, which Christ accepts upon Himself as His own (according to the Gospel, so it is), which are also shared through co-participation in the ministry and love of Christ by the apostles, but also by the faithful. That is, suffering, as well as grace and comfort, are common here.

2 Corinthians I, 7 — 'κοινωνοί' — 'koinonoi' — partakers, co-participants.

In general, about suffering and communion with Christ, see the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/renewal-of-Peter.html

Therefore now I will dwell on the fact that shared sufferings and their enduring, as well as shared comfort and consolation, bring people closer and make them partakers even more.

And also on what is meant in verse 5. Christ co-suffers with the faithful, and by this in their sufferings His sufferings multiply. But when the faithful think about Christ, His co-suffering and love give them comfort; this is spiritual comfort, it comes from the Holy Spirit, the Comforter. Therefore Christ's co-suffering with the faithful grants them comfort, consolation, encouragement. The very remembrance of that, the memory of that is already a certain comfort, but also there is an important meaning here that this additionally prompts the Holy Spirit to comfort people. One can say that Christ co-suffers with the faithful, this consoles them, but also the Holy Spirit comforts the faithful, so as together with them to also comfort Jesus Christ.

I will also say about verse 6. Why are the sufferings of the apostles (in general, of the saints) for the comfort of the faithful (to a certain extent)? Because the apostles and saints in sufferings manifest examples of virtues by which they inspire and comfort the faithful in their sufferings, if the latter relate with faith and love to that. God's comfort, God's encouragement in the Holy Spirit exceeds suffering and compassion. Therefore the faithful remember the examples of the sufferings of the saints, because in them there is more strengthening and encouragement than sorrow from suffering.

Why the consolation of the apostles (saints) is for the comfort of the faithful — this is easier to understand, because rejoicing together is a great comfort.

The apostles are a ground for boasting (a reason for boasting) for the faithful, as a wonderful model for them, and truly faithful people likewise are a boasting for the apostles; more generally — good students are a boasting for teachers.

(Matthew XXI, 28-32)
Matthew XXI, 28 — 'ἐν τῷ ἀμπελῶνι' — 'en to ampeloni' — in the vineyard. Where.

Matthew XXI, 31 — 'τίς ἐκ τῶν δύο' — 'tis ek ton duo' — which of the two. The preposition 'ek' here indicates a selection from a group of two people. 'εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ' - 'eis ten basileian tou theou' — into the Kingdom of God. Where to.

Matthew XXI, 32 — 'ἐν ὁδῷ δικαιοσύνης' — 'en hodo dikaiosynes' — in the way of righteousness; by the way of righteousness. Rather the second variant, as it is clearer grammatically. Indicates the manner of action, how.

The parable of the two sons. By sons are understood different people who have two different attitudes toward God's will, the will of the Father.

The question Who has accomplished [it]? concerns the final result, since it was decisive. In the parable, the consent / disagreement in the sons is connected with the so-called self-identification of a person — how a person connects themselves with God, and how important this is in their eyes for themselves. Consent means that a person considers themselves a believer and pious; to a certain extent for them this matters, they themselves perceive themselves as such. And disagreement — on the contrary — means that a person themselves does not want to connect themselves with God, at least at some stage of their life, that is, moves away from God and moves toward sin or directly in sins.

Instead, here further we see the importance of repentance. Sinful people who moved away from God and did not consider themselves righteous and pious repented largely through the preaching of John the Baptist, the preaching of repentance, whereas those who considered themselves pious and righteous did not want to do this; their own self-esteem hindered them. Instead, they received a second call to repentance — through seeing the conversion of obviously sinful people, but even that they did not want to do themselves, because they did not want to see their sins. And therefore, in the end, what will prompt them to repentance? And if God's will is precisely repentance, because all have sinned, then consequently, they did not fulfill God's will.

I draw attention to the fact that from the parable it in NO WAY follows that for salvation it is better first to be a sinful person. Yes, in the parable sinful people repented. But for this was needed the preaching of John the Baptist (in history — a unique case; if more generally, then a significant push to repentance was needed). And if a person does not feel such a push? What if sin altogether closes their vision? Then their state is even worse than that of the one who agreed but did not go.

Therefore the parable teaches primarily repentance, teaches to be open to conversion and repentance (not to search for some special pushes), as well as humility — a true vision of one's state and readiness for correction. (Matthew XXI, 43-46)
Matthew XXI, 43 — 'ἀφ’ ὑμῶν' — 'aph hymon' — from you. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates here that what is taken away will be completely, entirely, altogether taken.

Matthew XXI, 46 — 'εἰς προφήτην αὐτὸν εἶχον' — 'eis propheten auton eikhon' — for a prophet they held Him; perceived Him as a prophet; took Him for a prophet. The preposition 'eis' indicates here into what image came the perception of Jesus by people, that is, what the perception of Jesus by others BECAME.

Here omitted is the reading of the parable of the wicked vinedressers, which is read another time. This depicts the depth of the fall of those who rejected Jesus, and today read is the conclusion—that Jesus, being the Lord, will not remain despised.

Those who planned evil regarding Jesus feared the people, therefore acted to a certain extent secretly.

It must be said that by another nation here are named former Gentiles who will become Christians.
