Liturgy:
(Philippians II, 16-23)
Philippians II, 16 — 'εἰς καύχημα' — 'eis kaukhema' — that I may rejoice / for a praise. The preposition 'eis' here indicates the goal of the action. 'εἰς ἡμέραν' - 'eis hemeran' — for the day. That is, the full boast of the apostle will be realised only on the Day of the Second Coming of Christ. 'εἰς κενὸν' - 'eis kenon' — in vain; for naught. Set expression.

Philippians II, 19 — 'ἐν κυρίῳ' — 'en kyrio' — in the Lord. In Whom.

Philippians II, 22 — 'εἰς τὸ εὐαγγελίου' — 'eis to euangelion' — for the Gospel. The preposition 'eis' here indicates a shared goal.

The Apostle Paul testifies to his sacrificial ministry and also commands us to rejoice with him. See more on this at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/12102026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/20102025.html

Paul also writes of his intention to send Timothy (who likewise took a direct part in writing the letter), who was a good co-worker of Paul and even to a certain extent resembled a spiritual son to the apostle of the Gentiles. He characterises him as a splendid fellow-servant. Such a characterisation is rendered all the more vivid by the fact that many did not apply as much diligence as Timothy applied in order to labour in the field of serving Christ (verses 20-21).

To seek one's own means to care for one's own temporary interests in this life, neglecting in one degree or another the need to serve God, to serve Christ. One can put it otherwise: if God desires something, He desires it prior to the person themselves and calls them to the very same thing, but then it cannot in any way be said that this is the person's own, but rather God's. Whereas if one speaks of one's own, then God's is denied, and consequently it concerns something displeasing to God.

Paul awaits the resolution of an important matter concerning himself, and then he will be able to send Timothy, whose help was very valuable for Paul here.

(Luke VI, 37-45)
Luke VI, 38 — 'εἰς τὸν κόλπον' — 'eis ton kolpon' — into the bosom. Where to.

Luke VI, 39 — 'εἰς βόθυνον' — 'eis bothynon' — into the ditch / into a pit. Where to.

Luke VI, 41, 42 — 'ἐν τῷ (ἰδίῳ) ὀφθαλμῷ' — 'en to (idio) ophthalmo' — in the (own) eye. Where.

Luke VI, 42 — 'ἐκ τοῦ ὀφθαλμοῦ' — 'ek tou ophthalmou' — out of the eye. From where (from within the eye).

Luke VI, 44 — 'ἐκ τοῦ ἰδίου καρποῦ' — 'ek tou idiou karpou' — by its own fruit; from its own fruit. From what, through what, according to what, that is, it indicates the substantiation and reasonable cause. 'ἐξ ἀκανθῶν' - 'ex akanthon' — of thorns. 'ἐκ βάτου' - 'ek batou' — of a bramble bush. Here in both cases — from where. If one looks not at the literal direct sense of the parable, it speaks of the fact that good deeds in an evil person are not properly their own, but rather originate from God.

Luke VI, 45 — 'ἐκ τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ θησαυροῦ' — 'ek tou agathou thesaurou' — out of the good treasure (on the whole, in the outcome). 'ἐκ τοῦ πονηροῦ' - 'ek tou ponerou' — out of the evil (in the outcome, on the whole). 'ἐκ γὰρ περισσεύματος' - 'ek gar perisseumatos' — for of the abundance.
