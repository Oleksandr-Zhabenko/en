Liturgy:
(Ephesians I, 22-II, 3)
Ephesians I, 22 — 'ὑπὸ τοὺς πόδας αὐτοῦ' — 'hypo tous podas autou' — under His feet. Here the preposition 'hypo' is used with the accusative case. A parallel to the well-known prophecy of Ezekiel, where he saw the Son of Man with feet representing His human nature. Here it signifies that the Son of God, having become man, did not cease to be God and Ruler of all.

Ephesians I, 23 — 'τὰ πάντα ἐν πᾶσιν' — 'ta panta en pasin' — all things in all; all (creatures/things) to all. Both translation options are possible.

Ephesians II, 2 — 'ἐν αἷς' — 'en hais' — wherein. 'ἐν τοῖς υἱοῖς τῆς ἀπειθείας' - 'en tois hyiois tes apeitheias' — in the children of disobedience. Where, among whom. See the explanation for the next verse below.

Ephesians II, 3 — 'ἐν οἷς' — 'en hois' — among whom. 'ἐν ταῖς ἐπιθυμίαις τῆς σαρκὸς ἡμῶν' - 'en tais epithymiais tes sarkos hemon' — in the lusts of our flesh; by the lusts of our flesh. Rather the first option, although the second is also possible, emphasizing deep involvement in these lusts. Here this passage needs deeper examination. The word nature here, as in Romans XI, 11-24 (especially verses 21-24), does not mean human nature proper. Here the word is used in the sense of what a person represents (particularly to others), how a person lives, what they bring with them. Factually, it is connected significantly more with actions than with nature. By the way, St Gregory Palamas' distinction between nature and energies, and the explanation of their interrelation, is closely connected with these passages, as well as with the famous passage in the Catholic Epistle of the Apostle Peter on partakers of the divine nature (2 Peter I, 4).

Thus, here nature of people in the Apostle Paul refers to how they live and conduct themselves. It recalls the famous ancient aphorism — Habit is second nature. One can say Gentiles are shown here as those connected with disobedience, who commit evil and incur wrath regarding this, while simultaneously being God's creatures and even named children of God (just as Adam is juxtaposed as God's son in the Gospel according to Luke). Indeed, according to God's design, people (all) are called to be God's children according to the image of Christ through life in the Holy Spirit, through union with the entire Most Holy Trinity, but the estrangement of people by their sins, vices, impenitence, and the like is depicted simultaneously by such a meaningful expression.

Christ Is Risen!

God became Man.
And one can distinguish with the mind,
what is of God, and what is human.
But it is now utterly impossible
here to divide and to say:
God is separate from man,
or man is without God,
or that God is contrary to man,
whilst man is at odds with God.

Contemporaries saw a man,
but all that He did was also of God,
holy and true, good, life-giving.

And God arose, Christ is risen,
and human nature is truly named
the power of God, the beauty of God, the wisdom of God,
although it was suffering, exhausted, despised.
And the power of God is revealed through human nature,
and the weakness of God became the strength of men.

God is not a state, nor a status, nor a title,
He is not a role, nor a mode of action,
He is not life, nor even existence,
but He is the Living One, unsearchable,
invisible, uncontainable,
who became man without ceasing
to be God; remaining invisible,
He became visible; being intangible,
He becomes comprehensible, sensory, Whom
one can touch.
It is not man who ascended, who somehow acquired
God, a status, powers, feelings,
or anything similar, but God descended,
as the Indescribable, the Unrepresentable,
and became Man, became Jesus Christ.

And now forever, unto all ages,
wondrously He remains Man.

And the acts of God did not replace human acts,
The human soul and mind of Christ
know that they are simultaneously of God.

And even that which Christ did not do
as good people often do,
He did Sacramentally, as the God-Man,
becoming the Bridegroom of the Church, the King,
and much else besides,
for it pleased Him that in Him
should dwell all the fullness
of what is of God, done by Man,
of what is human, as gifted by God.

And today we join
the universal glorification,
by which nature praises,
magnifies the Risen Christ.

Also, verses Ephesians I, 22-II, 3 signify that the Church thus became the fulness (completion, a kind of 'final destination of movement', the Son of God will never leave her) of the Son of God through His human nature, particularly His body, but also He as God grants fulness, meaning, and significance to everything, fills everything with gifts, endows everything with what it is (that is, the nature of all creation is in the closest, most intimate way connected with the Son of God, Jesus Christ, and depends on Him), and this happens most fully not even in creation and providence (though these hold a primary place in the whole divine dispensation), but in the Incarnation and the salvation of the world.

In chapter II, Paul speaks of the state of creation and humans prior to union with Christ — a state of death and the dominion of the devil through the impossibility of completely overcoming and shaking off sins. The dwelling place of evil spirits, together with their leader, the devil, named prince (princes do not hold absolute authority), is named the air (given that they do not dwell in the spiritual heaven, having been cast down from there; also humans live surrounded by air, so air here represents the environment of human life).

Verse 3 says that those who accepted Christ at a mature age previously lived similarly to the Gentiles, in an environment alongside humans and demons (factually confirming the thought that air is the environment of life not only for demons, but also for humans). Factually, the Church here stands in opposition to this very environment of life (which can also be called death) of humans and demons, in the air.

(Mark X, 46-52)
Mark X, 46 — 'εἰς Ἰερειχώ' — 'eis Hiereikho' — to Jericho. 'ἀπὸ Ἰερειχώ' - 'apo Hiereikho' — from Jericho. The preposition 'apo' indicates that they were going out of Jericho, leaving it. 'παρὰ τὴν ὁδόν' - 'para ten hodon' — to the proximity with the road; alongside the road. The preposition 'para' here is used with the accusative case, indicating in sense that he sat near the road to be clearly visible to passers-by who gave him alms.

Mark X, 52 — 'ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ' — 'en te hodo' — in the way / on the way.
