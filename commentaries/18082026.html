Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians V, 15-21)
2 Corinthians V, 16 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν' — 'apo tou nyn' — from now on. Set expression, effectively an adverb. The preposition 'apo' indicates here the starting point of time upon an important change.

2 Corinthians V, 17 — 'ἐν Χριστῷ' — 'en CHristo' — in Christ. In Whom. Indicates unity with Christ, which renews a person.

2 Corinthians V, 18 — 'τὰ δὲ πάντα ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'ta de panta ek tou theou' — and all things are from God. The preposition 'ek' indicates here that God is the Source of all good, that God is the Creator, and that creation reflects the qualities of the Creator, which is why one can come to know God through His creation. This does not refer to actions, events, states, or the like, because, for example, sins, evil, and perishing are not from God. Here I will also quote regarding recent reading:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/16082026.html
Luke X, 22 — 'ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός' — 'hypo tou patros' — by the Father. Here the preposition 'hypo' is used with the genitive case and indicates the passive voice of the participle delivered. It carries two meanings here — delivery irrespective of the Incarnation, and delivery regarding the human nature of the already Incarnate Son of God. Both in Divinity the Son of God receives everything from the Father, of Whom He is begotten eternally, and according to human nature He also receives everything from the Father. The Incarnate Son of God as man possesses all God's gifts that are provided by God for people — particularly He is King, Prophet, High Priest, Christ, Savior, Deliverer, Conqueror, and the like. It must also be understood that some of these gifts of God are not properly those that God would have wished the Son of God to have when He created the world and people; for example, God did not want people to sin when He created them, but knew as Omniscient that they would sin. Then He sent the Son to save them, and thus, as Savior, Deliverer, Who suffered for us and the like, the Son of God became as man, receiving these gifts also in view of what happened to creation, which God did not want for creation. 2 Corinthians V, 19 — 'ὡς ὅτι θεὸς ἦν ἐν Χριστῷ κόσμον καταλλάσσων ἑαυτῷ' — 'hos hoti theos en en CHristo kosmon katallasson heauto' — namely, that God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto Himself; God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself; God was in Christ reconciling the world by Himself. Various translation options are possible, carrying great depth of meaning. The dative case can also sometimes be translated as an instrumental, which it replaces. If understood purely literally and directly, it refers to God the Father being in Christ Jesus, thereby reconciling the world to Himself. But Christ Himself as a Hypostasis cannot be removed from this. For it is Christ Who suffered and rose, He Who is the Judge and Savior, He Who is the High Priest, King, Prophet, and the like. His voluntary fulfillment of God's will regarding the salvation of humanity — a will common to the entire Most Holy Trinity — led to the Redemption of the human race from sin and death. The work of salvation, the work of reconciliation and reunion with God, is the work of the Father in the Son, but also the work of the Son, a single work of the Father, the Son —
and importantly to add — and the Holy Spirit. Here is a kind of proof of the unity of Christ with the Father — they have a single work, which is possible in full measure only in the case of a single, common Divine nature and essence. Had Christ not been consubstantial with the Father (and the Spirit), Their work would not be truly one, and it would be impossible to say that the Father reconciled the world to Himself in Christ, and then say that Christ reconciled the world to the Father by Himself. The unity and singularity of the work of Christ, the Father, and the Spirit is the brightest testimony to the consubstantiality of the Most Holy Trinity (see in particular the prophecies My glory will I not give to another! in Isaiah 42). 'θέμενος ἐν ἡμῖν τὸν λόγον τῆς καταλλαγῆς' — 'themenos en hemin ton logon tes katallages' — committed unto us the word of reconciliation. In whom. That is, He established that the faithful preach reconciliation with God in Christ, preaching Christ Himself.

2 Corinthians V, 21 — 'ἵνα ἡμεῖς γενώμεθα δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ' — 'hina hemeis genometha dikaiosyne theou en auto' — that we might become the righteousness of God in Him. In Whom. The words about sin point to the crucifixion, as in another place the Apostle Paul explains by recalling the Old Testament decree concerning a curse (but not sin!). It is appropriate here to see parallels with the expulsion of Adam and Eve from Paradise, as well as with the Annunciation of the Mother of God. Christ knew no sin just as the Most Pure Virgin Mary knew no man; that is, Christ was not in unity with sin, just as the Virgin was not in bodily unity with a man. Regarding Adam and Eve, the Lord said to Adam: Cursed is the ground for thy sake; thorns and thistles shall it bring forth to thee… till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return. These words regarding Adam were wondrously fulfilled in Christ! He received the crown of thorns on His head; His hands were pierced with nails (there is a prophecy that one cannot lean one's hand on a thistle, for it pierces it). Christ here became a curse, taking from Adam his being dust/ground. That is, Christ accepted all the consequences of sin, conquered sin and death through them, and granted victory to humanity.
I will also add a dialogue with Google Gemini Flash-3.6:
Still, the apostle distinguished between sin and its consequences (particularly the curse). Why does he use sin here instead of curse? The logic would break down if replaced with Him Who knew no curse.
Google Gemini Flash-3.6 answered:
Replacing sin with curse in 2 Corinthians V, 21 would indeed destroy Apostle Paul's theological and rhetorical logic. By using the exact word sin (ἁμαρτία), Paul deliberately reaches several deep levels:
1. Rhetorical and ontological parallelism
Paul builds a strict symmetrical contrast:
Him Who knew no sin (τὸν μὴ γνόντα ἁμαρτίαν) — radical sinlessness;
made Him to be sin (ἁμαρτίαν ἐποίησεν) — radical acceptance of the human condition;
that we might become righteousness (γενώμεθα δικαιοσύνη) — radical justification and divinisation.
Had Paul written made Him to be a curse (as he does in Galatians III, 13, where it is purely about the Law and its punishment), the direct antithesis with the righteousness of God would be lost. A curse is a consequence, whereas sin is contrasted with righteousness at the level of states of being themselves.
2. Old Testament linguistic background: Sin offering (ḥaṭṭā'āh)
In Old Testament Hebrew, the word חַטָּאָה / חַטָּאת (ḥaṭṭā'āh) has a dual meaning:
Sin / guilt proper;
Sin offering (the sacrificial rite where the animal becomes sin for the community; see Leviticus IV, 24, IV, 29, V, 9, where the Septuagint translates ḥaṭṭā'āh simply as ἁμαρτία).
For Paul, who thought in categories of Old Testament worship, made Him to be sin meant that God the Father set Christ as a living Sin Offering. Upon Him was placed the state of sinfulness of all humanity, although He Himself remained absolutely pure.
3. The boundary between being a sinner and becoming sin
Paul does not say that God made Christ a sinner (ἁμαρτωλός) — that would be an error and contradict the Savior's sinlessness.
He says that God made Him sin (ἁμαρτία) in a personified, corporate sense. Christ on the Cross embodied within Himself all human estrangement from God, personifying human sin as such, in order to crucify it in His Body, destroy it, and conquer it in the Resurrection.
4. Why curse is logically insufficient here
A curse is a legal and existential consequence of Violation.
Sin is the cause itself, the ontological disease and rupture with God.
Had Christ accepted only the curse (the consequence), it would be an external settling of a debt. By taking upon Himself sin (the cause), He heals human nature at its very root, granting in return not merely the removal of punishment, but the righteousness of God.
I wrote:
Sin is connected with loss — for example, a lack of good. Then the animal offered as a sin offering is itself, as it were, a loss, a sin.
Google Gemini Flash-3.6 answered:
5. Sin as privatio boni (privation/loss of good)
In Patristic tradition (from St Basil the Great and St Augustine to St John of Damascus), sin and evil have no ontological essence (hypostasis) of their own.
Sin is privatio boni — the deprivation, lack, distortion, or loss of good, just as darkness is merely the absence of light, and illness is the loss of health.
The etymology of the Greek word ἁμαρτία (hamartia) itself is a miss, failing to hit the target, that is, the loss of the true direction, loss of the fullness of being and of the connection with the Source of Life.
6. The sacrificial animal as materialized loss and sin
When a person sins, they create an ontological breach — a loss of good and of the fullness of being. The sin offering (ḥaṭṭā'āh) in Old Testament worship acted as a compensatory and simultaneously symbolic instrument:
Loss as a price (material dimension): By offering the best, unblemished animal from their herd, a person felt a real, tangible loss of their own resource, wealth, and labour. This visible loss corresponded to the invisible loss of their soul's or moral state.
Embodiment of sin itself (symbolic-substitutionary dimension): During the laying on of hands upon the victim's head (Leviticus IV, 4), an act of existential transfer occurred: the animal absorbed this deficit, this destruction and guilt of the person. It personified sin itself (that is, the lost good and present evil) and became sin that had to be destroyed (through the shedding of blood and burning outside the camp) in order to restore the violated balance and purity.
7. Culmination in the Golgotha Sacrifice
Your thought sheds additional light on why Apostle Paul says in 2 Corinthians V, 21 that God made Christ sin (ἁμαρτία):
Christ, in taking on human flesh, voluntarily enters the zone of our loss — into a world lacking the glory of God, subjected to corruption, suffering, and death.
On the Cross, He becomes that solitary Loss and Sin Offering, taking upon Himself the absolute deficit of human existence — godforsakenness, pain, and death itself (My God, My God, why hast Thou forsaken Me?).
Yet because He Himself is the Fullness of Life and Righteousness, by His loss He swallows up human loss (death is swallowed up in victory, I will add here). He fills the lack of good with His Uninexhaustible Good, transforming absence into the Fullness of the Resurrection.

(Mark I, 16-22)
Mark I, 16 — 'παρὰ τὴν θάλασσαν τῆς Γαλιλαίας' — 'para ten thalassan tes Galilaias' — by the sea of Galilee. The preposition 'para' here is with the accusative case and indicates that Christ approached the shore of the sea (lake). 'ἐν τῇ θαλάσσῃ' - 'en te thalasse' — in the sea. That is, they themselves were in the sea (in a boat) or on the shore, but the nets were in the sea. Where.

Mark I, 19, 20 — 'ἐν τῷ πλοίῳ' — 'en to ploio' — in the ship. Where.

Mark I, 21 — 'εἰς Καφαρναούμ' — 'eis Kapharnaoum' — into Capernaum. Where to. 'εἰς τὴν συναγωγήν' - 'eis ten synagogen' — into the synagogue. Where to.

Here it speaks of what particularly amazed people at the beginning (and throughout the entire duration) of Jesus' preaching — He taught as One having authority and power. Here I will now add that God at the beginning is compared to a human who seeks to inflict pain on someone else or even kill that person. Such a comparison regarding God, Who is Love and Righteousness, can be built only on opposition and contrast: Look, God is fundamentally and completely Other, Holy!. Therefore, from the point of view of morality and relationships, God is incomparably better and fundamentally Other, Holy; and from the point of view of power and authority — He has all power, unrestrained by anyone, to act as He desires.

The passage itself points to God's participation in these events. People, feeling the fear of persecution for Christ, for faith, can forget about God's participation, about God Himself. And then a person is left with significantly less strength to overcome such fear of suffering and even death. But in these words, God as if comes alive, becomes a living Participant in events. For God is not an idea, an abstract Mind or Force, but the Living Creator, Savior, King, and Provider, Loving and even Sacrificial. Confession (ὁμολογέω + ἐν) requires a preposition, because it is a union, an abiding in someone. It is not merely an external statement, but a state of immersion and spiritual interpenetration.
Denial (ἀρνέομαι + Accusative) occurs directly, without any preposition. Denial is not abiding in something — it is simply an act of rupture, pushing away, and external rejection of the object.
Interestingly, in Greek there was no natural construction for denying in someone, but even if one could be artificially created, it would be theologically false: it is impossible to abide inside Christ and at the same time deny Him. Denial is always a stance from the outside.
