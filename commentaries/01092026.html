Of New Year (Indiction):
(1 Timothy II, 1-7; Luke IV, 16-22)
1 Timothy II, 1 — four types of prayer are listed. It can be said that they differ in the manner of execution and purpose. 'δεήσεις' — 'deeseis' — supplications (prolonged attentive prayer-petition); 'προσευχάς' - 'proseukhas' — prayers (distinguished by high spirituality); 'ἐντεύξεις' - 'enteuxeis' — intercessions (petitions for sinful people); 'εὐχαριστίας' - 'euCHaRISTiaS' — thanksgivings (particularly also the liturgy, the Eucharist). The entire prayer life of the Church can be attributed to these four categories. See also at the link:
https://www.instagram.com/p/DYJ4551jdJY/?img_index=1

1 Timothy II, 2 — 'πάντων τῶν ἐν ὑπεροχῇ ὄντων' — 'panton ton en hyperokhe onton' — for all those who are in authority / pre-eminence; for all those who are higher. An important generalization can be seen here. For example, one can pray for teachers — because they are pre-eminent in education; for the military — because they are pre-eminent in war; for workers — because they are pre-eminent in labour; for scientists — because they are pre-eminent in the search for knowledge; and so on and so forth. That is, in fact, one can pray for everyone in the understanding that they are pre-eminent in something, since they bear greater responsibility in that. 'ἐν πάσῃ εὐσεβείᾳ καὶ σεμνότητι' - 'en pase eusebeia kai semnoteti' — in all godliness and dignity; in all godliness and honour.

1 Timothy II, 7 — 'εἰς ὅ' — 'eis ho' — whereunto / for which. The preposition 'eis' indicates the goal here. 'ἐν πίστει καὶ ἀληθείᾳ' - 'en pistei kai aletheia' — in faith and verity; by faith and truth. Both translation options are possible.

Verse 5 must be understood in the sense that Christ is both God and human being — He possesses two natures, which is why He can be the Mediator in the salvation of humanity. It must be understood that one cannot conceive a middle nature between Divine and human, between uncreated and created, for it could not possess the qualities of both as its proper properties. Therefore Christ is not a Being with a nature in between God and human being. He is called a man to emphasize that He is (besides being God) also perfect man. Since salvation consists in the union of the human being with God, the true Mediator unites in Himself two natures — of God and of human being — in One Single Person, Hypostasis, Jesus Christ.
I will delve deeper regarding the interpretation here. The Apostle Paul does not speak of the Holy Spirit because he is writing not about the Trinity, not about God as the main Focus of attention, but about God's actions regarding creation, about God's Revelation to the world. In such cases, the apostles often do not write everything that can be said about God; in particular, they may omit one of the Hypostases, speak of Christ's human nature, give additional information, etc. So it is here. This is not an exhaustive exposition of what can be said about the Father and the Son, and even more generally — about God. In particular, in other places the Apostle Paul directly calls the Son God, and the Holy Spirit — indirectly. Other apostles complement this with other passages.
It must be said that no single passage of Scripture is an exhaustive exposition of dogmatics and faith in general, even if it obviously looks like a solemn confession of common faith. This is because an exact complete confession of faith was not an indispensable condition for conversion to Christ and for a person to become faithful. In particular, many martyrs are known who became saints without having confessed or articulated the entire faith of the Church. But when it comes to the faith of the Church, nothing prevents making very detailed and broad descriptions of faith. Later Councils forbade changing the Creed, but cannot forbid deepening the revealed faith.
Therefore, in general, there is nothing surprising that the Apostle Paul here writes to his disciple Timothy not everything that both of them accept and believe.
Paul accents the thought that later terminology formulates as just stated above — either a Mediator can be by nature (a middle nature between God and human being), which is logically impossible, or a Mediator can be by Hypostasis (the Single Hypostasis of the Son of God — perfect God and perfect human being) — which is what is in reality. Someone might say that one can conceive a Mediator not by nature and not by hypostasis, but, for example, by functions, by deeds; but then healing of human nature does not occur, but only, perhaps, of deeds or functions. If nature has been damaged, then, as the Fathers of the Church justly say, it is nature itself that requires renewal, and therefore mediation that touches human nature. Therefore the Mediator is Human Being by nature. But not only Human Being. Just above in verse 3 Paul implicitly calls Christ Savior and God. Consequently, the Mediator is also God. And together — the God-man Jesus Christ.

(2 Corinthians XII, 20-XIII, 2)
2 Corinthians XIII, 2 — 'εἰς τὸ πάλιν' — 'eis to palin' — again. Set expression.

Paul writes that distrust towards the apostles leads the community into various divisions, quarrels, and anarchy, and this in turn to falls into various sins. Until the apostle can arrive to them, distrust can already have far-reaching consequences, which Paul would have to eradicate in sorrow and humiliation (like a person who did not achieve success in what he strove for).

Further Paul speaks of how he will restore discipline in the community and will not be lenient.

(Mark IV, 24-34)
Mark IV, 24 — 'ἐν ᾧ μέτρῳ' — 'en ho metro' — with what measure. That is, by what (instrumental case).

Mark IV, 25 — 'ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'ap autou' — from him. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that it will be taken away entirely, completely.

Mark IV, 28 — 'ἐν τῷ στάχυϊ' — 'en to stakhyi' — in the ear; upon the ear. It refers to the belonging of grains to the ear, that is, to its structure and setup. Where.

Mark IV, 30 — 'ἐν τίνι αὐτὴν παραβολῇ' — 'en tini auten parabole' — in what parable; by what parable. That is, Christ chooses the words and plot of parables carefully, so that there is very much spiritual meaning in them.

Mark IV, 31 — 'κόκκῳ' — 'kokko' — (to a) grain / kernel — from 'κόκκος' - 'kokkos' — grain, kernel, seed.

Mark IV, 32 — 'ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ' — 'hypo ten skian autou' — under the shadow of it. That is, in the shade of the tree. The preposition 'hypo' here is used with the accusative case and indicates under what the fowls of the air lodge.

Next comes a unique parable found only in Mark.

The man is similar to the Kingdom of God; the seed is what the Lord wants people to do for the Kingdom of God while living in this age; the earth that brings forth fruit represents the people who have accepted what the Lord wants and fulfill it; the reapers are the angels, and the harvest is the Second Coming of Jesus Christ. It is said that the man does not know how the earth brings forth fruit — meaning that people need to apply their strength to fulfill God's will; the manner of applying effort now, in this age, differs essentially from what the way of life will be in the Kingdom of God, where there will be no hardships, temptations, or sins, and therefore these efforts are named of itself the earth bringeth forth fruit (it depends on the people themselves whether they will fulfill God's will). But it must be remembered that although the manner differs, God's grace is always needed by people to fulfill God's will; here it is not explicitly mentioned (but is implied).

In the parable, in this way, through the example of the growth of wheat, God's general design for the Kingdom of God is visible, as well as what the faithful do within this design.

Of Venerable Simeon Stylites:
(Colossians III, 12-16; Matthew XI, 27-30)
Colossians III, 15, 16 — 'ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις ὑμῶν' — 'en tais kardiais hymon' — in your hearts. Where. 'εἰς ἣν καὶ ἐκλήθητε ἐν ἑνὶ σώματι' - 'eis hen kai eklethete en heni somati' — to the which also you are called in One Body. That is, in the Church of Christ. All the named virtues and actions give space to one another to grow, spiritually develop, strengthen, blossom, and bear fruit.

Colossians III, 16 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'en hymin' — in you. In whom. 'ἐν πάσῃ σοφίᾳ' - 'en pase sophia' — in all wisdom; by all wisdom. How and by what. That is, becoming participants in God's Wisdom, co-creating the world in harmony with God. 'ἐν τῇ χάριτι' - 'en te khariti' — in grace; in a good endowment; joyfully. Various translation options are possible here; the last is more in terms of meaning and nuances, rather than literal.

The reading from the Apostle is very eloquent and expressive. Love is called the bond of perfectness because it ought to unite all virtues within itself. A certain ideal of Christian life is described.
