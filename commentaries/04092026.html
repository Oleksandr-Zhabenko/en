Liturgy:
(Galatians II, 6-10)
Galatians II, 6 — 'ἀπὸ δὲ τῶν δοκούντων' — 'apo de ton dokounton' — but of those who seemed [to be somewhat]. The preposition 'apo' here emphasizes moreover, apart from that, that is, a transition to a new topic of conversation.

Galatians II, 8, 9 — 'εἰς ἀποστολήν' — 'eis apostolen' — unto the apostleship; for apostleship. That is, where God's help led — to the strengthening of ministry as an apostle. 'εἰς τὰ ἔθνη' - 'eis ta ethne' — unto the Gentiles; for the Gentiles. Here the preposition 'eis' indicates the spread of preaching not as something operated by Peter or Paul themselves, but as something spread by God's power and therefore beyond human control (similarly used regarding the spread of rumors, for instance, since they cannot be strictly controlled). Here Paul implicitly calls preaching among the Jews an apostleship. Yet right here he shows that God helps no less in preaching among the Gentiles, whom He also cares for and desires to save. That is, these are diplomatic words of the Apostle Paul with a simultaneously deep meaning.

Galatians II, 9 — 'εἰς τὴν περιτομήν' — 'eis ten peritomen' — unto the circumcision; for the circumcised. Both translation options are possible, although they differ. See the explanation above.

Paul characterizes those who caused arguments (precisely so, initially the Galatians had no such people, but they listened to Paul's teaching) as those who did not assist him in any way in preaching. And right here he continues the account of his ministry and the issue regarding which the letter was written.

Cephas — this is Peter (the Aramaic form of this Greek name of the Apostle Peter).

James — known as the Lord's brother, the first bishop of the Jerusalem Church, head at the Apostolic Council in AD 51, also called the Just, one of the 70 apostles, author of the Catholic Epistle and the first order of the Liturgy (not to be confused here with James the son of Zebedee, the brother of John the Theologian, who by that time was already the first martyr among the 12 apostles).

John — the apostle and evangelist John the Theologian, the beloved disciple of Jesus Christ.

(Mark V, 22-24, 35-VI, 1)
Mark V, 35 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀρχισυναγώγου' — 'apo tou arkhisynagogou' — from the ruler of the synagogue's house. The preposition 'apo' indicates disappointment here, that they did not preserve the faith that Jairus himself had shown them. That is, when he went to Jesus, he informed others, those closer in the synagogue, and they thought that indeed Jesus could help, because the girl was still alive, and the rumor of Jesus' miracles was spreading. But seeing that the girl had died, and then Jairus, who managed to lead Jesus to his home to help, they decided that that faith was in vain and became disappointed. Jairus, having trusted Jesus, may now lose not only his daughter, but also the respect of those around him. A double blow for the man, which makes Jesus' subsequent encouragement understandable.

Mark V, 36 — 'παρακούσας' — 'parakousas' — overhearing [the word spoken] (it implies those people were not speaking to Jesus, but He heard their words among themselves).

Mark V, 38 — 'εἰς τὸν οἶκον τοῦ ἀρχισυναγώγου' — 'eis ton oikon tou arkhisynagogou' — into the house of the ruler of the synagogue. That is, into Jairus' house, where his daughter lay dead along with the people.

Mark VI, 1 — 'εἰς τὴν πατρίδα αὐτοῦ' — 'eis ten patrida autou' — into His own country. Where to.

The damsel is not dead, but sleepeth, — spoken so as not to attract great attention; a miracle ought to bring joy, but maintain sobriety, and also not provoke a longing for miracles. Talitha cumi! — these words of Jesus in Aramaic even became the name of a Ukrainian musical group. Twelve years old — a kind of peak of childhood, when a girl combines child, teenage, and youthful traits within herself, which makes parental love for her particularly tender (in this case, Jairus' and the family's).

In Mark there are more details in comparison with Matthew and Luke (in particular, a direct quotation of Christ's words in Aramaic). According to many testimonies, Mark wrote the Gospel from the words of the Apostle Peter, and in this case — from the words of a direct witness of these events. Therefore his account here can be considered the most complete among all the evangelists.

Of the holy Prophet Moses the God-seer:
(James V, 10-20; Luke IV, 22-30)
James V, 12 — here it speaks of the virtue of restraint/moderation, which bestows life and fills even minor deeds, in someone's opinion, with significance and value. For words become true, often being fulfilled as a promise in the future, partly because a person by restraint adds the effort of heart, mind, and ultimately others may be possible, bringing it closer to fulfillment. A person themselves becomes as if a true word and is becoming similar to God's Word.
