Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians XV, 29-38)
1 Corinthians XV, 31 — 'ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' — 'en CHristo Iesou' — in Christ Jesus. In Whom, that is, Who is its Foundation.

1 Corinthians XV, 32 — 'ἐν Ἐφέσῳ' — 'en Epheso' — in Ephesus. Where.

Paul here refutes the argument against faith in the general resurrection, which is built on the basis of purely material notions, on an analogy with inanimate nature — inanimate nature not so often (by no means always) forms highly organized (highly ordered) complexes, but the resurrection of the dead concerns God's power and living people in the resurrection. Thus, the argument that God allegedly cannot create new bodies properly is rejected as erroneous.

Also he shows where unbelief in the general resurrection of the dead can lead (and thus, in many cases, does lead, as the experience of many people and entire nations testifies) — to an incorrect attitude toward life, in particular toward its bodily manifestations.

Further argumentation and explanation is built on the visibility of the death of a seed in the ground. When it falls there, it hides from human sight, germinates, but it looks as if it disappeared, perished, and then appears the result of its falling there, into the ground. This disappearance of the grain from the field of visibility symbolizes human death in the explanation of the apostle (and in the parallel place in the Gospel according to John XII, 24). At the same time, the thought that the grain remains alive is also present in Scripture — in particular Jesus Christ says that all live unto God (Luke XX, 38 ). See more about this at the link:
https://www.facebook.com/groups/1539152859716654/?multi_permalinks=4101814996783748

One can say that the state of the grain changes in the ground — it disappears from the field of vision of people, but germinates and brings forth fruit. So also death, and then resurrection — a person does not disappear completely, but is alive unto God (Luke XX, 38: 'πάντες γὰρ αὐτῷ ζῶσιν' — 'pantes gar autō zōsin' — for all live unto Him), and then at the time of resurrection rises from the dead in a new body. The human's new body has dual characteristics — both as God gives, and as is characteristic to the individual person themselves (the latter means that it bears the imprint of the person's personality, character, and features). One can say that from God a person receives divine characteristics of the resurrected body, in particular its incorruptibility and eternity, but also this body reflects what the person themselves is like (and this depends mostly on them).
See also important explanations at the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/DialogueOnWordsChristFear.html
It must be said that the resurrected body of a person will not have all their characteristic traits, but only those that have significance. For example, the wounds of Jesus Christ have significance for us also after His Resurrection at least until the Second Coming. Therefore they remained. Thus, one can think that at the Last General Judgment itself (which is also called in the Orthodox tradition the Dreadful Judgment) bodies will have also those significant characteristics that will show the state of a person during their earthly life. But after the Judgment, only those will be preserved that will have significance for all eternity — as the Lord says about it: and then the righteous shall shine like the sun in the Kingdom of God, that is, if until that time they did not shine like the sun, then after that, after the Judgment, they will shine. Also one can think that both the wounds of Christ and of the martyrs in the Kingdom of God will disappear, since salvation has already been accomplished.

(Matthew XXI, 23-27)
Matthew XXI, 23, 24, 27 — 'εἰς τὸ ἱερὸν' — 'eis to hieron' — into the temple; to the temple. Where to. 'ἐν ποίᾳ ἐξουσίᾳ' - 'en poia exousia' — in what authority; by what authority. Both translation options are possible. More details are at the link below and further here in the text:
https://gemini.google.com/share/901c8daff8ce
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/14072026.html
In particular, the Pharisees factually ask with whom Christ acts, while simultaneously questioning the legality of His power and Divine origin. By this they also acknowledge that Christ's words have real power, and actions end in accomplishment. But as confirmation of their vision of Christ, the Pharisees have only… their own vision. That is, Christ vividly refutes here, as well as in other cases, accusations regarding the action of the evil one together with Him.
On the part of Jesus, the question was intended to awaken reflection and shake the self-confidence of the chief priests and elders of the people, prompting them ultimately to repentance.

Matthew XXI, 25, 26 — 'ἐξ οὐρανοῦ' — 'ek ouranou' — from heaven. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates origin here. 'ἐξ ἀνθρώπων' - 'ex anthropon' — from men. The preposition 'ek' ('ex' before a vowel) here also indicates origin. 'παρ’ ἑαυτοῖς' - 'par heautois' — in the vicinity of themselves. The preposition 'para' in its form before a following 'he' here is with the dative case and indicates that this was their habitual way of thinking, duplicity, and avoidance of responsibility, and also that factually they reasoned among themselves, privately (yet known to Christ as God-man). This place in many manuscripts has 'en', that is, in themselves. Where.
Jesus shows that the Jews ought to have realized the error of their position regarding Himself, His activities, and teaching.
But they, even having all grounds to realize this, do not want to think about it, and their sinful passions gain the upper hand over them.
