Liturgy:
(1 Thessalonians I, 1-5)
1 Thessalonians I, 1 — 'ἐν θεῷ πατρὶ καὶ κυρίῳ Ἰησοῦ Χριστῷ' — 'en theo patri kai kyrio Iesou CHristo' — in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ. In Whom.

1 Thessalonians I, 4 — 'ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'hypo tou theou' — of God / by God. Here the preposition 'hypo' with the genitive case indicates the passive voice of the participle beloved (loved) and points to Who loves the faithful — the Father.

1 Thessalonians I, 5 — 'εἰς ὑμᾶς ἐν λόγῳ μόνον' — 'eis hymas en logo monon' — unto you in word only / to you only in word. Various translation options are possible. 'καὶ ἐν δυνάμει καὶ ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ καὶ πληροφορίᾳ πολλῇ' - 'kai en dynamei kai en pneumati hagio kai plerophoria polle' — also in power, and in the Holy Spirit, and in much assurance / conviction. 'ἐν ὑμῖν' - 'en hymin' — among you / in you. Where.

(Luke X, 22-24)
Luke X, 22 — 'ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός' — 'hypo tou patros' — by the Father. Here the preposition 'hypo' with the genitive case indicates the passive voice of the participle delivered. It carries two meanings here: transmission irrespective of the Incarnation, and transmission regarding the human nature of the already Incarnate Son of God. In His Divinity, the Son of God receives all from the Father, of Whom He is begotten eternally; according to His human nature, He also receives all from the Father. The Incarnate Son of God as man possesses all God's gifts intended by God for humanity — in particular, He is King, Prophet, High Priest, Christ, Saviour, Deliverer, Conqueror, and more. It must also be understood that some of these divine gifts were not originally what God willed the Son of God to have when creating the world and humanity; for instance, God did not will that humans should sin when creating them, yet as All-Knowing He knew that they would sin. Thus He sent the Son to save them; hence, as man, the Son of God became the Saviour and Deliverer Who suffered for us and the like, receiving these gifts also in view of what occurred with creation, which God did not will for creation. In Christ, God's will and God's permission coincided in a unique and single manner; He is unique in this. And since suffering is not proper to God by nature, one cannot say that all delivered to the Son is 'ek' the Father or 'para' the Father, nor can one say 'apo' the Father (though all three are used regarding certain aspects). Therefore, 'hypo tou patros' is used here. Here the use of the preposition encompasses all aspects of divine life and the salvation of humanity, emphasizing primarily the relationship of the Father and the Son.
