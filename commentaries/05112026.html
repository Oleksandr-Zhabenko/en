Liturgy:
(1 Thessalonians II, 9-14)
1 Thessalonians II, 9 — 'εἰς ὑμᾶς' — 'eis hymas' — unto you; for you. (In the sense of directed toward you). The preposition 'eis' here indicates the direction of the action and the purpose of the action.

1 Thessalonians II, 11-12 — 'παρακαλοῦντες ὑμᾶς καὶ παραμυθούμενοι καὶ μαρτυρόμενοι' — 'parakalountes hymas kai paramythoumenoi kai martyromenoi' — exhorting (encouraging, comforting) you, and encouraging, and testifying. Here it is evident how a father best prompts a child to something good. One can say that the first emphasizes communication; the second — motivation, encouragement, comfort, inspiration, and strength; whilst the third — authority, wisdom, respect, and values. Ideally, all combined should unite in a truly good father for a child. 'εἰς τὸ περιπατεῖν' - 'eis to peripatein' — that you would walk; for walking. Grammatically, the first translation option is better, although the second is more literal. 'εἰς τὴν ἑαυτοῦ βασιλείαν καὶ δόξαν' - 'eis ten heautou basileian kai doxan' — unto His kingdom and glory. Parallel with the conclusion of the Lord's Prayer in Matthew.

1 Thessalonians II, 13 — 'παρ’ ἡμῶν' — 'par hemon' - from beside us. The preposition 'para' in its form before a following 'he' takes the genitive case here. A similar construction is used when speaking of the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father, the Eternal Council of the Most Holy Trinity, God's design in general, as well as regarding the human nature of Jesus Christ. See the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/02082025.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/28052026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/16082026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/uk/commentaries/Pryjmennyky.html
Here it is implied that the apostles preached the Word of God in the Holy Spirit, according to God's design, joining others to the Church, the Body of Christ. 'ἐν ὑμῖν τοῖς πιστεύουσιν' — 'en hymin tois pisteuousin' — in you that believe. In whom. The importance of faith is underlined so that the Holy Spirit acts in people, that is, so that the faithful might be bearers of the Spirit.

1 Thessalonians II, 14 — 'ἐν τῇ Ἰουδαίᾳ' — 'en te Ioudaia' — in Judaea. Where. 'ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' - 'en CHristo Iesou' — in Jesus Christ. 'ὑπὸ τῶν ἰδίων συμφυλετῶν' - 'hypo ton idion symphyleton' — by your own countrymen. 'ὑπὸ τῶν Ἰουδαίων' - 'hypo ton Ioudaion' — by the Jews. The preposition 'hypo' here takes the genitive case, indicating in both instances the passive voice suffered and pointing to who caused those sufferings and afflictions.

The Apostle bears witness to the self-sacrificing conduct and preaching of the apostles, to their labour, in particular so as not to burden their listeners.
Further, he also shows that the apostles acted like a good father for his children, and reminds them that the preaching of the apostles is the Word of God acting in the faithful, whilst also testifying to the Thessalonians that they too imitate Christ and the prophets, as well as the Church in Israel which had already suffered persecutions from the Jews.

(Luke XI, 14-23)
Luke XI, 15, 18, 19 — 'τινὲς δὲ ἐξ αὐτῶν' — 'tines de ex auton' — but some of them. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates here that it refers to representatives of a certain opinion. 'ἐν Βεελζεβούλ' - 'en Beelzeboul' — in Beelzebub / by Beelzebub. Rather the second in sense. The first indicates that Jesus, in the opinion of those accusing Him here, acted by the power of Beelzebub, with his participation and authority; whilst the second points to the supposed direct assistance of the evil one, who acted, in the opinion of the Pharisees, in Jesus and through Him.

Luke XI, 16 — 'ἐξ οὐρανοῦ' — 'ex ouranou' — from heaven. That is, heavenly in origin and form. 'παρ’ αὐτοῦ' - 'par autou' — from beside Him. The preposition 'para' here takes the genitive case. Such a construction is used regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father, the Eternal Council of the Most Holy Trinity, God's design in general, and regarding the human nature of Jesus Christ. Luke XI, 19 — 'ἐν τίνι' — 'en tini' — in whom / by whom. Both translation options are possible. See above regarding Beelzebub.

Matthew XII, 28 — 'ἐν πνεύματι θεοῦ' — 'en pneumati theou' — in the Spirit of God; by the Spirit of God. Most translations give the second variant. The choice affects understanding, since in the first case Jesus speaks of His human nature, comparing with the children of the Pharisees, who are also people. The human nature of Jesus is divinised, filled with the Holy Spirit, which is why Jesus also has the name Christ, that is, Anointed One. In contrast to the sons of the Pharisees, who cast out demons by unclear means and authority, Jesus can from a theological perspective cast out demons by operating in particular through His human nature (which must one way or another participate in His action as God according to the dogma of the 6th Ecumenical Council), though then properly the source of power and authority is the Holy Spirit. Also, in this case, it is necessary to remember that the human nature of Jesus is inseparably united with the Divine nature in the single Hypostasis of the Incarnate Son of God; therefore, it acts accordingly, knowing, as the fathers say, that it is the human nature of the Son of God, to Whom everything is subjected as God. On the other hand, the second variant of translation is also completely logical and concerns more the Divine nature of Jesus, because it emphasizes His Divine dignity. A comparison with the parallel passage in Luke (Luke XI, 20), where the expression by the finger of God is used, points rather to the second variant than to the first, because it is difficult to think of the human nature of Jesus in the finger of God. Considering what has been said, I am also inclined to think that the second variant is more appropriate here.
