Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians XV, 39-45)
1 Corinthians XV, 41 — 'ἐν δόξῃ' - 'en doxe' - in glory; by glory. Both variants of translation are possible. The first indicates that the differences between the heavenly bodies are more noticeable when they are in glory, and also at the centre of attention (glory also has the property of drawing additional attention). The second indicates that glory itself allows the heavenly bodies to be distinguished better, and therefore contributes to knowledge. Here one can see the deep Biblical foundation of the teaching of the beloved Saint Gregory Palamas about the Divine uncreated energies (which can also be called glory in the Biblical sense), precisely through which and through whose consequences it is easier to know God. See also more about glory at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/21092026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/Beauty.html

1 Corinthians XV, 42 — 'ἐν φθορᾷ' - 'en phthora' - in corruption. In terms of meaning, this is the only possible variant of translation, since one cannot make corruption the beginning and cause of the important act of sowing. For example, sufferings endured for Christ, martyrdom in particular, add glory to the faithful, but it cannot be said that they themselves sow eternal life, God's reward, God's love and so on, which are in fact the true causes of glory (I do not list them all here, because there are very many). 'ἐν ἀφθαρσίᾳ' - 'en aphtharsia' - in incorruption. Here it is less obviously logical that this is the right translation, but since there is a clear contrast, the same construction is also grammatically appropriate.

1 Corinthians XV, 43 — 'ἐν ἀτιμίᾳ' - 'en atimia' - in dishonour (that is, in disgrace, or one could also say — in the loss of uniqueness — let us recall that in Scripture it is often the righteous who have names, which we do not see among sinners). 'ἐν δόξῃ' - 'en doxe' - in glory. See the more detailed explanation above regarding verses 41 and 42. 'ἐν ἀσθενείᾳ' - 'en astheneia' - in infirmity; in powerlessness. 'ἐν δυνάμει' - 'en dynamei' - in power. When something high and good is meant, then here it is first of all God's gifts and qualities — glory, honour, power.

1 Corinthians XV, 45 — 'εἰς ψυχὴν ζῶσαν' - 'eis psykhen zosan' - into a living soul. 'εἰς πνεῦμα ζῳοποιοῦν' - 'eis pneuma zoopoioun' - into a life-giving spirit. The preposition 'eis' emphasises movement into the named reality. Became into a soul — that is, the body was so quickened by the soul that it is as if it enters into the soul, while being a body. Likewise becomes into the Spirit — the body is so quickened and filled by the Holy Spirit that it becomes like Him, and through it the grace of life is also imparted, which is why it is called life-giving. Of course, the Holy Spirit imparts grace, it is He who quickens the body and gives it life, but the body too transmits the grace received, becoming like the Spirit. The spirit of a human being also takes a direct part in this, since the spiritualisation and quickening of the body take place through it, so one may think that the body becomes like the human spirit as well. Just as the Risen Jesus passed through closed doors, so too the risen bodies in glory are like the spirit.

The Holy Spirit says through the beloved Apostle Paul a little earlier in the Epistle: What you sow will not come to life unless it dies. And what you sow, you do not sow the future body, but only a grain, which may come up — of wheat or of something else. But God gives it a body as He desires: to each seed its own body (1 Corinthians XV, 36-38). The grain, until it sprouts in good soil in accordance with the calling it has — to be a grain and to sprout into wheat — remains one, alone. The grain is not joined by a body to other grains (perhaps it is joined only by the place of storage) and is not sprouted wheat. And if someone looked at it, they would think that it is alone, a single grain. But what is important is growth in accordance with the calling; it is important for the grain to remain wheat, and in good soil it sprouts. And the body and the relationships are different from the grain; the sower does not know exactly what the wheat will look like. See at the link:

(Luke IV, 31-36)

Luke IV, 31 — 'εἰς Καφαρναοὺμ' - 'eis Kapharnaoum' - into Capernaum. Where to. 'ἐν τοῖς σάββασιν' - 'en tois sabbasin' - on the Sabbaths. When.

Luke IV, 32 — 'ἐν ἐξουσίᾳ ἦν' - 'en exousia en' - was in authority; was authoritative. That is, His word had authority. In particular, His words were fulfilled, for example, when He performed miracles and healings. See also in detail at the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/14072026.html

Luke IV, 33 — 'ἐν τῇ συναγωγῇ' - 'en te synagoge' - in the synagogue. Where.

Luke IV, 35 — 'ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ' - 'ap autou' - from him. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that the unclean spirit was commanded by Christ to go out entirely and completely, which he (it) did. 'εἰς τὸ μέσον' - 'eis to meson' - into the midst; into the middle. A set expression.

Luke IV, 36 — 'ἐν ἐξουσίᾳ καὶ δυνάμει' - 'en exousia kai dynamei' - in authority and power; by authority and power; with authority and power. All variants of translation are possible. That is, as the Bearer of authority and power (and it is even better to say here — as their Source).

Of Hieromartyrs Dionysius the Areopagite, Bishop of Athens, Rusticus, presbyter, and Eleutherius, deacon:
(Acts XVII, 16-34; Matthew XIII, 44-54)
Acts XVII, 16 — 'Ἐν δὲ ταῖς Ἀθήναις' — 'en de tais Athenais' - and in Athens. Where. 'ἐν αὐτῷ' - 'en auto' - in him. In whom.

Acts XVII, 17 — 'ἐν τῇ συναγωγῇ' — 'en te synagoge' - in the synagogue. Where. 'ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ' - 'en te agora' - in the agora; in the place of city trade and the settling of affairs. Where.

On verses Acts XVII, 19-28 see the link and the others cited there:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/19052026.html

Acts XVII, 31 — 'ἐν ᾗ' — 'en he' - in which. When. 'ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ ἐν ἀνδρὶ' - 'en dikaiosyne en andri' - in righteousness by a Man; by righteousness — by a Man. Both variants of translation are possible; the first emphasises the righteousness of the Father, and the second — the righteousness of the Son. 'ἐκ νεκρῶν' - 'ek nekron' - from the dead. The preposition 'ek' indicates here the place from which Christ's transition took place — from the dead to the living.

Acts XVII, 33 — 'ἐκ μέσου αὐτῶν' — 'ek mesou auton' - from among them. From where. A set expression.

Acts XVII, 34 — 'ἐν οἷς' — 'en hois' - among whom.

In Athens Paul preaches and mentions the piety of the Athenians, who had an altar to an Unknown God, and Paul says that it is precisely Him whom he preaches (a missionary method, one might say) (Acts XVII, 22-31). Why did Paul not characterise the sacrifices on that altar as idolatry, as sacrifices to demons, whereas the sacrifices of the pagans to idols are sacrifices to demons? What is the difference?

This is not an entirely clear question, but one can see the following. Before the pagans began to offer sacrifices to idols, they either were witnesses of unusual appearances, which they understood as gods, or they invented those gods for various reasons. In both of these cases the idols were a direct result of deception. And deception comes from the evil one, who is the father of falsehood and deceit.

But when people offered sacrifices on the altar to the Unknown God, it was a result of… truth. The truth that something was lacking for them in paganism, that they were seeking the true God. The truth that they did not know Him and modestly acknowledged this. The truth that this was an expression of their genuine piety (they must have believed in this Unknown God if, knowing nothing about Him, they nevertheless built an altar to Him, acknowledging their ignorance). And therefore, since the Source of truth is the True God, this altar was — to Him. But faith in the Unknown God is weak because they did not know how exactly to believe in Him correctly and how important it is.

The reading directly mentions the beloved Saint Dionysius the Areopagite and his conversion, and that is why precisely this passage is read.
