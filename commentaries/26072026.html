Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians I, 10-18)
1 Corinthians I, 10, 11 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'en hymin' - among you; within you. 'κατηρτισμένοι' - 'katertismenoi' - perfectly joined together; restored to the optimal state, perfected in their finest state and applications. 'ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ νοῒ' - 'en to auto noi' - in (that which has just been spoken about) the same mind; sharing one (that which has just been spoken about) mindset. 'ἐν τῇ αὐτῇ γνώμῃ' - 'en te aute gnome' - in the same (that which has just been spoken about) judgment/thought; of one accord. Here we have a double reminder — a call to adhere to the Apostolic Tradition, and simultaneously an exhortation to unity within it. Paul teaches the preservation of the apostolic faith while guarding against divisions, asking for unity of mind. Within this framework, he allows for individual distinctions and joint growth into maturity. The Apostles of Christ were not identical in temperament or talent, yet they remained in complete unity in Christ, in the Holy Spirit, in the One God glorified in Trinity. Each Apostle was able to manifest grace and serve Christ and the Church according to his unique personhood through communion with God.

1 Corinthians I, 11 — 'ὑπὸ τῶν Χλόης' — 'hypo ton Khloes' - by Chloe's people [those of Chloe]. The preposition 'hypo' with the genitive case highlights that Chloe's household acted simply as the source conveying this report.

1 Corinthians I, 13 — 'εἰς τὸ ὄνομα' — 'eis to onoma' - into the name. The preposition 'eis' indicates that actions done into someone's name are intended to bring one into dynamic union with the bearer of that name. For instance, doing something in the name of God implies seeking union with God through that act.

1 Corinthians I, 15 — 'εἰς τὸ ἐμὸν ὄνομα' — 'eis to emon onoma' - into my own name.

1 Corinthians I, 17 — 'ἐν σοφίᾳ λόγου' — 'en sophia logou' - in wisdom of word; by wisdom of speech. Both translations are accurate, though the latter is often clearer in modern English.

Because those who were dividing themselves into factions had forgotten and neglected the foundations of the faith, Paul forcefully reminds them of core truths. Judging by Paul's tone, some had begun to view the person performing the baptism as a kind of personal key to salvation — the Corinthians were arguing as if the identity of the baptizer were more vital than their common faith and the unity of the Church. Paul rejects this idea with intense emotional emphasis.

Paul clearly distinguishes the essential from the secondary. Had Christ sent Paul primarily to baptize, he would not have thanked God (using an impassioned counterargument to halt their factions) that he baptized almost none of them. But since administration of baptism was not the primary purpose of his specific apostolic calling (which was to preach), he exhibits pastoral flexibility.

The term in verse 18 derives from 'μωραίνω' ('moraino') — to make or be foolish; to become tasteless, flat, or insipid; to lose flavour; to be naive, gullible, or foolishly trusting. People who reject the preaching of the Cross of the Lord — His Crucifixion — regard it as sheer naivety or foolishness. Yet the inverse is true: human wisdom that fails to know God's wisdom has lost its flavor before God, and people have become truly gullible by placing faith only in what is comfortable and easy to believe. Divine faith operates on a fundamentally different plane.

(Matthew XIV, 14-22)
Matthew XIV, 15 — 'εἰς τὰς κώμας' — 'eis tas komas' - into the villages. Where to.

Matthew XIV, 19 — 'εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν' — 'eis ton ouranon' - into the heaven; up toward the heaven. Where to.

Matthew XIV, 21 — 'χωρὶς γυναικῶν καὶ παιδίων' — 'khoris gynaikon kai paidion' - besides women and children; apart from women and children. Both renderings are appropriate in context.

Matthew XIV, 22 — 'εἰς τὸ πλοῖον' — 'eis to ploion' - into the boat; to the boat. Where to. 'εἰς τὸ πέραν' - 'eis to peran' - to the other side; across. An established idiom translated by the latter phrase. Where to.

The Lord did not send the crowds away empty-handed, giving a supreme example of divine care and providence.

The miracle of feeding the vast multitude with a few loaves and fishes far surpasses the similar miracle wrought through the Prophet Elisha, revealing the divine dignity and authority of Jesus.

Furthermore, the miracle demonstrates that God's manifestation is at once measured and abundant. It is measured because He worked through a very small initial amount of physical food, yet abundant because He satisfied everyone completely and richly. Divine grace operates in a similar manner — God bestows it with boundless generosity, yet always suited to the capacity of the recipient.
