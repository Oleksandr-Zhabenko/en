(1 Corinthians XII, 12-26)
1 Corinthians XII, 13 — 'ἐν ἑνὶ πνεύματι ἡμεῖς πάντες εἰς ἓν σῶμα' — 'en heni pneumati hemeis pantes eis hen soma' — in one Spirit we all into one Body; in one Spirit we all for one Body. Various translation options are possible. Variants with for indicate that Baptism and the receiving of the gifts of the Holy Spirit are needed also, in particular, in order to provide for the unity of the Body of Christ, that is, the Church. The preposition 'en' indicates the actual state, and the preposition 'eis' indicates movement toward the one Body or this one Body as the goal of movement. The unity of the Holy Spirit is a precondition of the unity of the Church, the Body of Christ. One can also say that the recognition of the gifts of the Holy Spirit promotes the unity of the Church. Here Google Gemini Flash-3.6 provides a good expression:

When a human being participates in the Trinitarian action through the preposition 'en', there is no confusion or mixture of subjects (which would be a theological error). Instead, we witness a profound mystical synergy. The human person preserves their distinct identity and free will, yet acts from within the Divine sphere. It is a state of hypostatic distinction maintained within perfect spiritual unity, where God remains the transcendent Source, and man acts as a living, distinct co-worker.

https://gemini.google.com/share/901c8daff8ce

1 Corinthians XII, 15, 16 — 'ἐκ τοῦ σώματος' — 'ek tou somatos' — from the body; out of the body. The preposition 'ek' indicates selection out of a group, and also that the chosen member is a representative of the whole Body, being a part of the whole Body. 'παρὰ τοῦτο' - 'para touto' — because of this; therefore. The preposition 'para' here is with the accusative. Here it indicates that such reasoning of a member of the body, if it were correct, would have such logic as confirmation of its state. But the Apostle Paul brings the logic of those who think like the imaginary erroneous words of the parts of the body to its completion in his clear example, by which he makes it effectively refuted.

1 Corinthians XII, 18, 25 — 'ἐν τῷ σώματι' — 'en to somati' — in the body; in the Body. Where. Both options are meant here, which emphasizes the aptness of the model of the organism and body for the Church.

In verses 1 Corinthians XII, 22-23, and further in 24-26, it is about functional asymmetry — the members of the body are used according to purpose by a person not equally often and with different functions. Nevertheless, those members that have some special functions are used less than those that have constant significant functions. And when the time comes for the use of precisely the special functions, then those parts of the body receive significant priority, being little needed the rest of the time. Since a person does not want to lose any significant functions, they care for all members of the body, including those that are fully needed only sometimes. This care and concern, disproportionate to the time of use, is nevertheless needed to ensure the integrity and fullness of all functions of the human organism.

Paul explains what it means to be members of the Body of Christ from the point of view of the manifestations of God's gifts and ministries, as well as mutual need in the Church, the community of the faithful.

The explanation is very eloquent if understood as a certain comparison of people with parts of the body, and the Church (in general here meaning every local community of the Church, from which later came the concept of the Local Church) as a whole as the whole body. I will also add that the word catholic in the Creed, in Greek καθολική (εκκλησία), originally meant one that has within itself the entire fullness of the spiritual gifts and ministries necessary for the salvation of people, in the opinion of at least some researchers.

The unusual words of the parts of the body about themselves also indicate that God cares for all parts of the body, despite the fact that they may not fully understand their calling, also in the time of their search. The described reasonings of the parts of the body are possible in the time when they (that is, the faithful people) are in a state of searching for themselves in the Church. And God cares for them in this time as well.

It is necessary to emphasize that spiritual gifts are given to members of the Church even when they do not know which ones specifically, and even when they do not understand what exactly are their spiritual gifts. Such Divine generosity is needed for their own development, growth, knowledge of God, and also for service to others even before the awareness by people of their gifts. God wants people to know Him, know themselves, know others, know and discern their gifts and the gifts of others, but grants them often earlier than this last occurs, thus, in advance, providentially.

And the second thought — although different parts of the body have different gifts and ministries, there is no such part of the body that would perform the functions of all the others.

All this is for the edification of the Church as the Body of Christ.

Matthew XVIII, 18 — 'ἐν οὐρανῷ' — 'en ourano' — in heaven; with heaven. More likely the first, since the second would mean using heaven as an instrument or means for binding and loosing. An important detail here: unlike verses 10, 14, 19 — where the plural is used throughout — here it is the singular. One may think that the apostles (and subsequently their successors) are here in a certain sense heavenly people, heavenly — but not all-heavenly. See more concerning heavenly people at the link:
https://www.instagram.com/p/C8Iav6ssKOI/
One may think that people arrive by God's design (apart from the Mother of God, possibly John the Baptist, and might be also some others) into one of God's dwellings (of which there are many in the Father's house). They will not enjoy all of them. And the forgiveness of a sin by an apostle (or his successor) opens a place, grants a place in one of those dwellings, if previously that place was inaccessible on account of sin. Analogously the reverse is also true — if an apostle (or his successor) binds something, then sin continues to hinder access to that heavenly dwelling. But God reserves for Himself the Supreme Judgement — and He may show mercy to a person (taking all things into account, and above all from His boundless mercy). Similarly these rules of which Christ speaks here carry greater weight and universal significance if they are violated by people (including apostles — hypothetically, since the apostles took care to observe them — or their successors): in that case such a violation is known to all the heavens — that is, it requires unconditional repentance. For example, if a clergyman, a successor of the apostles, shows contempt for any person (see Matthew V, 22), they fall under the Supreme divine Judgement (not the judgement of the Church, but God's — or at minimum that of an Ecumenical Council). Therefore apostolic succession enables one to join people to heaven, but does not permit the execution of a final judgement or the determination of people's destinies. On the other hand it places great responsibility upon the successors of the apostles. It must also be said that if someone acts according to God's will, then this concerns all the heavens — for it concerns God's will.
End of quote.

It is necessary to note that in the verse Matthew XVIII, 18 stands the singular regarding heaven, and here the plural. The apostle Peter answered from the name of all the apostles, since Christ asked all the apostles; Simon expressed their common faith, stood out as the one who did this. Factually it concerns that Peter spoke from the name of the New Testament Church, as its voice and testimony, and therefore received an assurance regarding heavens in the plural, for the Church is called to lead people into the heavens, into all the mansions which the Father has prepared. Opened by the Father, experienced by the Church, confessed by Peter. Why does Christ not say that Peter spoke from the name of all the Church directly, but instead speaks about the building by Him of the Church upon the foundation of the rock of Peter? Christ addresses the faithful and expects faith which makes possible the acceptance of all the Divine Revelation, all the Divine will. If the apostles could not manifest such faith, then Christ would continue their preparation into this necessary step. Peter boldly manifested, and therefore received the naming of blessed (happy). I will note also that the Church built by Christ opposes the gates of hades. Hades does not have a Church as a building, it has only gates — there one can enter, but it is impossible to become a part of the edification, only one can begin to belong into it, remain in it. The Church is a living building, since it is built out of people and angels who become participants of the edification, a part of her very self. Peter receives a promise that Christ shall build upon living faith the Church, as upon a rock, for there is no living faith without a faithful person or angel, without the one who confesses faith, who lives by it. Matthew XVIII, 19 — 'συμφωνήσωσιν' — 'symphonesosin' — will agree, (figuratively) will harmonize / be in accord. 'ἐξ ὑμῶν' - 'ex hymon' — from among you. The preposition 'ex' (form 'ek' before non-aspirated vowels) indicates from where these two are, that is, a selection out of the Church, that is, that these are representatives of the community of the faithful. 'παρὰ τοῦ πατρός' - 'para tou patros' — from the Father. The preposition 'para' here is with the genitive case. Such a construction is used regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father, regarding the Most Holy Trinity, regarding the whole of God's plan, also regarding the human nature of Jesus Christ.
