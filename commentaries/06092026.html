Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians I, 21-II, 4)
2 Corinthians I, 21 — 'εἰς Χριστὸν' — 'eis CHriston' — into (to, towards) Christ; in Christ. The preposition 'eis' here emphasizes movement towards Christ, a gradual conformity to Him Himself.

2 Corinthians I, 22 — 'ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις' — 'en tais kardiais' — in our hearts. Where / inside.

2 Corinthians I, 23 — 'εἰς Κόρινθον' — 'eis Korinthon' — unto Corinth. Where to.

2 Corinthians II, 1 — 'ἐν λύπῃ' — 'en lype' — in heaviness / sorrow. In what, how.

2 Corinthians II, 2 — 'ἐξ ἐμοῦ' — 'ex emou' — by me; from me. That is, the cause or occasion of which was I.

2 Corinthians II, 3 — 'ἀφ’ ὧν' — 'aph on' — of them. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates here that the apostle would find occasion for joy rather than that they themselves would try to make him glad. Here is a subtle nuance in the use of 'apo' compared to 'ek' — the latter indicates an essential cause, whilst the former here indicates an accompanying, additional one. In context, it is precisely about those people not actually setting themselves such a goal — to make Paul glad — and he was fully conscious of that.

2 Corinthians II, 4 — 'ἐκ γὰρ πολλῆς θλίψεως καὶ συνοχῆς' — 'ek gar polles thlipseos kai synokhes' — for out of much affliction and anguish. That is, by reason of. 'εἰς ὑμᾶς' - 'eis hymas' — toward you; unto you. That is, regarding you.

(Matthew XXII, 1-14)
Matthew XXII, 1 — 'ἐν παραβολαῖς' — 'en parabolais' — by parables; in parables. Both translation options are possible; the first indicates that parables were a method and means of Revelation, whilst the second indicates that God's Revelation was presented in the form of parables.

Matthew XXII, 3, 4, 9 — 'εἰς τοὺς γάμους' — 'eis tous gamous' — to the marriage / wedding. Where to (invited, to come).

Matthew XXII, 5 — 'εἰς τὸν ἴδιον ἀγρόν' — 'eis ton idion agron' — to his field. Where to.

Matthew XXII, 10 — 'εἰς τὰς ὁδοὺς' — 'eis tas hodous' — into the roads. Where to.

Matthew XXII, 13 — 'εἰς τὸ σκότος τὸ ἐξώτερον' — 'eis to skotos to exoteron' — into outer darkness. Where to.

The famous parable about the need first to respond to the invitation of God's grace, of God, and then to align one's life according to the calling.

In the image of the king here is God (the Father); the king's son is the Son of God, Jesus Christ; the servants are the prophets, apostles, saints, and even angels. The destruction of the murderers is spiritual death, which occurs after mortal sins. It is implied that those who refused to accept the invitation of Jesus Christ, and instead persecuted and even killed the king's (God's) servants, suffered spiritual death in this life and do not inherit the Kingdom of God. It is important that even those who refused, but did not persecute the king's servants — those who refused for the sake of their own affairs: trade, profit, wealth, and the like — also do not take part in the Kingdom of God. In the parable it is not said that they perished spiritually, but they do not take part in this life in fulfilling God's will, in living according to it — that is, in Gospel language, they entered not into the Kingdom of God, and are in great danger of not entering there in eternity either (that is, actually perishing, entering into eternal torment). The city of the murderers represents such a worldly order that prompts people to reject God's invitation to enter the Kingdom of God through Jesus Christ; such an order is also sinful and judged by God.

The people called on the roads are those whom the Lord calls, who have not made their choice in favour of the riches of this world instead of God's will. And the conclusion of the parable shows that it is not enough to respond to God's call, but one must also possess a clean life (symbolically depicted by clean wedding garments) or be cleansed by repentance.

The parable, in the image of typical relationships of that time understandable to Jesus' listeners, shows the importance of responding well to God's call, not being excessively attached to the earthly, as well as the importance of a good life.

Of Archangel Michael of God:
(Hebrews II, 2-10; Luke X, 16-21)
Hebrews II, 3 — 'ὑπὸ τῶν ἀκουσάντων' — 'hypo ton akousanton' — by them that heard Him. The preposition 'hypo' here is used with the genitive case, indicating the passive voice of the verb was confirmed and those by whom it was confirmed. 'εἰς ἡμᾶς' - 'eis hemas' — unto us; for us. Both translation options are possible, with the second indicating that this is God's gift to the faithful.

Hebrews II, 7, 9 — 'παρ’ ἀγγέλους' — 'par angelous' — a little lower than the angels; to the proximity with (alongside) the angels. The preposition 'para' in its form before a following vowel is used here with the accusative case, carrying the sense of juxtaposition and comparison.

Hebrews II, 8 — 'ἐν τῷ γὰρ ὑποτάξαι' — 'en to gar hypotaxai' — for in that He put all in subjection. When and in what.

Hebrews II, 10 — 'εἰς δόξαν' — 'eis doxan' — unto glory. The preposition 'eis' indicates here where the Lord brought His children.
