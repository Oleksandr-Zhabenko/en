Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians XV, 1-11)
1 Corinthians XV, 1 — 'ἐν ᾧ' — 'en ho' — wherein. In what.

1 Corinthians XV, 3 — 'ἐν πρώτοις' — 'en protois' — first of all. Set expression.

1 Corinthians XV, 6 — 'ἐξ ὧν' — 'ex hon' — of whom. The form of the preposition 'ek' before a following vowel indicates here a selection from a group of people, that is, referring to representatives of witnesses who saw Christ Risen.

1 Corinthians XV, 10 — 'εἰς ἐμὲ' — 'eis eme' — toward me. The preposition 'eis' emphasizes the fruits of grace for the Apostle Paul and others.

The beginning of the reading relates primarily to the continuation of the reading. If Christians did not preserve the Gospel, the faith as such that they accepted, then they would not actually have true faith, and then untrue faith would not be worth the attitude that ought to be toward true faith in Christ. Further Paul briefly reminds the Corinthians of the Resurrection of Jesus Christ and His appearances to the disciples. Many of the appearances mentioned are not described in the Gospels, so this testimony is important for understanding that Christ appeared more than is described in the Gospels, and these words supplement the Gospels in this. Some people try to deny the truth of the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, relying on various assumptions to possibly explain the events. But all these assumptions do not withstand criticism in view of all the available testimonies. The women and apostles, who were not expecting the Resurrection, saw and communicated with Jesus. He ate food; Thomas, who wished to be convinced, received confirmation; Jesus appeared under various circumstances, to different people, particularly to those who were unprepared and even hostile to Him (Paul himself was previously a persecutor, but was converted after the miraculous appearance of the Risen Jesus). The conviction of people that the dead do not rise by themselves here collided with the clear fact of the Resurrection from the dead of Jesus Christ. A fact about which long before that prophets prophesied, who were also witnesses of other miracles. Further Paul speaks of the action of God's grace upon himself, of his calling and subsequent labor, co-working with God's grace.
Born out of due time — in the times of the apostles there was no natal medicine in its modern form, so children who were born prematurely were smaller, weaker, and often sickly, with these or those disorders. Therefore Paul, humbly comparing himself with other witnesses, speaks of himself as though he yields to them.
Not I, but grace…, — here Paul's conscious, purposeful, and persistent labor is not denied, but the strengthening, direction, encouragement, bestowing, accomplishment, help, blessing, and the like are emphasized, everything that the Lord bestowed upon Paul during his co-working with grace.

Matthew XIX, 23, 24 — 'εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν τῶν οὐρανῶν' — 'eis ten basileian ton ouranon' — into the Kingdom of Heaven. Where to.

Matthew XIX, 26 — 'παρὰ ἀνθρώποις' — 'para anthropois' — in the vicinity of people. 'παρὰ δὲ θεῷ' - 'para de theo' — in the vicinity of God. The preposition 'para' here is with the dative case. Here the Lord indicates that one can distinguish life according to God's will as that which concerns closeness to God, from when a person does not live according to God's will — similarly to (many) people. The famous narrative about the rich young man. It has many fine explanations; I will dwell on only a few points.

Regarding the words There is none good but one, that is, God — it is considered that Christ indirectly asks the young man about his faith in Him (Do you believe that I am God?).

More specifically I will begin from the end. Jesus' answer to the disciples' question shows that the disciples, in asking the question, did not understand the significance of God's grace in the matter of human salvation. They thought that a person is saved… by their own righteousness. Moreover, in the Old Testament, wealth is connected with righteousness, for the Lord blesses for fulfilling the commandments. But in reality, righteousness itself, if it is true, has as its cause the action of God's grace, and the co-working of the person themselves in this. As written at the links above, God always begins First. He is Alpha (Beginning) and Omega (Goal, End), as Revelation of John the Theologian says.

A person cannot be saved by their own powers; salvation is always God's gift. I wrote about this earlier, mostly when examining the epistles of the Apostle Paul, who writes much about it. Nevertheless, a person ought to accept this gift and co-work with God in the matter of salvation. With God all things are possible.

The story with the young man shows part of the difficulties faced by an attempt to achieve salvation by one's own powers. The young man in the end found it difficult to determine whether he needed to follow Christ. And he went away sorrowful.

I wrote earlier that the young man placed his trust in wealth (this is spoken of in a parallel passage in another evangelist). To place trust in something here means to place it at the centre of one's life. Where the Lord Himself ought to be.

To enter into the Kingdom of God — that is, already in this life to consciously live according to God's will and calling. Cannot enter — lacks the power, the ability so to live, as is clearly seen in the example of the young man.

And one more thing — Christ called the young man to perfection through voluntary sacrificial poverty. The young man was excessively attached to wealth, which is why as a path to perfection Christ explicitly tells him to distribute his wealth for the sake of heavenly treasure.
