Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians XIII, 4-XIV, 5)
1 Corinthians XIII, 9, 10, 12 — 'ἐκ μέρους' — 'ek merous' — in part; partially. Set expression.

1 Corinthians XIII, 12 — 'δι’ ἐσόπτρου' — 'di esoptrou' — by means of a mirror as an instrument. Famous words, which are explained by the fact that mirrors of that time were very imperfect and one saw in them in general terms, approximately, although even such an opportunity was often very valuable. 'ἐν αἰνίγματι' - 'en ainigmati' — in a riddle; in obscurity. How. Mysterious and not fully clear are all circumstances, phenomena, events, and the like — that is, emphasizing not the presence of a task to solve or know, but rather the presence of uncertainty and unclarity.

The reading combines two important topics: love and spiritual gifts, such as prophesying and speaking in tongues. The first is the larger part of the so-called hymn to love, the famous words of the Apostle Paul describing true love. Put briefly, love in this sense is a bond of perfection (that which binds them, the connecting link), as Paul speaks of it in another place. One can understand it such that all virtues are separate constituent parts of a tree, whereas love is the root and trunk on which everything rests, and of which everything is a manifestation. A continuation of this thought in greater depth is a fine poem at the link:
https://www.instagram.com/oleksandr_zhabenko/p/DUu8adRDfD0/
This reflection is valuable from both directions of movement — from love to virtues and from virtues to love. One can see in this that true love gives birth to all virtues. One can also see that true virtues express true love, for they have meaning only in it (when it is present).
Someone will say that therefore, love is very high and almost unattainable. But no. Its seed, its germ is present in every even small manifestation of true virtue, as can be seen from the parables of the Savior. And this same thought is visible also from yesterday's reading:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/28072026.html

Regarding two points from the verses, I will say a little more, because proper attention is often not paid to them.

1 Corinthians XIII, 6 — see John III, 21 — does the truth — it is necessary to remember that truth is a supreme virtue and value on a par with love; knowing the truth (and being in it) stands higher than salvation and is a manifestation of the perfection of creation. See, in particular, at the links:
https://www.facebook.com/Oleksandr.S.Zhabenko/posts/

And also the famous passage regarding the mirror — in those times mirrors were not of such high quality as now; it was difficult to really see anything in them clearly, but one could see general outlines approximately. Therefore Paul speaks of an unclear view, in general terms in a mirror. Sometimes translations, to convey the sense rather than the letter, write as through a dim glass, which roughly conveys the character of the image.

Regarding the part about spiritual gifts — Paul means that the value of gifts is determined primarily not by their supernaturalness, but by their significance for the salvation of people. Prophecy is therefore better because it leads people to repentance and, thus, salvation more easily and effectively. One can say in other words that all spiritual gifts are equal in importance in themselves, for all equally originate from the Single Source — God, God's grace; all are consequences and fruits of salvation and glorification in Christ and the Holy Spirit. But the comparative value of these or those gifts and ministries is determined not by the intrinsic value of each gift, but by how it has unfolded in the Church for the salvation of many. Hence also the conclusion about the significance of the activity of the human person himself for comparing the value of gifts — all have gifts equal in intrinsic value, but bring forth fruit differently, exert effort also differently, by which the final value is determined. Somewhat similar to the concept of added value in economic theory. In connection with this, the combination with the Gospel, where this same topic is raised, is very fitting. Another interesting detail is that the explained gift of tongues with understanding of listeners and readers is factually equated to Christian (not Old Testament) prophecy.

(Matthew XX, 1-16)
Matthew XX, 1, 2, 4, 7 — 'εἰς τὸν ἀμπελῶνα' — 'eis ton ampelona' — into the vineyard. Where to.

Matthew XX, 2 — 'ἐκ δηναρίου τὴν ἡμέραν' — 'ek denariou ten hemeran' — at a denarius a day; for a denarius per day. Set expression of such content.

Matthew XX, 3 — 'ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ' — 'en te agora' — in the marketplace. Where.

Matthew XX, 8 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ἐσχάτων ἕως τῶν πρώτων' — 'apo ton eskhaton heos ton proton' — from the last even unto the first. The preposition 'apo' indicates here the starting point of counting a certain range.

Matthew XX, 15 — 'ἐν τοῖς ἐμοῖς' — 'en tois emois' — in my own; with my own. Literal translation yields the first variant, which shows that the master is in his vineyard and acts in his property, one can say in his authority, as was expected and agreed upon.

A peculiar parable, full of comfort, but also with a certain lesson. I draw attention to the fact that the feelings, thoughts, and evaluations of people often fail them. Their attitude changes in the course of the development of events and circumstances, with experience and the like. Instead, God, Who is depicted in the image of the master, addresses the essence — there was an agreement on a reward of one denarius, and He fulfilled what was promised in full. More about the peculiar quantum nature of the calculation (physicists will understand) is below. The parable is wonderfully used by Saint John Chrysostom in his Paschal Homily, which is read at the Paschal service every year. In English, this Homily is online at the link:
https://www.ewtn.com/catholicism/teachings/st-john-chrysostom-easter-homily-203
To understand the basic logic, one needs to understand what a denarius is, and why it is one. Historically, the meaning is that precisely 1 denarius constituted the daily wage of a worker. And to understand what this denarius is, one must remember that there are things that can be and are only in the singular. For example, this is salvation. It either exists in a person or does not. This is the soul. It can also be either saved or lost. This can be any virtue — and in the end one can say that a person has this virtue, or does not have it (at least God can say this in every case). And therefore God (the master in the parable) does not break, does not divide the denarius into parts, because this is not provided for regarding all those spiritual realities of which we just spoke. And then the logic becomes clear. And if to someone something seems too generous, then this is a manifestation of God's great love and mercy, generosity, goodness, etc.

It is interesting to note that the reading echoes the reading from the Apostle. There also regarding the inner spiritual value of God's gifts it is said that it is the same (see above). But in the dialogue at the end two thoughts are visible — God's salvation is granted (precisely granted!) not for merits or endured hardships (the burden of the day and the scorching heat), but with a view to the result — worked in the vineyard or not. The second is that this result must be sufficient to receive the reward — that is, really worked. God in this way encourages and calls us to synergy with Him.
