Liturgy:
(Ephesians III, 8-21)
Ephesians III, 9 — 'οἰκονομία τοῦ μυστηρίου' — 'oikonomia tou mysteriou' — dispensation / administration of the mystery. Similar to what was written yesterday at the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/22092026.html
but here it emphasizes a special trust manifested by God to people (similarly to how entrusting a great secret indicates the level of trust and relationship). 'ἀπὸ τῶν αἰώνων' — 'apo ton aionon' — from the beginning of the world / from the ages. The preposition 'apo' is used here in the sense of beginning from, which is its typical usage in such expressions. 'ἐν τῷ θεῷ' - 'en to theo' — in God; by God. Rather the first option, as the second implies that God hid the mystery for some purpose.

Ephesians III, 10 — 'ἐν τοῖς ἐπουρανίοις' — 'en tois epouraniois' — in the above-heavenly places; in the heavenly places. A similar expression is found in Ephesians I, 3, but there it pertains to Christ Himself in His Ascension. Therefore here too one can think that it refers implicitly to the ascended and glorified body of Christ, which is mysteriously united with the Church. That is, it is not about the angelic orders being in the super-heavenly, but that through the Ascension and glorification of Christ, through His manifested love for the Church, particularly as His Body, God's mystery, God's wisdom is preached to the angels. See also:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/17092026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/10102024.html
'πολυποίκιλος σοφία' — 'polypoikilos sophia' — manifold wisdom. Stated primarily as a negation of the idea of monotony and dullness in God's wisdom. That is, by no means these latter two. And since God's wisdom is not monotonous, but engaging, it is always desirable for a human being not only for the intellect, but also for the feelings and will, capable of satiating the whole person.

Ephesians III, 11, 21 — 'ἐν (τῷ) Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' — 'en (to) CHristo Iesou' — in Christ Jesus. In Whom.

Ephesians III, 12 — 'ἐν ᾧ' — 'en ho' — in Whom. 'ἐν πεποιθήσει' - 'en pepoithesei' — with confidence / in trust. In what.

Ephesians III, 13 — 'ἐν ταῖς θλίψεσίν' — 'en tais thlipsesin' — at my tribulations. In what.

Ephesians III, 15 — 'ἐξ οὗ' — 'ex hou' — of Whom. Here the preposition 'ek' in its form before a following 'hou' indicates the Source of origin. 'πατριά' - 'patria' — family, lineage; tribe; fatherhood. Here is a profound theological thought; for details see the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/21092026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/Trinity.html
In this sense originates also all causality, the ability to give, activity, while at the same time the Father is higher than any causality as its Source, so in this sense one can say Supracausal.
Self-existent means having being, life in Himself, without introducing any iterations. In the words of the Gospel of John, As the Father has life in Himself; so HAS He GIVEN to the Son to HAVE life in Himself, it refers to the Godhead, God's essence manifesting itself in this context as life, while being higher than life. Similarly, the Hypostasis of the Father manifests Himself as the Cause, Who is the Source of all causality, but is logically (ineffably, apophatically) higher than causality. And nothing in creation is essentially similar to God, yet everything testifies of Him; so too causality and primacy, which we know from creation, testify of God, but are not such as God's. 'ἐν οὐρανοῖς' — 'en ouranois' — in heaven / in the heavens. Where. A parallel to the Lord's Prayer.

Ephesians III, 16 — 'εἰς τὸν ἔσω ἄνθρωπον' — 'eis ton eso anthropon' — into the inner man. The preposition 'eis' here serves as part of a phrase indicating movement toward that has reached its goal, movement toward the inner man in the Holy Spirit, that is, simply understood — toward a certain ideal that is attained (not merely attainable). See also theological reflections regarding another passage at the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/Adultery-briefly.html

Ephesians III, 17 — 'ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις' — 'en tais kardiais' — in your hearts. Where.

Ephesians III, 18 — 'ἐν ἀγάπῃ' — 'en agape' — in love; by love. Both translation options are possible. 'τεθεμελιωμένοι' - 'tethemeliomenoi' — rooted and grounded (having a foundation). Compare with what is stated above.

Ephesians III, 19 — 'γνῶναί τε τὴν ὑπερβάλλουσαν της γνώσεως' — 'gnonai te ten hyperballousan tes gnoseos' — to know and surpass knowledge. The Apostle Paul expresses a bold hope that later Christians will know God better. It is precisely the idea of better knowledge, unfathomable knowledge that lies in the words surpass knowledge. 'εἰς πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα τοῦ θεοῦ' - 'eis pan to pleroma tou theou' — into all the fulness of God. Indicates movement toward God and growth. Since it is impossible to measure God's essence, fulness of God refers to His actions, divine energies, grace. Concerning Christ, the apostle says that in Him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily, whereas here he speaks of being filled. The former indicates that Christ is truly God and man (for dwelleth indicates an essential and inviolable bond here, meaning Christ possesses the divine essence, being the Son of God, the Second Hypostasis of the Most Holy Trinity), and not merely filled with God's grace. In contrast, the words about being filled here speak of receiving grace (ideally — all that is possible for a human being who is not Christ Himself).

Ephesians III, 20 — 'ἐν ἡμῖν' — 'en hemin' — in us. In whom, where.

Ephesians III, 21 — 'ἐν τῇ ἐκκλησίᾳ' — 'en te ekklesia' — in the Church. Where. 'εἰς πάσας τὰς γενεάς' - 'eis pasas tas geneas' — throughout all ages / for all generations.

I will focus a bit more on the word regarding forgiveness. As I wrote earlier, true, sincere, and deep prayer, pleasing to God, is performed very similarly to forgiveness, which is why the Lord, speaking of the power of prayer, speaks also of forgiveness. Recently I saw this same thought in the Compendium of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, prepared by Pope Benedict XVI. I was glad to learn that Catholics think so too.

One must forgive from the whole sincere heart, soul, and mind, and so too must one pray sincerely.

Forgiveness rather gives relief to feelings, diminishing the old and giving way to the new, often accompanying a person's renewal and transfiguration. So too prayer — no matter how fervent, it requires relief and renewal in the action of the grace of the Holy Spirit. This is partly similar to the difference between infatuation and love — the former has more noticeable experiences and emotions, perhaps more vivid, whereas the latter grants more certainty and overall gives greater fulfillment. The former is partial, often inconsistent with reason, values, etc., whereas love fills the whole person and grants wholeness.

Forgiveness, if it is good, allows for reducing evil in the present and future. So too prayer is called to the same end.

Forgiveness ought not to hinder or stop (and true forgiveness does not hinder or stop) the struggle against evil, sin, and passions, nor should true prayer. If a person can properly stop and overcome evil while forgiving, that is good. So too with prayer. Evil often divides a person — instead, one must withstand this — giving strength to good feelings in forgiveness, and to the will in struggle and victory.

I will add a few words about the word 'ἀφῆτε' — 'aphete' — you forgive, set free, let go, release; scatter (as an army in battle); remit debts; leave (it also has other meanings that do not seem relevant in this context; overall a very polysemous word). I will focus on the polysemy of this word. Generally, its clearest meaning in the New Testament is you forgive, and the form of forgiveness (as it ought to be, as it manifests itself) is you release, let go. That is, in the most general sense, Gospel forgiveness and commandments pertain to the remission of sins, debts, the possibility for them not to affect us ourselves, becoming invulnerable to them because of their missing us, etc. That is, an unforgiven sin in this sense strikes the person who does not forgive, thereby causing them pain. And to forgive means to make it so that sin misses, passes by without causing harm. This corresponds to dodging a blow in martial arts — sin strikes at a person, but the one who forgives it in the Gospel sense dodges the impact. But this same word also carries the meaning of routing an army in battle or war — that is, in certain cases forgiveness is equivalent to complete, final victory in a war or battle. Sometimes forgiveness can mean separation, that is, being in safety, not simply dodging an impact, but moving to a safe place. And so forth; that is, Christian forgiveness is polysemous and has many manifestations and forms.
