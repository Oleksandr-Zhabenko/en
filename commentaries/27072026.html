Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians XI, 31-XII, 6)
1 Corinthians XI, 31 — 'ἑαυτοὺς διεκρίνομεν' — 'eautous diekrinomen' - discerned ourselves; judged ourselves; were attentive to ourselves. Most likely the latter is intended here. The Apostle speaks not so much of self-condemnation as of vigilance—the attentiveness required lest we fall asleep. If we were sufficiently attentive to ourselves and properly discerned what is necessary, there would be fewer sins.

1 Corinthians XI, 32 — 'ὑπὸ τοῦ κυρίου παιδευόμεθα' — 'hypo tou kyriou paideuometha' - are disciplined [chastened, instructed] by the Lord. The preposition 'hypo' with the genitive case indicates the passive state of the verb disciplined/instructed; the Lord as a loving Father provides spiritual upbringing. He directs our attention and mind toward self-knowledge, repentance, growth in virtue, and conversion. 'παιδευόμεθα' - 'paideuometha' - were disciplined/nurtured as children. 'κατακριθῶμεν' - 'katakrithomen' - might be condemned; receive a verdict (as a potential outcome). All this indicates that we must be truly attentive to ourselves, for through such vigilance one may reach a state where grave sins are avoided, removing the cause for condemnation. One can thus become conformed to Christ — not by one's own power or alertness alone, but through active synergy ('synergeia') with God and His divine grace.

1 Corinthians XI, 33 — 'εἰς τὸ φαγεῖν' — 'eis to phagein' - for eating; to eat. Denotes purpose.

1 Corinthians XI, 34 — 'ἐν οἴκῳ' — 'en oiko' - at home. Where. 'εἰς κρίμα' - 'eis krima' - unto judgment; unto condemnation. That is, the faithful do not gather in order to be judged, yet through negligence and sinfulness they may lapse into sin and fall under judgment.

1 Corinthians XII, 3 — 'ἐν πνεύματι θεοῦ' — 'en pneumati theou' - in the Spirit of God. Rather than instrumental (by the Spirit of God), because the latter risks presenting the Holy Spirit merely as an instrument rather than as Lord. The overall meaning of the phrase is in the Holy Spirit. 'ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ' - 'en pneumati agio' - in the Holy Spirit. Analogous to the preceding. To speak in the Holy Spirit signifies uttering what the Holy Spirit, God Himself, wills and bestows. Google Gemini Flash-3.6 adds here a beautiful passage by the link:
https://gemini.google.com/share/901c8daff8ce

When a human being participates in the Trinitarian action through the preposition 'en', there is no confusion or mixture of subjects (which would be a theological error). Instead, we witness a profound mystical synergy. The human person preserves their distinct identity and free will, yet acts from within the Divine sphere. It is a state of hypostatic distinction maintained within perfect spiritual unity, where God remains the transcendent Source, and man acts as a living, distinct co-worker.

1 Corinthians XII, 4-6 — 'διαιρέσεις' — 'diaireseis' - varieties, distributions, divisions, distinctions.

1 Corinthians XII, 6 — 'πάντα ἐν πᾶσιν' — 'panta en pasin' - all things in all [persons/things]. While both translations are grammatically valid, the personal sense (all things in all people) is more precise in meaning. The passage conveys that God bestows all good gifts upon everyone; everything that is genuinely good originates from God. Since all true value comes from Him, He works all truly valuable things in all people.

The Apostle continues to speak concerning human conscience, exhorting believers to remain mindful and repent of sins so as not to eat and drink judgment unto themselves.

He then addresses the pedagogical value of trials encountered by the faithful.

Paul goes on to regulate the practice of the 'agape' feasts (love-feasts) so that they may be conducted worthily.

At the opening of Chapter XII, he introduces the topic of spiritual gifts ('charismata') and ministries — encompassing everything the Lord bestows upon the faithful not merely for their personal benefit, but for others and the building up of the Church.

He warns against divisions based on differences in gifts, teaching once more unity of mind and oneness in Christ, in the Holy Spirit, and in God.

This is a crucial New Testament passage witnessing to the Most Holy Trinity. The order in which the Divine Persons are named moves in reverse sequence relative to our ascent to the Father — in the Holy Spirit, through the Son, unto the Father — outlining the path of human divinisation ('theosis').

(Matthew XVIII, 1-11)
Matthew XVIII, 1, 4 — 'Ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ὥρᾳ' — 'En ekeine te hora' - at that hour; at that time. Set phrase. When. 'ἐν τῇ βασιλείᾳ τῶν οὐρανῶν' - 'en te basileia ton ouranon' - in the Kingdom of Heaven. Where.

Matthew XVIII, 2, 4-5 — 'ἐν μέσῳ αὐτῶν' — 'en meso auton' - in the midst of them. Where. Set phrase. 'παιδίον' - 'paidion' - a little child.

Matthew XVIII, 3 — 'εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν τῶν οὐρανῶν' — 'eis ten basileian ton ouranon' - into the Kingdom of Heaven. Where to. The preposition 'eis' denotes directional movement toward an ultimate destination.

Matthew XVIII, 5 — 'ὃς ἐὰν δέξηται ἓν παιδίον τοιοῦτο ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματί μου' — 'hos ean dexetai hen paidion toiouto epi to onomati mou' - whoever receives one such little child in My name. The concluding phrase carries dual depth: one may receive a child for the sake of Christ (to fulfill His will), or as a Christian (as one who represents Christ and bears His name). Both dimensions are contained within these words. In truth, such a person receives the child as Christ, while simultaneously manifesting Christ to that child. In this mutual, reciprocal manifestation of Christianity lies the heart of receiving Christ. When Christ speaks in the parable of the Last Judgment (Matthew XXV, 31-46) of you did it unto Me, He desires above all this very reciprocal Christianity.

Matthew XVIII, 6, 10 — 'μικρῶν' — 'mikron' - little ones [in size, number, or social standing — here primarily the latter]. 'πιστευόντων εἰς ἐμέ' - 'pisteuonton eis eme' - who believe in(to) Me. The preposition 'eis' stresses dynamic movement toward Christ in faith, highlighting the compelling power of trust in Him. 'ἐν τῷ πελάγει τῆς θαλάσσης' - 'en to pelagei tes thalasses' - in the depth of the sea. Where.

Matthew XVIII, 7 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν σκανδάλων' — 'apo ton skandalon' - because of offenses/stumbling blocks/temptations. The preposition 'apo' points to an accompanying or secondary condition alongside the root cause. Stumbling blocks / temptations in themselves are not the ultimate tragedy, but falling into temptation is harmful; when offenses abound and press heavily, avoiding a fall becomes difficult. Nevertheless, the cause of suffering is not the temptation itself, but the fall. We must remember that crowns are awarded for overcoming temptation. Christ subsequently unpacks this truth more deeply.

Matthew XVIII, 8, 9 — 'ἀπὸ σοῦ' — 'apo sou' - from you. The preposition 'apo' emphasizes casting off, removal, and the total severance of connection. 'εἰς τὴν ζωήν' - 'eis ten zoen' - into the [eternal] life. Where to. 'εἰς τὸ πῦρ' - 'eis to pyr' - into the [eternal] fire. Where to. 'εἰς τὴν γέενναν τοῦ πυρός' - 'eis ten geennan tou pyros' - into the hell [Gehenna] of fire. Where to. The preposition 'eis' highlights both movement and the final destination.

Matthew XVIII, 10 — 'ἐν οὐρανοῖς' — 'en ouranois' - in heavens. Where.

Verse 11 is absent in certain textual witnesses of the Gospel.

This passage is extraordinarily rich; let us summarize its core points:

Why is a little child placed in the midst of the Apostles as an example? Because a child is constantly learning, receptive to what is new, and entering into it. This capacity to receive new life, to develop, and to experience continual renewal is what we must learn from little children. To enter the Kingdom of God — that is, to begin living according to God's will — is possible only when a person truly begins to learn like a little child, being renewed daily after the image of Jesus Christ.

Receiving a little child is a profound concept. While often understood as adopting children in the name of Jesus Christ out of love, it yields a broader meaning: in receiving a child, guided solely by pure love and faith, a person exhibits the very disposition God intended when one genuinely receives Jesus Christ. In this sense, a little child icons the Crucified and Risen Savior.

This principle extends to any instance where a Christian or any human being manifests pure love, faith, and humility: in doing so, they receive Christ. For the person who has already done this, receiving Christ signifies new life and continual spiritual renewal.

Regarding temptations ('skandala'): Christ warns that woe comes upon the world because of offenses, and especially upon the person through whom the offense comes:

1. Temptation can lead to a fall and spiritual ruin, even for many people or whole communities.

2. In speaking of overcoming temptation, Christ notes that it is better to enter life maimed — meaning that in victory over sin, one sacrifices what was tied to the flesh (a hand, an eye, a foot) to avoid total destruction. Temptation involves loss; though victory is far better than capitulation, a state free from stumbling blocks remains ideal.

3. We must distinguish internal temptations from external ones. It is always better to be free of internal lusts. External temptations (such as those from the evil one) do not prove a readiness to sin, but expose human weakness; when overcome rightly, they yield glory. Saint James notes that he who overcomes temptation receives a crown of life, yet James also writes that God tempts no one, but each is tempted by his own desire ('epithymia'). To conquer desire and temptation brings a crown, yet remaining pure from such desire is higher still. This differs from Christ's temptation in the wilderness, which served divine providence: the Holy Spirit led Christ into the wilderness, and upon being defeated, the devil was put to shame, while Christ lost nothing through the ordeal.

Regarding the right hand or right eye: these do not denote physical body parts, but represent prized connections or habits that lead to sin. To overcome sin, two key qualities are needed: decisive resolution (pluck it out and cast it from you) and a child-like capacity to receive new life (whoever does not receive the Kingdom of God like a little child will not enter it, Mark X, 14-15).

To conquer sin and embrace purity, one must first recognize the temptation (the right member). Then, one must cultivate a love for purity, fidelity, God, neighbor, and one's true self in the heart, decisively casting sin aside. This establishes a movement from the external senses (eyes, hands, feet) inward to the heart. When a person is liberated from sin, the heart fills with gratitude toward God. Where a person is deeply bound to sin, such liberation feels like denying oneself — as if losing a part of one's very self.

Regarding the warning against contempt (do not despise one of these little ones): Christ states that their Guardian Angels always behold the face of the Father. That which Great King David sought in the Psalms (make Your face to shine upon Your servant), and which only the greatest saints beheld in part, is the constant reality for the angels of these little ones. God sends these angels as ministering spirits (Hebrews I, 14) to demonstrate His love and care for them. Therefore, to despise one of these little ones is to despise God's great love.
