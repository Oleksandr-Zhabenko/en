Liturgy:
(Ephesians I, 1-9)
Ephesians I, 1, 3 — 'ἐν Ἐφέσῳ' — 'en Epheso' — at Ephesus. Where. These words are absent in some manuscripts. 'ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' - 'en CHristo Iesou' — in Christ Jesus. In Whom.

Ephesians I, 2 — 'ἀπὸ θεοῦ' — 'apo theou' — from God. The preposition 'apo' in such usage emphasizes that grace and peace are gifts of God.

Ephesians I, 3 — 'ἐν πάσῃ εὐλογίᾳ' — 'en pase eulogia' — with all spiritual blessings; in every blessing. Both translation options are possible. The first indicates that God's blessings in Christ are exceedingly blessed, wonderful, and beautiful, whilst the second — that they are granted to people. See also about the nuances of such usage at the link:
https://gemini.google.com/share/901c8daff8ce
'ἐν τοῖς ἐπουρανίοις' — 'en tois epouraniois' — in the heavens. Literally in the super-heavenly (places). Here the prefix emphasizes that this blessing pertains to Christ Himself, Who ascended into the heavens, that is, it is He Who is in the heavens, and through Him — the blessing.

Ephesians I, 4, 9 — 'ἐν αὐτῷ' — 'en auto' — in Him; in Himself. Rather the first translation option, although the second is also possible. Then it refers to the Council of the Most Holy Trinity. 'ἐν ἀγάπῃ' - 'en agape' — in love; by love. The second option is also possible, although rather the first. See the link above.

Ephesians I, 5 — 'προορίσας' — 'proorisas' — having foreordained; having predetermined; having set beforehand. Here it refers to God designing human beings so that together with Him they might freely live in eternal love and joy, be good, holy, and the like. It does not speak here of God determining who will be saved and who will not, but of the very purpose of creating the world and humans in particular. 'εἰς υἱοθεσίαν' - 'eis hyiothesian' — unto the adoption of children; for adoption as God's children. Very capacious words. 'εἰς αὐτόν' - 'eis auton' — to Himself. Here it indicates also that God grants humans Himself and thereby makes them children of God, children to Himself (which is properly adoption).

Ephesians I, 6 — 'εἰς ἔπαινον' — 'eis epainon' — to the praise; for praise. Where to and/or for what. 'ἐν τῷ ἠγαπημένῳ' - 'en to egapemeno' — in the Beloved. In Whom.

Ephesians I, 7 — 'ἐν ᾧ' — 'en ho' — in Whom. In Whom.

Ephesians I, 8 — 'εἰς ἡμᾶς' — 'eis hemas' — toward us; for us. Where to and for whom. The first option signifies that God multiplied wisdom in human nature, in people through Christ, whilst the second — that it is granted to people and for their sake. 'ἐν πάσῃ' - 'en pase' — in all; with all. Both translation options are possible.

The weekday readings from the Epistle of the Apostle Paul to the Ephesians begin. The Epistle, like others, is profoundly theological.
As with most other epistles, the Apostle Paul begins with an address, praise of God, and spiritual wishes, as well as thanksgiving. By this, as noted earlier, the apostles' preaching differs particularly from the preaching of the prophets.
The introduction is solemn, elevated, and festive, since the entire epistle touches upon the high mysteries of God, so the very beginning tunes one to the height of thought.
Nevertheless, this does not mean pride, but a humble thought regarding high things.

(Mark VII, 24-34)
Mark VII, 24 — 'εἰς τὰ ὅρια Τύρου' — 'eis ta horia Tyrou' — into the borders / coasts of Tyre. 'εἰς οἰκίαν' - 'eis oikian' — into an house. Where to.

Mark VII, 26, 29 — 'ἐκ τῆς θυγατρός' — 'ek tes thygatros' — out of her daughter. The preposition 'ek' indicates here from where the demon was to be cast out.

Mark VII, 28 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ψιχίων τῶν παιδίων' — 'apo ton psikhion ton paidion' — of the children's crumbs. The preposition 'apo' indicates that this is a gift from the children to the dogs.

Mark VII, 30 — 'εἰς τὸν οἶκον' — 'eis ton oikon' — to her house. Where to.

The casting out of the demon from the daughter of the Gentile woman.

As this passage is understood, Christ's dialogue did not aim to offend or disparage the woman or Gentiles (that is, at that time — all non-Jews) in general, for Christ knew that He would heal the daughter, and knew of the woman's faith. But the Jews often sinned with a contemptuous attitude toward Gentiles. Therefore Christ behaves as those Jews… in order to actually put them to shame. For He shows what the woman's faith and humility actually are, her trust in God in Christ — those qualities that the Jews often lacked.
The situation is thus very similar to the healing of the centurion's servant and the praise of his faith. See, in particular in Matthew:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/02072023.html
If we delve even deeper, there is a parallel with the prophet Elijah's stay in those lands at a time when there had been no rain for three and a half years at his word. Then too a woman obeyed Elijah, and through him — the Lord, and was blessed thereby. Christ is Greater than Elijah. It is also an example of how, although God's Revelation in Old Testament times was primarily directed to the Jews, the Lord cared for other nations as well, and clearly manifests this in the time of Christ.
And further, that Christ did not despise the Gentile woman can be seen from the fact that… He praised her immediately after her words. If someone in pride despised another, and saw the humility or at least modesty of the person they despised, they would... become even more haughty (emotions would take over). But Christ, instead of haughtiness — manifests love and mercy. This also directly shows that He had no desire to offend and did not offend the woman, but on the contrary, wanted to protect and praise her (to set her as an example).
