Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians XII, 10-19)
2 Corinthians XII, 10 — 'ἐν ἀσθενείαις, ἐν ὕβρεσιν, ἐν ἀνάγκαις, ἐν διωγμοῖς' — 'en astheneiais, en ybresin, en anankais, en digmois' — in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses. In what. These words of the Apostle Paul can be better understood in view of the usage slightly earlier in 2 Corinthians XII, 5, 9 'ἐν ταῖς ἀσθενείαις' - 'en tais astheneiais' — in the infirmities; by the infirmities. Both translation options are possible — the first indicates that these circumstances serve as a background for the action of God's grace, which is what is truly important; whilst the second indicates that the infirmities themselves become an occasion for glory and boasting if they do not hinder or even help a person to unite with God's grace, with God. Also 'ἐν ἀσθενείᾳ' - 'en astheneia' — in weakness; through weakness. See more at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/19102025.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/03112024.html
It can also be said that difficult circumstances reveal the weakness of human nature, but if a person overcomes them, if they remain faithful to God and glorify God, then these circumstances do not hinder the action of grace, of God, but also reveal and show more clearly God's help, strengthening, love, and comfort.

2 Corinthians XII, 11 — 'ὑφ’ ὑμῶν συνίστασθαι' — 'hyph hymon synistasthai' — to be compelled by you; to be commended by you. The preposition 'hypo' in its form before a following 'hy' is used here with the genitive case and indicates the passive voice of the verb to be compelled; to be commended. This expression here indicates that the apostle's words were caused by the necessity to align his words with the expectations of the Corinthians, who wanted to see heroic virtues. The apostle boasts not because he wants to boast, but because otherwise the Corinthians would have listened to those who spread deception among Christians, since those people did not refrain from praising themselves. The apostle truthfully commends himself so that the Corinthians, seeking what is praiseworthy in the eyes of men, would not fall victim to such pursuits and the initiative (resourcefulness) of false teachers.

2 Corinthians XII, 12 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν ἐν πάσῃ ὑπομονῇ' — 'en hymin en pase hypomone' — among you in all patience; among you with all patience. Both translation options are possible, similarly to verses 5, 9, 11 above.

2 Corinthians XII, 19 — 'κατέναντι θεοῦ ἐν Χριστῷ' — 'katenanti theou en CHristo' — before God (the Father) in Christ.

The reading is a continuation of Paul's apologia regarding his personal ministry and his fellow worker Titus.

The omitted passage is where Paul, for the sake of defense, in an outburst recounts the story of his conversion and God's revelations to him personally. On Paul's (Saul's) conversion, I wrote at the links:

Paul says that God's power is made perfect (a word derived from 'end, goal') in weakness. That is, human weakness can serve as a means of overcoming all temptations through the action of God's power in the process. It can be put this way: in Paul's example in this situation we see that for a person whom God strengthens and upholds, to whom He gives power, their own human weaknesses can then serve as a means of victory over temptations (and therefore over sins). It can be said otherwise: if God upholds a person, gives power, then whether a person is strong or weak — they can conquer temptations and sins through this Divine power. It is worth recalling that in the Old Testament there is a similar example — the Lord saved Israel from the Midianites through Gideon and 300 men, with a small number, so that the Israelites would not subsequently boast that they conquered by themselves, rather than God conquering for them.

Since the words in verse 10, if understood literally in one and the same sense, are absurd and foolish, Paul mentions this apparent foolishness in verse 11. It must be borne in mind that weak and strong are not in one and the same respect, so in reality, despite the paradoxical nature (factually in literature there is such a stylistic device as an oxymoron, which occurs particularly in the Gospels as well, although the apostle's words are not merely a stylistic device, but convey a deep truth), there is nothing foolish or irrational in these words. To commend / boast — in the biblical sense — means to consider worthy, proper, deserving of praise, worthy of recommending to others. Paul means that the Corinthians ought to value his labor, his gifts from God for their own edification, his work, as well as the work of his fellow laborers who acted in the same way as Paul (Titus was not only a fellow worker of Paul, but also his disciple). Further Paul refutes the argument about hidden motives and actions, when his apostleship and love for the Corinthians were manifest.

Speaking of parents laying up for the children, Paul expresses his faith in a certain progress in the development of society and humanity — the next ought to be better than the previous. Also Paul asserts by these words that subsequent generations ought according to God's plan to live better than previous ones. If this is not so, then it is wrong.

Verse 19 shows that often people who excuse themselves feel a certain guilt within themselves, qualms of conscience; therefore Paul, denying that he is excusing himself, testifies that he is not guilty of anything in his apostolic ministry among the Corinthians.

(Mark IV, 10-23)
Mark IV, 11 — 'ἐν παραβολαῖς' — 'en parabolais' — in parables. That is, people see certain riddles, meanings that are not direct or immediate, but indirect.

Mark IV, 15 — 'παρὰ τὴν ὁδὸν' — 'para ten hodon' — to the proximity with the road; alongside the road The preposition 'para' here is used with the accusative case and indicates that it refers to seeds that fell near the road, where they are visible and where the ground is hard. 'ἐν αὐτοῖς' - 'en autois' — in them. Where.

Mark IV, 17 — 'ἐν ἑαυτοῖς' — 'en heautois' — in themselves. Where.

Mark IV, 21 — 'ὑπὸ τὸν μόδιον' — 'hypo ton modion' — under a bushel (a measuring vessel of about 9 litres). 'ὑπὸ τὴν κλίνην' - 'hypo ten klinen' — under a bed. The preposition 'hypo' here in both cases is used with the accusative case and indicates under what something is placed.

Mark IV, 22 — 'εἰς φανερόν' — 'eis phaneron' — abroad; into the light. Set expression with the meaning of the second translation option.
