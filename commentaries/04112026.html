Liturgy:
(1 Thessalonians II, 1-8)
1 Thessalonians II, 2 — 'ἐν Φιλίπποις' — 'en Philippois' — at Philippi. Where. 'ἐν τῷ θεῷ ἡμῶν' - 'en to theo hemon' — in our God. In Whom. 'ἐν πολλῷ ἀγῶνι' - 'en pollo agoni' — with much contention / in great struggle. Rather in what conditions (external), than in what state (internal). The apostles eagerly preached, but externally there were afflictions.

1 Thessalonians II, 3 — 'ἐκ πλάνης' — 'ek planes' — of deceit / out of error. 'ἐξ ἀκαθαρσίας' - 'ex akatharsias' — of uncleanness. The preposition 'ek' in both cases emphasizes that the calling was true and holy. 'ἐν δόλῳ' - 'en dolo' — in guile / with deceit. Analogous to the previous, but denying guile in preaching and conduct.

1 Thessalonians II, 4 — 'καθὼς δεδοκιμάσμεθα ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ πιστευθῆναι' — 'kathos dedokimasmetha hypo tou theou pisteuthenai' — as we were allowed of God to be put in trust with the gospel / in the measure we were tested by God to receive trust and commission. The preposition 'hypo' here takes the genitive case and indicates the passive voice tested, as well as Who tested the apostles. The apostle uses the image of stewardship and commissioning someone to act on behalf of the master. A grammatically similar turn is used to describe authority in Romans XIII. See more about that at the links:

The authority of the apostles was directly established by God, in the measure to which the apostles could exercise it, whereas regarding any other authority it remains a question whether people are called to it directly by God. Here it concerns apostolic authority — yes, it was indeed established by God Himself, each of them called by God personally in view of the measure in which he could fulfill this ministry. One can state it otherwise: if authority (any authority, in the general case) carries out its activity according to God's will, in accordance with God's commandments and designs, then it is personally called by God, similarly as we have in these words regarding the preachers of the Gospel — the apostles. If, however, authority and its representatives violate God's will or even oppose God, then upon their authority there is only God's permission, but not His will; God permitted that authority. The measure of being called by God is whether the ministry is performed according to God's will, whether it is carried out and established lawfully, rightly, and honestly.

1 Thessalonians II, 5 — 'ἐν λόγῳ κολακίας' — 'en logo kolakias' — by flattering words / in flattering speech. 'ἐν προφάσει πλεονεξίας' - 'en prophasei pleonexias' — by a cloke of covetousness / under the pretext of greed. Both translation options are possible in both places.

1 Thessalonians II, 6 — 'ἐξ ἀνθρώπων' — 'ex anthropon' — of people / from people. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates that the glory mentioned here — human glory obtained dishonestly — indeed originates from men, being a consequence of their limitation. 'ἀφ’ ὑμῶν' - 'aph hymon' — from you. 'ἀπ’ ἄλλων' - 'ap allon' — from others. The preposition 'apo' in its forms in both instances points out that those who seek dishonest glory from men deceive them and steal the capacity of someone to glorify, stealing glory. 'ἐν βάρει' - 'en barei' - burdensome / as a burden; by weight. Implies that the apostolic ministry requires constant, intense labour.

1 Thessalonians II, 7 — 'ἐν μέσῳ ὑμῶν' — 'en meso hymon' — among you / in the midst of you. Set expression. Where, among whom.

If the apostles had sought glory from people (here named in the sense of the primary motive of actions, the goal), they could not have sought God's things with all their heart, nor could they have so cared for salvation and possessed the necessary zeal, or worse — they could have drawn away from Christ to the point of opposing Him Himself. It is not a matter of diminishing the significance of glory among people, but of seeing its limitations in comparison with God's glory, as well as in the integrity of the apostles' ministry and faith, in fulfilling the commandment of love for God in fullness. It must also be remembered that among pagans especially, glory received from them may have sinful roots, which is why in reality it can even be a manifestation of sins, and not a cause of true joy, but of sorrow. In this latter meaning, the explanation of the words is self-evident.
The reading concludes with words of love and attachment. For a person, their soul is of supreme value, therefore to give one's soul out of love is spoken figuratively of extraordinary love.

(Luke XI, 9-13)
Luke XI, 11 — 'τίνα δὲ ἐξ ὑμῶν' — 'tina de ex hymon' — which of you. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates here selection of a representative from a group, that is, generally speaking of all disciples of Christ in the person of each one mentioned here.

Luke XI, 13 — 'ἐξ οὐρανοῦ' — 'ex ouranou' — from heaven. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates from where the Father will bestow the gift of the Holy Spirit and from where the Holy Spirit Himself will come.

The explanation that the Heavenly Father is kinder than any earthly parents is eloquent.
…being evil — that is, not essentially evil (the source of evil), not criminals or true scoundrels, but as those who turn out to be evil, unkind, sinful, in a word. Christ sees the efforts of people to be good, but also sees that often they fail at it.
If compared with the parallel passage in Matthew (Matthew VII, 7-11), Luke has a distinction from Matthew: Matthew writes about good things (gifts), whereas Luke writes about the Holy Spirit as the gift. Nevertheless, there is no contradiction between the evangelists, for the gift of the Holy Spirit is also a gift that bestows other gifts, while at the same time a person receives gifts with the participation of the Holy Spirit. But still there is a distinction, let us pay attention to it.
Luke, writing about the Holy Spirit, thereby says: The Holy Spirit is the most valuable and greatest gift, for He is God Himself. And what can be greater than God?. And if the Lord gives the greatest gift — the Holy Spirit, then with Him He will also grant that which is added. And if it pleases God not to grant something, but still to bestow the Holy Spirit, He is the Comforter, and overall it means that it is better so, and God knows that.
On the other hand, if one simply asks for gifts, one may receive God's bounties, but not receive the wealth of the Holy Spirit, since He is a Person Who may not enter where He is not desired.
On the other hand, for many people asking for specific gifts is more understandable than asking for the gift of the Holy Spirit, so Matthew also correctly conveys the words of Christ. One must remember them both in their unity and distinction.
