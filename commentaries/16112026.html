Liturgy:
(2 Thessalonians I, 1-10)
2 Thessalonians I, 1 — 'ἐν θεῷ πατρὶ ἡμῶν καὶ κυρίῳ Ἰησοῦ Χριστῷ' — 'en theo patri hemon kai kyrio Iesou CHristo' — in God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. In Whom.

2 Thessalonians I, 2 — 'ἀπὸ θεοῦ πατρὸς ἡμῶν καὶ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ' — 'apo theou patros hemon kai kyriou Iesou CHristou' — from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. The preposition 'apo' indicates that grace and peace are God's gifts to humanity.

2 Thessalonians I, 3 — 'εἰς ἀλλήλους' — 'eis allelous' — toward each other. Set expression.

2 Thessalonians I, 4 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν ἐνκαυχᾶσθαι ἐν ταῖς ἐκκλησίαις τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'en hymin enkaukhasthai en tais ekklesiais tou theou' — we glory in you in the churches of God / we boast of you in God's Churches. By sense, only these translation options apply here.

2 Thessalonians I, 5 — 'εἰς τὸ καταξιωθῆναι' — 'eis to kataxiothenai' — that you may be counted worthy. The preposition 'eis' indicates the goal.

2 Thessalonians I, 6 — 'παρὰ θεῷ' — 'para theo' — in the vicinity of God; near God. The preposition 'para' here takes the dative case, indicating that righteousness brings one closer to God.

2 Thessalonians I, 7 — 'ἐν τῇ ἀποκαλύψει' — 'en te apokalypsei' — in the revelation / at the revealing. 'ἀπ’ οὐρανοῦ' - 'ap ouranou' — from heaven. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that Christ will descend from heaven.

2 Thessalonians I, 8 — 'ἐν πυρὶ φλογός' — 'en pyri phlogos' — in flaming fire. In what.

2 Thessalonians I, 9 — 'ἀπὸ προσώπου τοῦ κυρίου καὶ ἀπὸ τῆς δόξης' — 'apo prosopou tou kyriou kai apo tes doxes' — from the face of the Lord, and from the glory. The preposition 'apo' here in both instances indicates a false (apparent) cause of destruction. People will perish not from God's glory and the presence of the Lord, but from their own unrepented sins and vices. See more regarding the analogous usage of this preposition at the links:

2 Thessalonians I, 10 — 'ἐν τοῖς ἁγίοις αὐτοῦ' — 'en tois hagiois autou' — in His saints; by His saints. Since the glory of the saints does not increase essentially God's own glory, the first translation option is better. 'ἐν πᾶσιν τοῖς πιστεύσασιν' - 'en pasin tois pisteusasin' — in all them believing / in all believers. Analogous to the previous. 'ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ ἐκείνῃ' - 'en te hemera ekeine' — in that day.

After greeting and giving thanks to God, the Apostle Paul comforts and encourages the Thessalonians in expectation of the Second Coming of Christ and says that it has not yet arrived (contrary to the opinion of some who thought otherwise even then, yet there are also those who thought so subsequently, including today).
Paul writes from the point of view of the Thessalonians themselves — that is, he simply expresses moral and emotional support to them in their sufferings for Christ without delving deeply into theological and ethical questions. The Thessalonians longed for justice, so Paul writes of God as the Righteous Judge. The Lord is indeed such, yet one must not forget God's mercy and love, which stand higher than judgment.
In view of the expectation of the glorious Second Coming of Jesus Christ, the Apostle prompts the faithful toward goodness and growth in faith and piety.

(Luke XII, 13-15, 22-31)
Luke XII, 13 — 'τις ἐκ τοῦ ὄχλου' — 'tis ek tou okhlou' — one of the crowd / someone from the crowd. The preposition 'ek' indicates here from where the man spoke.

Luke XII, 15 — 'ἀπὸ πάσης πλεονεξίας' — 'apo pases pleonexias' — of covetousness / from all greed. The preposition 'apo' underlines that one must beware of it in every way and completely. 'ἐν τῷ περισσεύειν' - 'en to perisseuein' — in the abundance. 'ἐκ τῶν ὑπαρχόντων' - 'ek ton hyparkhonton' — of the things which he possesses / from all that he owns. The preposition 'ek' indicates origin. That is, the fullness of life, its richness and completeness, does not originate from possessions, from property. The word order here leads more fittingly to such a translation, although the rendering that life does not depend on the abundance of possessions is more widespread.

Luke XII, 25 — 'τίς δὲ ἐξ ὑμῶν' — 'tis de ex hymon' — and which of you. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates selection from among a group, and here generally representation of this group — that is, the disciples, and more broadly, all people.

Luke XII, 27 — 'ἐν πάσῃ τῇ δόξῃ' — 'en pase te doxe' — in all his glory / in all the [known] glory.

Luke XII, 28 — 'ἐν ἀγρῷ' — 'en argo' — in the field. Where. 'εἰς κλίβανον' - 'eis klibanon' — into the oven. Where to.

The beginning of the reading — a unique tradition of Luke — shows Jesus declining to judge between brothers regarding an inheritance. Next, He Himself teaches what the attitude toward property should be so that such a division is fair and right. Had the petitioner's brother heeded Jesus' words and had there been grounds to share the property, he would have shared it. Yet at the same time, the brother's request might also have been prompted by a desire to gain additional property through Jesus' authority. In effect, this was an attempt to draw Jesus into a matter for which there was no divine will; therefore, Jesus responds with a question, and then with teaching.
