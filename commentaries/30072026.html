1 Corinthians XIV, 6 — 'ἢ ἐν ἀποκαλύψει ἢ ἐν γνώσει ἢ ἐν προφητείᾳ ἢ διδαχῇ' — 'e en apokalypsei e en gnosei e en propheteia e didakhe' — in revelation, or in knowledge, or in prophecy, or teaching; by revelation, or by knowledge, or by prophecy, or by teaching. Both options are possible, as well as their combinations. Google Gemini Flash-3.6 gives additional valuable notes by the link:

1 Corinthians XIV, 8 — 'ἄδηλος' — 'adelos' — unknown, secret, hidden, incomprehensible, indistinct. 'εἰς πόλεμον' - 'eis polemon' — to battle, unto war. The preposition 'eis' indicates here the directionality of the action.

1 Corinthians XIV, 9 — 'εἰς ἀέρα' — 'eis aera' — into the air; into the wind. A set expression meaning in vain.

1 Corinthians XIV, 10 — 'ἐν κόσμῳ' — 'en kosmo' — in the world. Where. 'ἄφωνον' - 'aphonon' — voiceless; soundless; without sound. Instead of sense, Paul names the sound. One can think that the sound, the phonetics of all languages was to his liking. Paul likely also had a fine musical ear.

1 Corinthians XIV, 11 — 'ἐν ἐμοί' — 'en emoi' — in me; to me.

1 Corinthians XIV, 19 — 'ἐν ἐκκλησίᾳ' — 'en ekklesia' — in the Church. Where. 'ἐν γλώσσῃ' - 'en glosse' — in glossolalia; in such a way as if it were glossolalia.

Paul speaks about the gift of speaking in various tongues. I wrote about that earlier, here presenting it briefly.

Quoting somewhat rewritten:

On the day of Pentecost,
the saint apostles received
the gift of speaking in different languages,
that is, without learning,
they could preach in the languages of the nations
to which they went with the good news of Christ.
But when Paul speaks of the Corinthians
who had the gift of speaking in languages,
he is talking about uttering different sounds
under the influence of the Holy Spirit.
These sounds could have a more musical character,
from which melodies could be born,
in particular for prayers, psalms, liturgy.
And states of admiration
could be a way of expression, joy, elation, etc.
They occurred unexpectedly without the will of the person themselves,
but if they occurred at least once in someone,
they could occur again many times in the future.
That is why the apostle speaks of ten thousand words in tongues,
although the glossolalia were never too verbose.
Many Christians considered these sounds
as similar to the languages of angels,
which also communicate with each other.
In the previous chapter,
the apostle mentions the languages of angels.
For many outsiders such glossolalia
could be estimated ambiguously.

End of quotation.

(Matthew XX, 17-28)
Matthew XX, 17, 18 — 'εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα' — 'eis Hierosolyma' — into Jerusalem. Where to. 'ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ' - 'en te hodo' — on the way. Where, when. 'εἰς θάνατον' - 'eis thanaton' — unto death. The preposition 'eis' indicates here where the elders of the people will send Christ.

Matthew XX, 19 — 'εἰς τὸ ἐμπαῖξαι' — 'eis to empaixai' — unto mockery. Here the preposition 'eis' indicates where the elders will direct Christ (as above in verse 18).

Matthew XX, 20 — 'παρ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'par autou' — from beside Him; from Him. The preposition 'para' (in form before a vowel) here is with the genitive case. Such a construction is used regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father, regarding the Most Holy Trinity, regarding the whole of God's design, also regarding the human nature of Jesus Christ. Here it indicates an attempt by the mother to obtain a promise from Jesus that would evaluate the spiritual gifts of her children, their relationship with Jesus, and result in a high position for those disciples in the future.

Matthew XX, 21, 23 — 'ἐκ δεξιῶν' — 'ek dexion' — on the right side. 'ἐξ εὐωνύμων' - 'ex euonymon' — on the left side. Set expressions. 'ἐν τῇ βασιλείᾳ σου' - 'en te basileia sou' — in Your Kingdom. Where, when.

Matthew XX, 23 — 'οἷς ἡτοίμασται ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός μου' — 'hois hetoimastai hypo tou patros mou' — for whom it is (already) prepared by My Father. The preposition 'hypo' here is with the genitive case, indicating the passive voice of the participle corresponding in translation to [for whom it] is prepared.

Matthew XX, 26, 27 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'en hymin' — in you; among you. Where, among whom.

The second part, in particular the words of Christ at the end, requires detailed, deep analysis. I have written earlier, drawing attention to the grammatical complexity of this and parallel places in the other synoptic Gospels, but did not fully understand the whole depth, so today I want to deepen the research to see the full picture. Let us compare Matthew XX, 26-28, Mark IX, 35-37, and Luke XXII, 25-27.

In Matthew:
'οὐχ οὕτως ἔσται ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'oukh houtos estai en hymin' — it WILL not BE so in you; it WILL not BE so among you. Here is a verb that factually means the 3rd person singular future tense indicative mood of the verb to be, that is, as written here — will be. Grammatically, this can be either a prediction of what will be, or an indirect way of expressing a command (the so-called imperative future or future imperative, as noted by the AI assistant Llama-4-Maverick, 07.08.2025). In the first case, this is not a commandment of Christ, but His warning and prophecy, while in the second it is nonetheless a way to express a commandment — factually let it be so.

'ἀλλ’ ὃς ἐὰν θέλῃ ἐν ὑμῖν μέγας γενέσθαι' — 'all hos ean thele en hymin megas genesthai' — but whoever desires (intends, loves, has pleasure and joy) among you to become (be made) great (greater). I present various meanings of the verbs, as here they complement one another well.

'ἔσται ὑμῶν διάκονος' — 'estai hymon diakonos' — WILL BE your servant (one who executes someone's orders, tasks). An analogous future tense form to the previous one above. Likewise it can mean both a forecasting of the future of such a person and an imperative future or future imperative with the meaning let them be, that is, a commandment rather than a prophecy.

'καὶ ὃς ἂν θέλῃ ἐν ὑμῖν εἶναι πρῶτος' — 'kai hos an thele en hymin einai protos' — and (if) whoever among you desires (intends, loves, has pleasure and joy) to be first. Primacy here conveys the highest degree, the pinnacle of achievement, that is, the maximum intensification of the striving for greatness, superiority.

'ἔσται ὑμῶν δοῦλος' — 'estai hymon doulos' — WILL BE your slave (devoted to another even to the state of acting contrary to one's own desires and interests). The same future tense form, which can both signify a prophecy and an imperative future or future imperative and then convey a commandment.

'ὥσπερ ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου οὐκ ἦλθεν διακονηθῆναι' — 'hosper ho hyios tou anthropou ouk elthen diakonethenai' — just as the Son of Man came not to be served. It is about how the Son of God, having all authority, nonetheless does not seek His own advantage, but acts sacrificially for our salvation, seeking our salvation, and not servants for Himself. God did not create and save people so that people would fulfill God's will, but this fulfillment of God's will leads people to salvation, to eternal life in the blessedness and joy of love.

'ἀλλὰ διακονῆσαι καὶ δοῦναι τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν' — 'alla diakonesai kai dounai ten psykhen autou lytron anti pollon' — but to serve and to give His soul (that is, His life) as a ransom (as explained, the word means the ransom of a slave unto freedom) for (that is, instead of) many. Christ suffered instead of people, that is, for people.

Now Mark IX, 35-37.

'εἴ τις θέλει πρῶτος εἶναι' — 'ei tis thelei protos einai' — if anyone desires to be first.

'ἔσται πάντων ἔσχατος καὶ πάντων διάκονος' — 'estai panton eskhatos kai panton diakonos' — WILL BE last of all and servant of all. Analogous to the place in Matthew.

'καὶ λαβὼν παιδίον ἔστησεν αὐτὸ ἐν μέσῳ αὐτῶν, καὶ ἐναγκαλισάμενος αὐτὸ εἶπεν αὐτοῖς' — 'kai labon paidion estesen auto en meso auton kai enankalisamenos auto eipen autois' — and having taken a little child, He set it in the midst of them, and having folded it in His arms, He said to them.

'ὃς ἂν ἓν τῶν παιδίων τούτων δέξηται ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματί μου, ἐμὲ δέχεται· καὶ ὃς ἂν ἐμὲ δέχηται, οὐκ ἐμὲ δέχεται ἀλλὰ τὸν ἀποστείλαντά με' — 'hos an hen ton paidion touton dexetai epi to onomati mou, eme dekhetai kai hos an eme dekhetai ouk eme dekhetai alla ton aposteilanta me' — (if) whoever receives one such little child in My name receives Me, and whoever receives Me receives not Me, but Him who sent Me (that is, the Father).

Now in Luke XXII, 25-27.

'οἱ βασιλεῖς τῶν ἐθνῶν κυριεύουσιν αὐτῶν, καὶ οἱ ἐξουσιάζοντες αὐτῶν εὐεργέται καλοῦνται' — 'hoi basileis ton ethnon kyrieuousin auton kai hoi exousiazontes auton euergetai kalountai' — the kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them, and those exercising authority over them are called benefactors. That which pertains to a purely secular understanding of authority and greatness.

'ὑμεῖς δὲ οὐχ οὕτως' — 'hymeis de oukh houtos' — but you will / shall not be so.

'ἀλλ’ ὁ μείζων ἐν ὑμῖν γινέσθω ὡς ὁ νεώτερος' — 'all ho meizon en hymin ginestho hos ho neoteros' — but LET the greater among you BECOME like the younger. Here is precisely the imperative mood — a commandment. But it is softened by the comparison 'like'. That is, the Lord does not call to cast off all signs of authority or high position, but to act as if not having such.

'καὶ ὁ ἡγούμενος ὡς ὁ διακονῶν' — 'kai ho hegoumenos hos ho diakonon' — and the leader (ruler, guide, primate, conductor, leader, chief, one who is reckoned with and whose opinion matters) — like the servant. Analogously — not to get rid of leadership, but to act like a minister.

'τίς γὰρ μείζων, ὁ ἀνακείμενος ἢ ὁ διακονῶν; οὐχὶ ὁ ἀνακείμενος; ἐγὼ δὲ ἐν μέσῳ ὑμῶν εἰμι ὡς ὁ διακονῶν' — 'tis gar meizon, ho anakeimenos e ho diakonon? oukhi ho anakeimenos? ego de en meso hymon eimi hos ho diakonon' — for who is greater — he that reclines at table, or he that serves? is not he that reclines? But I am in the midst of you as he that serves. Analogously — Christ did not lose His authority, but instead of seeking its strengthening, seeks good for the disciples, serves them and others.

In general, the question whether it is simply future tense and prophecy, or imperative future and commandment remains open. But general logic and context point to how to understand it overall.

There are three images, three kinds of attitude to superiority, primacy, greatness, and the like.

1. Entirely secular, where superiority and dominion are the goal — said regarding the Gentiles.

2. Whoever has nothing good that he has not received from God, and who can seek to be better, first, higher — this is the second group (factually, all of us).

3. Finally Christ Himself, the little child, the Father, the apostles — the third group. Whereby it is best seen on the example of Christ Himself — the faithful can seek superiority, not having it in themselves, not being themselves the source of their glory or advantages, achievements, but having received gifts from the Generous God, and Christ Himself — Who has everything by nature as God, but instead of seeking authority (which He has and can demand corresponding attitude from everyone), instead seeks not authority or superiority, but us ourselves. Mentioned also here is the famous moral imperative of Kant.

That is, the key to understanding these words in all three cases is not whether it is the imperative mood or prophecy, but these three groups, three attitudes. And among them the example of Christ Himself.

If a young man and woman are invited to a wedding, then uniqueness and humility must be united. And here I see… our Lord Jesus Christ. He, being God, Almighty, Pre-eternal, All-Blessed, took on flesh, became human for our sake, even went to the cross, died, and descended into hell to save us. And every person is called to become like Christ in various instances. Particularly when invited to a wedding alongside a beloved. This cannot be achieved through glory, honour, or by displaying one's merits (real or imagined), but I believe it can only be achieved through love. It is love that must drive us so that we may be like God in this, like Christ. And it is precisely in love that one can, in the very best sense, truly become and be the only one. Because any other method of becoming 'the only one' implies some form of disqualification of others, whereas love signifies the actualisation of uniqueness and the very act of becoming 'the only one'.
