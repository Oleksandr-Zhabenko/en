Liturgy:
(Ephesians IV, 14-19)
Ephesians IV, 14 — 'ἐν τῇ κυβείᾳ' — 'en te kybeia' — by the sleight / cunning of men; in tricks, craftiness. It speaks of the inventiveness of certain people who attempt by it to gain followers contrary to the Word of God. 'ἐν πανουργίᾳ' - 'en panourgia' — in cunning craftiness; with deceitful craftiness. Both translation options are possible.

Ephesians IV, 15, 16 — 'ἐν ἀγάπῃ' — 'en agape' — in love; by love. Both translation options are also possible. 'εἰς αὐτόν' - 'eis auton' — into Him. Here the preposition 'eis' emphasizes movement and gradual development, growth.

Ephesians IV, 16 — 'ἐξ οὗ' — 'ex hou' — from Whom. Indicates the Source of origin and the beginning of movement. 'συναρμολογούμενον καὶ συμβιβαζόμενον' - 'synarmologoumenon kai symbibazomenon' — fitly joined together and compacted; assembled from separate parts and united. 'ἐν μέτρῳ' - 'en metro' — in measure. Set expression. 'εἰς οἰκοδομήν' - 'eis oikodomen' — unto the edifying; for building up. The preposition 'eis' here indicates the goal of the action.

Ephesians IV, 17 — 'ἐν κυρίῳ' — 'en kyrio' — in the Lord. 'ἐν ματαιότητι' - 'en mataioteti' — in the vanity.

Ephesians IV, 18 — 'ἐν αὐτοῖς' — 'en autois' — in them. Where.

Ephesians IV, 19 — 'εἰς ἐργασίαν' — 'eis ergasian' — unto lasciviousness / uncleanness; into wantonness. Indicates the direction of falling, decay. 'ἐν πλεονεξίᾳ' - 'en pleonexia' — with greediness / covetousness. In what.

In the reading, the contrast between the life and development of the Church of Christ and the Gentile way of life is again visible. True Gentiles of that time were indeed alienated (far) from the life of God (in God), for they did not know the One True God and were not raised in an environment where at least divine values were widespread; therefore they lived according to their own notions of good and evil. Good Gentiles resembled Christians to a significant, recognizable degree, and then often became such; whereas all other Gentiles lived to the extent of their own efforts. Therefore, their consciousness was occupied by passions alongside good impulses, and their life was no better than their consciousness. Not knowing God, they often gave themselves over to their own desires, passions, lusts, and ultimately sins.
The Apostle Paul writes about that state. Similar descriptions of Gentilism and its adherents in those times are also found in other Epistles of Paul, as well as in the Catholic Epistles of the Apostles Peter and Jude. As I wrote earlier, such a state for the Gentile way of life persists today as well, though predominantly in the form of a tendency, inclination, temptation, or possibility.
Regarding the Church and the life of the faithful in God, here we see development and forward movement. The faithful have commandments, models, and ideals that allow them at least to see when they act or think well, and when they do not. They have a goal and can always draw nearer to it. This goal, these ideals are truly attainable, and the movement towards them is fruitful and productive, headed by Jesus Christ Himself, the Head of the Church.

Paul says that growth occurs in a manner where the faithful are joined together by that which every joint supplieth. That is, the unity of the faithful in the Church in terms of the means for achieving it is limited by nothing. Everything necessary for the unity of the faithful is included, added into the development of the Church and the growth of the faithful in Christ. The situation may look ideal, but over the nearly two-thousand-year history of the New Testament Church, we see many examples of precisely such an organic inclusion of what is necessary for unity and growth into the life of the Church. Paul writes that such things occur according to God's love, the love of the faithful, and mutual edification.
