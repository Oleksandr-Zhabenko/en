Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians XIII, 3-13)
2 Corinthians XIII, 3 — 'ἐν ἐμοί' — 'en emoi' — in me. In whom. That is, the Corinthians tried to know whether the apostle acts according to the will, doctrine, love, and truth of Christ, that is, whether Paul is indeed His representative in all this. Several important points need to be understood here. First, they believed in the reality of Paul's apostleship itself, particularly among them. Second, they believed in Christ Himself. The question only arose whether the decisions Paul himself made were such as Christ wanted to see them. The apostle previously showed examples of the action of the Holy Spirit and Christ in him, so he reminds the faithful to remember that they themselves belong to Christ. The task of the apostle is not for them always to seek his decision when needed, but for them to learn to act according to Christ. 'εἰς ὑμᾶς' - 'eis hymas' — to you-ward; toward you. That is, the Corinthians already have experience and testimony of life in Christ, which encourage them to move and grow further. 'ἐν ὑμῖν' - 'en hymin' — in you; among you. Where.

2 Corinthians XIII, 4 — 'ἐσταυρώθη ἐξ ἀσθενείας, ἀλλὰ ζῇ ἐκ δυνάμεως θεοῦ' — 'estaurothe ex astheneias alla ze ek dynameos theou' — crucified through weakness, yet He liveth by the power of God. The preposition 'ek' (also in the form 'ex') indicates an important cause here — Christ could be crucified as a human being, but He is also alive as God Almighty. Factually, the human weakness voluntarily assumed by the Son of God is the cause of Christ's crucifixion in the proper sense — both as that which made the crucifixion itself possible, and as that which became the prompt to save people through Suffering and Death on the cross, as well as the glorious Resurrection. 'ἐν αὐτῷ' - 'en auto' — in Him. Here are polysemous words — weak also in experiencing suffering for Christ's sake, and in having their human weaknesses. 'ἐκ δυνάμεως θεοῦ εἰς ὑμᾶς' - 'ek dynameos theou eis hymas' — from (by) the power of God toward you. It refers here to the grace of the Holy Spirit granted to the faithful. 2 Corinthians XIII, 9 — 'κατάρτισιν' — 'katartisin' — instruction, perfection, restoration.

2 Corinthians XIII, 10 — 'εἰς οἰκοδομὴν καὶ οὐκ εἰς καθαίρεσιν' — 'eis oikodomen kai ouk eis kathairesin' — to edification, and not to destruction. The preposition 'eis' indicates the purpose here.

2 Corinthians XIII, 12 — 'ἐν ἁγίῳ φιλήματι' — 'en hagio philemati' — with a holy kiss; in a holy kiss. I wrote earlier in more detail; see the links:

Verse 8 means for the truth, for the sake of the truth. The power of the apostles is in the truth itself, so this power is both for it and for its sake, but in no way against it. Paul in verse 9 writes about judgment in order to perform the work of judgment in time. Also here is testimony that the apostle's judgment is aimed at edification and resolution, not at destruction.

(Galatians I, 1-10, 20-II, 5)
Galatians I, 1 — 'ἀπ’ ἀνθρώπων' — 'ap anthropon' — from men. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that Paul is separated from men, but since a negation stands here, it is also implied that Paul received apostleship not through subjective choice and appointment to an office, but through ministry and calling by God Himself. 'ἐκ νεκρῶν' - 'ek nekron' — from the dead. Here the transition from death to life is emphasized.

Galatians I, 3 — 'ἀπὸ θεοῦ πατρὸς καὶ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ' — 'apo theou patros kai kyriou hemon Iesou CHristou' — from God the Father, and from our Lord Jesus Christ. The preposition 'apo' indicates that grace and peace are gifts of God the Father and Jesus Christ to people. Since the focus of attention here is precisely the bestowing, and not God as Such, the Holy Spirit is not named directly.

Galatians I, 4 — 'ἐκ τοῦ αἰῶνος τοῦ ἐνεστῶτος πονηροῦ' — 'ek tou aionos tou enestotos ponerou' — from this present evil world. The preposition 'ek' indicates here departure from this age and transition to God's life, life in God, emphasizing God's salvation.

Galatians I, 5 — 'εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων' — 'eis tous aionas ton aionon' — for ever and ever. Set expression.

Galatians I, 6 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ καλέσαντος' — 'apo tou kalesantos' — from Him that called. Indicates departure, people's leaving God. 'ἐν χάριτι Χριστοῦ εἰς ἕτερον εὐαγγέλιον' - 'en khariti CHristou eis eteron euangelion' — in the grace of Christ unto another gospel; by the grace of Christ for another gospel. Here it is implied that a person called to faith in Christ, in responding, becomes a participant in God's life in grace, through union with grace, and therefore with God. And departure occurs contrary to the action of grace through human disobedience, temptation, and sin. See also about translation nuances at the link:
https://share.gemini.google/901c8daff8ce

Galatians I, 8, 9 — 'ἐξ οὐρανοῦ' — 'ex ouranou' — from heaven. Indicates origin or location. Either preaches from heaven, or came from heaven — here, generally speaking, both translations are possible. 'παρ’ ὅ' - 'par ho' — than that which; in comparison with that. The preposition 'para' in its form before a following vowel here with the accusative means something else, not that which can be known upon proper comparison.

Galatians I, 21 — 'εἰς τὰ κλίματα' — 'eis ta klimata' — into the regions. Where to.

Galatians I, 22 — 'ἐν Χριστῷ' — 'en CHristo' — in Christ. In Whom.

Galatians I, 24 — 'ἐν ἐμοί' — 'en emoi' — in me. That is, in connection with my life and God's action in it.

Galatians II, 1 — 'εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα' — 'eis Hierosolyma' — to Jerusalem. Where to.

Galatians II, 2 — 'ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν' — 'en tois ethnesin' — among the Gentiles. 'εἰς κενόν' - 'eis kenon' — in vain. Set expression.

Galatians II, 4 — 'ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' — 'en CHristo Iesou' — in Christ Jesus. In Whom.

The beginning of the weekday readings from the Epistle of Apostle Paul to the Galatians.

The Galatians (Greek: Γαλάται, Latin: Galatae) were a confederation of Celtic tribes who invaded Greece and Asia Minor in 279–277 BC. The Galatians settled in the central part of Asia Minor — Galatia. (Source:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galatians_(people)
)

Written on the occasion of the spread among the Galatians of those who taught that Christians converted from among the Gentiles also needed to be circumcised and observe the whole ceremonial Law of Moses (that is, in effect to be Jews, proselytes, and Christians). Factually, such a teaching made Jewish culture a component of the Christian religion, and turned all Gentile Christians into proselytes. In a spiritual sense, this was dangerous because attention was shifted from salvation in Christ to the rites of the Old Testament, which were not saving. To preach another gospel than that we have preached unto you — there is Apostolic Tradition, it is about this.

Galatians I, 1 — Paul means that he received the gift and calling of apostleship directly from Jesus Christ, as did other senior apostles (of the 12). Regarding the 70 apostles, the Gospel mentions the election by Jesus of 70 (72) apostles, but from Tradition it is known that some of those included among the 70 holy apostles were appointed to this by senior apostles, particularly Peter and Paul, and were often their helpers and companions.

Paul further speaks of salvation in Christ (compare: Romans XII, 2), as well as of what we now call Apostolic Tradition (what we received from the holy apostles, what they handed down to us).

(Mark V, 1-20)
Mark V, 1 — 'εἰς τὸ πέραν τῆς θαλάσσης εἰς τὴν χώραν τῶν Γερασηνῶν' — 'eis to peran tes thalasses eis ten khoran ton Gerasenon' — over unto the other side of the sea, into the country of the Gerasenes (Gadarenes). Where to.

Mark V, 2 — 'ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου' — 'ek tou ploiou' — out of the ship. The preposition 'ek' here indicates from where they came. 'ἐκ τῶν μνημείων' - 'ek ton mnemeion' — out of the tombs. 'ἄνθρωπος ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ' - 'anthropos en pneumati akatharto' — a man in (with) an unclean spirit. Here participation in the life of the unclean spirit is implied, but a mutual participation. Mark V, 3, 5 — 'ἐν τοῖς μνήμασιν' — 'en tois mnemasin' — in (among) the tombs. Where.

Mark V, 4 — 'διεσπάσθαι ὑπ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'diespasthai hyp autou' — had been plucked asunder by him. The preposition 'hypo' in its form before a following vowel here with the genitive indicates the passive voice of the participle had been plucked asunder.

Mark V, 5 — 'ἐν τοῖς ὄρεσιν' — 'en tois oresin' — in the mountains. Where.

Mark V, 6 — 'ἀπὸ μακρόθεν' — 'apo makrothen' — afar off; from a distance. Set expression.

Mark V, 8 — 'ἐκ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου' — 'ek tou anthropou' — out of the man. The preposition 'ek' is used here; it indicates from where the departure is to occur.

Mark V, 12, 13 — 'εἰς τοὺς χοίρους' — 'eis tous khoirous' — into the swine. 'εἰς αὐτούς' - 'eis autous' — into them. In both cases — where to.

Mark V, 13 — 'εἰς τὴν θάλασσαν' — 'eis ten thalassan' — into the sea. Where to. 'ἐν τῇ θαλάσσῃ' - 'en te thalasse' — in the sea. Where.

Mark V, 14 — 'εἰς τὴν πόλιν καὶ εἰς τοὺς ἀγρούς' — 'eis ten polin kai eis tous agrous' - in the city, and in the country. That is, in that whole locality. The preposition 'eis' indicates here the spread of rumors.

Mark V, 17 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ὁρίων αὐτῶν' — 'apo ton orion auton' — out of their coasts. The preposition 'apo' indicates that they asked Jesus to go away, to completely leave their coast.

Mark V, 18 — 'εἰς τὸ πλοῖον' — 'eis to ploion' — into the ship. Where to.

Mark V, 19 — 'εἰς τὸν οἶκόν' — 'eis ton oikon' — to thine house. Where to.

Mark V, 20 — 'ἐν τῇ Δεκαπόλει' — 'en te Dekapolei' — in Decapolis (a political union of cities in that country). Where.
