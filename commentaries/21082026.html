Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians VII, 10-16)
2 Corinthians VII, 10 — 'εἰς σωτηρίαν' — 'eis soterian' — unto salvation; for salvation; so as to be saved. The last translation is clearer and more transparent. The preposition 'eis' indicates the purpose of the action here.

2 Corinthians VII, 11, 16 — 'ἐν παντὶ' — 'en panti' — in everything; in all things. Here is a passage that needs to be looked at more deeply. Paul rebuked the Corinthians regarding the sins of one of them and their incorrect reaction, and here writes what the Corinthians did in response to the rebuke. It must be said immediately that the apostle Paul's words cannot be understood in general for any rebuke. Many cases are well known where it was precisely the guilty people who manifested exactly what Paul here calls proof of purity in the matter. That is, in general, all this is not proof of innocence in something. But why does the apostle write so here? The logic is indeed deeper in this case. He names first of all that which is connected with the volitional sphere of a person. That is, he notices that the rebuke caused a strong volitional reaction. The apostle was concerned that an incorrect attitude toward sin would lead to a demotivation of life in Christ, to a cooling of love, about which Christ warns in the Gospels. And the volitional reaction of the Corinthians showed their motivation, their sincere deep desire to continue living piously and devoutly in Christ, and of those who sinned — to repent. Thus, that sin did not decrease the motivation of the Corinthians, that is, it did not defile their will. Therefore Paul speaks of purity. An important passage, as it also shows how true repentance manifests itself in many cases.

2 Corinthians VII, 12 — here the apostle shows that it is not about other people, but about what concerns each reader or listener of the Epistle personally in relationships with God and other faithful. It also shows that it is valuable to realize the significance of certain deeds, particularly for encouragement and reassurance in further labor. Also another important statement: Paul did not try to become a party to the conflict; he tries to preserve impartiality, unbias, and critical thinking.

2 Corinthians VII, 13 — 'ἀπὸ πάντων ὑμῶν' — 'apo panton hymon' — from you all. The preposition 'apo' indicates here the receiving of relief and comfort from the Corinthian addressees by Titus as a certain gift from them.

2 Corinthians VII, 14 — 'ἐν ἀληθείᾳ' — 'en aletheia' — in truth; by truth. How. Rather the former, as it is more logical and grammatically correct.

2 Corinthians VII, 15 — 'εἰς ὑμās' — 'eis hymas' — [in movement; when recalling] toward you. It implies movements of the heart, that is, a figurative expression about the gradual drawing closer of Titus and the Corinthians.

2 Corinthians VII, 16 — 'θαρρῶ ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'tharro en hemin' — bold in you; zealous in you. It refers to trust and reliability, so in meaning the translation will be to rely on you; to have confidence in you.

Paul praises Titus and the Corinthians for the comforts manifested and good behavior.

(Mark II, 18-22)
Mark II, 19 — 'ἐν ᾧ' — 'en ho' — in them. The preposition in emphasizes the value of the bridegroom as a priceless treasure.

Mark II, 20 — 'ἀπ’ αὐτῶν' — 'ap auton' — from them. The preposition 'ap' (form of 'apo' before a following vowel) emphasizes that the bridegroom will be taken away completely, entirely. 'ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ' - 'en ekeine te hemera' — in those days. Set expression.

Mark II, 21 — 'ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'ap autou' — from it. The new tears away from the old, that is, the Lord does not exalt the importance of the old or the new, but their integrity. In the case of the apostles, this is obvious.

Mark II, 22 — 'εἰς ἀσκοὺς παλαιούς' — 'eis askous palaious' — into old wineskins. Where to.

Three short parables (images for reflection and cognition) regarding fasting and the state of Christ's disciples when He is with them. The meaning of the first comparison (with the wedding guests) is fairly transparent. Regarding the patch and the wineskins — the Lord means that between the old attitude toward piety (ceremonial) and the Gospel there are noticeable differences, so that it is impossible to contain both well. And since the disciples are called to renewal in Christ, in the New Covenant, Christ in no way demands from them what might even harm them in this primary matter. A good example also for gradual learning of the new life together with Christ — one needs to prepare for renewal, not a reformatting of the old. For true virtue is not a reformatted old way of life that was not virtuous. Therefore Christ in this way protects the disciples from the rebukes of the Pharisees.
