Liturgy:
(1 Timothy I, 1-7)
1 Timothy I, 2 — 'τέκνῳ ἐν πίστει' — 'tekno en pistei' — child in the faith; child according to the faith. By sense, the latter option applies: that is, Paul led Timothy to a deeper faith, becoming a spiritual father to him. 'χάρις, ἔλεος, εἰρήνη ἀπὸ θεοῦ πατρὸς καὶ Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν' - 'kharis, eleos, eirene apo theou patros kai CHristou Iesou tou kyriou hemon' — grace, mercy, and peace, from God our Father and Jesus Christ our Lord. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that grace, mercy, and peace are God's gifts to humanity.

1 Timothy I, 3 — 'ἐν Ἐφέσῳ' — 'en Epheso' — at Ephesus / in Ephesus. Where. 'εἰς Μακεδονίαν' - 'eis Makedonian' — into Macedonia. Where to.

1 Timothy I, 4 — 'ἐν πίστει' — 'en pistei' — in faith; by faith. Both translation options are possible: the first indicates that faith is a good condition for growth in God, whilst the second shows that it also edifies (builds up).

1 Timothy I, 5 — 'ἀγάπη ἐκ καθαρᾶς καρδίας καὶ συνειδήσεως ἀγαθῆς καὶ πίστεως ἀνυποκρίτου' — 'agape ek katharas kardias kai syneideseos agathes kai pisteos anypokritou' — charity [love] out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned. The preposition 'ek' indicates here that the love in question originates from purity of heart, from purification, a good conscience, and deep faith. See also in greater detail at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/22112026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/30112025.html

1 Timothy I, 6 — 'ἐξετράπησαν εἰς ματαιολογίαν' — 'exetrapesan eis mataiologian' — have turned aside unto vain jangling / strayed off the road into empty talk. The preposition 'eis' indicates the direction into which they turned aside; that is, this was not their goal, yet they strayed off the road into it. A different word is used here than the one used regarding Christ's teaching on prayer in the Sermon on the Mount in Matthew VI. It indicates that this is not merely a minor shortcoming, but a defining characteristic of those people's lives.

The beginning of the First Epistle of the Apostle Paul to Timothy — an apostle of the Seventy, one of Paul's closest co-workers and his disciple. The epistle is regarded as pastoral, meaning it concerns the ministry of Timothy (and hence not his alone) as a pastor of the Church, in particular as an example for bishops.
The commandment refers to God's commandments, the brief summary of which is set forth immediately afterward. As is known, purity of heart is not merely sincerity (though it includes it as a component), but purification from sins and sinful passions — that very purity mentioned in the sixth Beatitude. A good conscience signifies constant awareness of good and evil, as well as doing and thinking good rather than evil. And unfeigned faith means faith not for outward show (in those times faith was widespread as part of social life, so many people might seek the appearance of faith, yet not always faith itself), but true faith, divine faith.
The Apostle Paul entrusted Timothy with seeing to the cessation of futile disputes and speculations in Ephesus, where individuals had previously appeared attempting to captivate and attract people's attention through empty intellectual constructs. Paul testifies that those speculations are futile.

(Luke XIV, 12-15)
Luke XIV, 14 — 'ἐν τῇ ἀναστάσει' — 'en te anastasei' — at the resurrection / in the resurrection; by the resurrection. In general, both translation options are possible, although the second asserts that the resurrection itself repays something to people. The first option is clearer: after the resurrection there will be the judgment and the Kingdom of God.

Luke XIV, 15 — 'ἐν τῇ βασιλείᾳ τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'en te basileia tou theou' — in the kingdom of God. Where and when.

A unique tradition of Luke.
The same logic appears in the work at the link below; it is characteristic of Lukan reasoning overall:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/DialogueOnWordsChristFear.html
The Lord calls for disinterestedness (selflessness) in doing good.
If a person does good hoping for repayment from other people, that expectation makes them a part and participant in purely human relationships, in which there is also room for sins and passions — for everything that exists outside the Gospel as well. And even if the person is not tempted by this directly, the expectation itself already serves for them either as a cause for disappointment, or as a cause for excessive reliance on people, or as a cause for uncertainty and anxiety. In short, even if the person does not sin outright, they are as it were shaken by this uncertainty regarding people.
Whereas a person who does good for the Lord's sake must be certain that they will receive good from God. Indeed, as is stated elsewhere, such a person shall in no wise lose their reward. See at the links:
