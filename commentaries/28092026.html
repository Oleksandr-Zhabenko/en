Liturgy:
(Ephesians IV, 25-32)
Ephesians IV, 29 — 'ἐκ τοῦ στόματος ὑμῶν' — 'ek tou stomatos hymon' — out of your mouth. From where.

Ephesians IV, 30 — 'καὶ μὴ λυπεῖτε τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'kai me lypeite to pneuma to hagion tou theou' — and grieve not the Holy Spirit of God. The verb used here can be translated as grieve not; do not afflict with sorrow, cause no sorrow or grief, do not throw into distress (do not cause unpleasantness); offend not; do not make restless by causing pangs of conscience. The last option does not fit regarding the Holy Spirit, but all the preceding ones pertain exclusively to a living Being. One can grieve, cause sorrow, cause unpleasantness, or offend only someone. Thus, it is obvious that this passage is one of the clearest testimonies to the Personhood of the Holy Spirit. Romans VIII, 27 — 'κατὰ θεὸν' — 'kata theon' - according to God. God — here is the Father, and the Holy Spirit operates in the world according to His common will with the Father and the Son. He resembles the image of caring hands with which someone embraces and holds close, taking care of their child. To a certain extent, the Holy Spirit represents the faithful, in particular the saints, before the Father. And if the request of a man is the request of a man, a creation, then the request of the Holy Spirit is the request of God with all the consequences. Also here it is well visible that the Holy Spirit is a Personality, because He intercedes. Intercede, however, can only a conscious person. Consequently, He is a Personality, a Hypostasis of the Divine nature. Therefore often in prayer the faithful say: Intercede us, O God, by Thy grace!. In the verse Romans VIII, 27 is used the mind [thought] of the Spirit (that which He thinks). Here it is clearly visible that the Holy Spirit also thinks, is intelligent [rational]. Here is an important moment for theology: the Father knows the mind of the Spirit, and in another place the apostle Paul says that the Spirit knows all things, even the deep things of God (consequently, the thoughts of the Father). Thinking is a manifestation of the nature, and the latter is common in the Trinity, but the Hypostases think, realizing the powers and freedom of the nature. And it is important that each Hypostasis of the Trinity knows what the Other thinks. This also is a testimony of the perichoresis of the Hypostases of the Trinity, and also of the fact that God's knowledge does not have a certain finite volume of knowledge, meaning it actually is boundless. For in the case of a finite volume of knowledge of the Hypostases, each would have to know all that He knows Himself, but also all that the Two Others know, and at the same time know that which He knows Himself. This tripling is impossible for a finite volume of knowledge, therefore God's knowledge has an infinite volume, is boundless. And also it is important to emphasize that the possession by each Hypostasis of the fullness of the Divine nature in no way becomes an obstacle for Them Each to perfectly know the thoughts and will of One Another. Full and perfect possession of the Divine nature does not prevent the Hypostases from fully and perfectly knowing One Another. These are not Three private Hypostases, but these are Three Loving and Open for Communion Hypostases. See regarding that at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/18062026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/26062025.html
'ἐν ᾧ' — 'en ho' — whereby; in Whom. Both translation options are possible. 'εἰς ἡμέραν' - 'eis hemeran' — unto the day; for the day. In sense, the second or third options are better, since it refers to the future day of redemption, the eternal one, which is to come in the Kingdom of God.

Ephesians IV, 31 — 'ἀφ’ ὑμῶν' — 'aph hymon' — from you. That is, let it be taken away completely, entirely. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates that what is removed ought to be taken away altogether, completely, entirely. Put away — here it implies that thus the faithful will be freed from the burden of these and similar sins that weigh them down.

Ephesians IV, 32 — 'εἰς ἀλλήλους' — 'eis allelous' — one to another; for one another. For whom. 'ἐν Χριστῷ' - 'en CHristo' — in Christ; for Christ's sake. Both translation options are possible, although the second is difficult to comprehend; it imperfectly conveys the thought that the Father sent the Son to save the world by Him, and therefore to forgive sins to people particularly as a component of salvation.

The Apostle Paul gives instructions on how Christians ought to live and conduct themselves. I shall focus very briefly on some of the more complex points necessary for understanding.

We are members one of another — since the faithful constitute the Church of Christ, and the latter is the Body of Christ, the faithful are members of the Body of Christ, the Church, and through this are interconnected with one another, needing one another. Whereas untruth, deception, craftiness, and the like greatly harm unity in Christ; therefore it is necessary to be truthful, sincere, authentic, and true.

If a person is angry passionately, unrighteously, then with a certain retention of this state within oneself, the person becomes open to the heightened influence of evil spirits, who can tempt them even further and lead to a great fall. Therefore, also in view of this, one must not hold onto anger, offense, and the like.

The phrase minister grace — implies that the word itself is valuable, and also points, leads, pertains to, and testifies of another thing important for salvation. The word, therefore, is not the source of grace, but grace is the source of such words; it prompts a person and grants them to them. By receiving and accepting well the consequences of grace, a person is capable of accepting grace itself better and faster, whence Paul's words.

Sealed unto the day of redemption — that is, the Holy Spirit is given until the Kingdom of God as a seal and earnest, that is, as a sign of belonging, of citizenship in the Kingdom of God, to establish relationships of trust, but not in all fulness, which will be manifested in the Kingdom of God itself. A seal changes that to which it is affixed, setting a certain image; so too the Holy Spirit now renews the faithful according to the image of Jesus Christ.

And also God through Jesus Christ manifested special love and mercy; therefore we too must be merciful and loving.

(Luke III, 19-22)
The weekday readings from the Gospel according to Luke, my beloved saint, begin.

Luke III, 19 — 'ὑπ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'hyp autou' — by him. Reproof is used with the preposition 'hypo'. The preposition 'hypo' here is with the genitive case and indicates that the reproof was made by John to Herod (passive voice reproved; exposed).

Luke III, 20 — 'ἐν φυλακῇ' — 'en phylake' — in a prison. Where. As I wrote regarding other similar passages, here it indicates that John did not offer overt resistance. It can also be part of an abbreviated phrase shut up in prison that he might sit (be) there.

Luke III, 21 — 'ἐν τῷ βαπτισθῆναι' — 'en to baptisthenai' — when they having been baptized; during baptism. When.

Luke III, 22 — 'ἐξ οὐρανοῦ' — 'ex ouranou' — from heaven. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates here the origin of the Voice. 'ἐν σοί' - 'en soi' — in Thee. In Whom.

The beginning of the public ministry of Christ in adult age, His Baptism by John the Forerunner in the Jordan.

Here it speaks of the Holy Spirit as Living.
