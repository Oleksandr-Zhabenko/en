Galatians V, 1 — 'ἐλευθερίᾳ' — 'eleutheria' — freedom; liberty. 'ἠλευθέρωσεν' - 'eleutherosen' — made us free; set free.

On freedom, there is a fine work in several parts at the link:

Galatians V, 6 — 'ἐν γὰρ Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' — 'en gar CHristo Iesou' — for in Christ Jesus. In Whom.

Galatians V, 8 — 'ἐκ τοῦ καλοῦντος' — 'ek tou kalountos' — of Him that calleth. Here the preposition 'ek' indicates the Source of origin.

Galatians V, 10 — 'εἰς ὑμᾶς ἐν κυρίῳ' — 'eis hymas en kyrio' — confidence in you in (through) the Lord. It speaks of hope in the Lord that the faithful will preserve inner peace.

Paul uses the example of Sarah and Hagar and their children to demonstrate the power of God's promise and the value of freedom.
In Christ we have access to all of God's promises. Therefore, the event involving Isaac and Ishmael has a prototypical significance — the Lord prompted Abraham to leave Isaac, the son of the promise, as the heir, not because it was necessary at that moment, but primarily because it was to be a prototype in Christ regarding the faithful in the New Testament, who were to be sons of the freewoman, and not of the bondwoman. It is precisely in this way that one must understand why the Lord spoke those words. The dispute between Abraham's children could have been settled differently, but for the sake of the prototype and all the faithful in the New Testament, the Lord said to do it in that way. It must be said that He continued to care for Hagar and Ishmael, and the latter became the ancestor of the Arabs.

In chapter V, Paul expands on this thought, and also testifies that there is nothing good in returning to the Old Testament at a time when one must move into the New. There is no sense in becoming Jews when one needs to become Christians. The Galatians faced precisely such a temptation.

If anyone forced Christians to be circumcised and to observe the entire ceremonial Law of Moses, then instead of Christ, instead of grace, they returned to the Old Testament, when there was not yet redemption, when there was no deliverance in Christ from the slavery of sin, but there was preparation and expectation for Him, for the Messiah-Christ. And if anyone, knowing this and realizing this, continues to compel the observance of the whole ceremonial Law of Moses including circumcision, then such a person neglects grace in Christ, of which Paul writes.

(Mark VI, 54-VII, 8)
Mark VI, 54 — 'ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου' — 'ek tou ploiou' — out of the ship. The preposition 'ek' indicates from where they came out.

Mark VI, 55 — 'κακῶς ἔχοντας' — 'kakos ekhontas' — those having something very bad, evil (those that were sick). As I wrote earlier, Mark names severe and heavy diseases in this way. See more at the link:
https://www.facebook.com/Oleksandr.S.Zhabenko/posts/

Mark VI, 56 — 'εἰς κώμας ἢ εἰς πόλεις ἢ εἰς ἀγρούς' — 'eis komas e eis poleis e eis agrous' — into villages, or cities, or country. Where to. 'ἐν ταῖς ἀγοραῖς' - 'en tais agorais' — in the market-places / gathering places for assemblies and trade. Where.

Mark VII, 1 — 'ἀπὸ Ἱεροσολύμων' — 'apo Hierosolymon' — from Jerusalem. The preposition 'apo' indicates that they had completely left Jerusalem.

Mark VII, 4 — 'ἀπὸ ἀγορᾶς' — 'apo agoras' — from the market-place / gathering place for assembly and trade. The preposition 'apo' indicates that they leave those places.

Mark VII, 6 — 'ἀπ’ ἐμοῦ' — 'ap emou' — far from Me. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that those people have moved far away and lost communion with God.

A quotation from Isaiah XXIX, 13; see also about it at the link:

Regarding the parallel passage in Matthew (Matthew XIV, 35 — XV, 11), it is written at the link:

Mark adds explanations of Jewish traditions for Romans, as he wrote for Gentile Christian Romans who might not know the details of Jewish life.

It must be said that Christ does not speak against hygiene or washing dishes (some of these rules are in the Law, which the Lord fulfilled; also He did not oppose greetings and foot-washing as such, for He Himself took part in them and even set an example by washing the disciples' feet at the Last Supper). But He opposes attributing to rules of hygiene and human agreements the weight of God's ordinances and commandments, equating them in importance and meaning, which the Pharisees did.

(Galatians V, 11-21)
Galatians V, 13 — 'εἰς ἀφορμήν' — 'eis aphormen' — for an occasion. That is, freedom ought not to serve as an occasion for the flesh. Freedom, therefore, serves as a beginning for the spiritual, for everything to which the spirit prompts a person. Therefore, spirit and freedom in these words of the Apostle Paul are inextricably linked. In the works of the Fathers, freedom is often connected with reason, with reflection, but here freedom is something more — it is freedom according to God's design — freedom for the spiritual. Nor does this mean that the spiritual cannot include the bodily — for instance, in another place Paul says that even what you do bodily is spiritual, because it is done in Christ.

Galatians V, 14 — 'ἐν ἑνὶ λόγῳ' — 'en heni logo' — in one word. 'ἐν τῷ' - 'en to' — in this. Set expression.

Galatians V, 15 — 'ὑπ’ ἀλλήλων' — 'hyp allelon' — one of another. The preposition 'hypo' in its form before a following vowel here with the genitive case emphasizes the possibility of people using other people and warns against it.

Galatians V, 18 — 'ὑπὸ νόμον' — 'hypo nomon' — under the Law. Here the preposition 'hypo' is with the accusative case. As I wrote at the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/10092026.html
it indicates here that the Law is as it were raised above human being, as if placed higher than them. In the Gospel Christ says that the sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath, and that He is Lord of the sabbath (particularly the One Who is awaited on the sabbath, on the day of rest).

(Mark VII, 5-16)
Mark VII, 6 — 'ἀπ’ ἐμοῦ' — 'ap emou' — far from Me. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that those people have moved far away and lost communion with God.

Mark VII, 11 — 'ἐξ ἐμοῦ ὠφεληθῇς' — 'ex emou ophelethes' — mightest be profited by me. The preposition 'ek' indicates that this is something valuable for the owner, something essential.

Mark VII, 12 — 'οὐκέτι ἀφίετε αὐτὸν οὐδὲν ποιῆσαι' — 'ouketi aphiete auton ouden poiesai' — you suffer him no more to do ought. That is, those Pharisees permit and forgive if a child does nothing to help their parents, allegedly for God's sake (in reality, as researchers note, this could be a fictitious gift to God, which could remain in the use of the owner).

Mark VII, 15 — 'εἰς αὐτόν' — 'eis auton' — into them. 'τὰ ἐκ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου' - 'ta ek tou anthropou' — those things which come out of them. The preposition 'ek' paired with the preceding 'eis' here indicates from where those things originate which are mentioned further as the cause of defiling a person.
