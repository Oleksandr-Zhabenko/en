Liturgy:
(1 Thessalonians II, 14-19)
1 Thessalonians II, 14 — 'ἐν τῇ Ἰουδαίᾳ' — 'en te Ioudaia' — in Judaea. Where. 'ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' — 'en CHristo Iesou' — in Jesus Christ. 'ὑπὸ τῶν ἰδίων συμφυλετῶν' - 'hypo ton idion symphyleton' — by your own countrymen. 'ὑπὸ τῶν Ἰουδαίων' - 'hypo ton Ioudaion' — by the Jews. The preposition 'hypo' here takes the genitive case, indicating in both instances the passive voice suffered and pointing to who caused those sufferings and afflictions.

1 Thessalonians II, 16 — 'εἰς τὸ ἀναπληρῶσαι' — 'eis to anaplerosai' — to fill up / for filling up; so as to fill up. Here the preposition 'eis' indicates the goal of the action. 'εἰς τέλος' - 'eis telos' — to the uttermost / unto the (logical) end. In sense — as a logical consequence of the entire conduct of the Jews who opposed God and people.

1 Thessalonians II, 17 — 'ἀφ’ ὑμῶν' — 'aph hymon' — from you. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates that the apostles and disciples are here in different places. 'ἐν πολλῇ ἐπιθυμίᾳ' - 'en polle epithymia' — with great desire / in great longing. That is, under the influence of and as a manifestation of an unanimous, wholehearted, complete, undeniable desire.

1 Thessalonians II, 19 — 'ἐν τῇ αὐτοῦ παρουσίᾳ' — 'en te autou parousia' — at His coming. When.

The Apostle Paul encourages the Thessalonians, demonstrating their solidarity in all things with other Churches, in particular in Jerusalem, where the faithful first experienced persecutions and afflictions.
He speaks also of personal support, as well as the personal relationship of the community with Paul.
He praises the faithful once again.

(Luke XI, 23-26)
Luke XI, 24 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου' — 'apo tou anthropou' — out of the human being / from the human being. The preposition 'apo' emphasizes that the unclean spirit completely departs from the human being. 'εἰς τὸν οἶκόν μου' - 'eis ton oikon mou' — into my house. Where to. That sinful person who leaves themselves without participation in the good, pure, and holy receives the name of a house for the unclean spirit, having become like him in the absence of good.

The parable in the context of the Pharisees' accusations regarding an unclean spirit in Jesus carries the understanding that the swept and garnished house may very well turn out to be the Pharisees themselves, and more generally — those people called by God who consciously reject Christ. Then there is nothing that could hinder even worse unclean spirits from acting upon that environment and upon each such person individually, as the parable figuratively states. At the same time, such a specific understanding is fully generalized to the case of any conscious rejection of Jesus Christ and God as a whole. Then the empty house signifies the absence of power that would not merely stop, but also drive away evil spirits so they do not take possession of the house, that is, the people. In the case of a swept house, only God's restraint and mercy prevent something even worse than what is stated in the parable (demons are in reality far more numerous than seven).

Therefore, the parable serves as a warning for all, as well as a call to sensitivity and alertness, so that the enemy does not secretly dig through the house, as is spoken of in another place.
