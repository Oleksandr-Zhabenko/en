Liturgy:
(Galatians IV, 8-21)
Galatians IV, 11 — 'εἰς ὑμᾶς' — 'eis hymas' — upon you.

Galatians IV, 18 — 'ἐν καλῷ' — 'en kalo' — in a good thing; in that which is good. In what. 'ἐν τῷ' - 'en to' — in that; when.

Galatians IV, 19, 20 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'en hymin' — in you. In whom.

Galatians IV, 21 — 'ὑπὸ νόμον' — 'hypo nomon' — under the Law. Here the preposition 'hypo' is used with the accusative case. As I wrote yesterday, it indicates that the Law is as it were raised above human being, as if placed higher than them. In the Gospel Christ says that the sabbath was made for human being, and not human being for the sabbath, and that He is Lord of the sabbath (particularly the One Who is awaited on the sabbath, on the day of rest).

Paul reminds the Galatians of their state prior to and at the beginning of their conversion, comparing it with that into which they might be tempted to fall then. He also recalls their sincerity in accepting Christ, as well as Paul himself, in whom they trusted completely and to whom they even entrusted themselves.

The mention of Paul's weakness is not clear — perhaps Paul means the same thing as in 2 Corinthians XII, or perhaps some eye ailment (subsequent words about eyes may point to this), but the latter lacks confirmation from known sources.

Paul compares the recognition of the necessity to observe the ceremonial component of the Law of Moses with serving the elements of the world (heavenly elements, rudiments of the world). Serving these elements was widespread among Gentiles in various forms, and the Galatian Christians prior to conversion were Gentiles. It must be understood that the comparison is non-literal (observing the ceremonial component of the Law of Moses is not idolatry, worshipping creation instead of the Creator), but signifies that, like serving the elements, it has a show of wisdom, but lacks saving power. If attention in spiritual life is shifted to this, then a person simply acts unwisely and incorrectly, which can really harm their salvation, and not only their own, but also that of other people.

John VIII, 3, 9 — 'ἐν μέσῳ' — 'en meso' — in the midst. Where.

John VIII, 5 — 'ἐν τῷ νόμῳ' — 'en to nomo' — in the Law (of Moses). Where.

John VIII, 6, 8 — 'κατέγραφεν εἰς τὴν γῆν' — 'kategraphen eis ten gen' — wrote on the ground; wrote into the earth. That is, made writings where — into the ground. Such a verb is often used in the New Testament when referring to the Scriptures of the Old Testament. Here this gesture of Christ is a parallel to the tables of the Law of Moses, which were originally written by the finger of God. Christ shows that He wrote them; He, and not Moses, is the Lawgiver and Judge. There is a well-known interpretation that He was writing the secret sins of the accusers, who, having read them, were ashamed (Christ showed also that He is the Searcher of hearts and knows all). It can also be added that the tables were written twice — the first time by the finger of God on the heavenly stone, and the second — by Moses on earthly stones. The earthly tables were to reflect the heavenly ones. So here too it can be seen that Christ as the God-man as it were reminds of both writings — the heavenly one, which He made as God, and the earthly one, since He became man, similar particularly to Moses.

John VIII, 9 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν πρεσβυτέρων, ὥστε πάντας' — 'apo ton presbyteron oste pantas' — (beginning) at the eldest, even unto the last. The preposition 'apo' signifies here the starting point of reckoning (range).

John VIII, 11 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν' — 'apo tou nyn' — from henceforth; from now on. From when. The preposition 'apo' indicates the starting point of reckoning here.

A famous passage where Christ acts as Lawgiver and God, Judge, Searcher of hearts, and All-knowing Saviour. At the same time He acts also as Human Being. The passage teaches us to fight against sins.
