Liturgy:
(1 Timothy III, 1-13)
1 Timothy III, 4 — 'ἐν ὑποταγῇ' — 'en hypotage' — in subjection / in obedience. In what.

1 Timothy III, 6 — 'εἰς κρίμα' — 'eis krima' — into condemnation / into judgment. Where to.

1 Timothy III, 7 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ἔξωθεν' — 'apo ton exothen' — of them which are outside / from those outside. The preposition 'apo' indicates here either a certain objectivity in the testimony of the Gentiles (since their testimony is less influenced by internal disagreements or doubts), or conversely — insufficient precision (since they do not know everything that ought to be known). 'εἰς ὀνειδισμὸν' - 'eis oneidismon' — into reproach; into gossip / censure. Where to.

1 Timothy III, 9 — 'μυστήριον τῆς πίστεως ἐν καθαρᾷ συνειδήσει' — 'mysterion tes pisteos en kathara syneidesei' — the mystery of the faith in a pure conscience. That is, such a person who knows the faith, holds the faith — in particular the mysteries of the faith — and lives piously and devoutly. In contemporary conditions, this signifies both sound preparation and education, as well as a virtuous life.

1 Timothy III, 13 — 'ἐν πίστει τῇ ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ' — 'en pistei te en CHristo Iesou' — in the faith which is in Christ Jesus; by the faith which is in Christ Jesus. Both translation options are possible: the first indicates that faith in Christ fosters boldness, openness, and confidence, whilst the second shows that it is itself the driving force for this, its strength and inspiration.

A description of the requirements for clergy. In those times everyone could be married, and most were, although this was never obligatory.
Women are mentioned as deaconesses, who also existed in those days. Today this institution has been preserved in the Patriarchate of Alexandria, and there are also views that it ought to be restored more broadly.
The list of requirements is fairly clear; primarily it concerns tested and experienced candidates who also hold a good reputation among the gentiles, since the majority of people surrounding them at that time were gentiles (Timothy, like Paul, laboured predominantly in a gentile environment).
Many of these requirements became part of canon law with certain modifications (in particular, bishops today, and for many centuries now, are exclusively unmarried).

(Luke XVI, 1-9)
Luke XVI, 3 — 'ἐν ἑαυτῷ' — 'en heauto' — within himself / in himself. 'ἀπ’ ἐμοῦ' - 'ap emou' — from me. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates that the stewardship will be taken away completely.

Luke XVI, 4 — 'ἐκ τῆς οἰκονομίας' — 'ek tes oikonomias' — out of the stewardship / from management. The preposition 'ek' indicates from where the steward will be removed. 'εἰς τοὺς οἴκους ἑαυτῶν' - 'eis tous oikous hauton' — into their houses. Where to.

Luke XVI, 8 — 'εἰς τὴν γενεὰν τὴν ἑαυτῶν' — 'eis ten genean ten heauton' — into their generation; into their own kind / generation; for their own kind / generation. All translation options are possible. Meant here is that the children of this world are regarded as more resourceful among their own surroundings. Yet resourcefulness itself, where stewardship is required, is often a sign of disorder, and perhaps even the absence of order and rightness.

Luke XVI, 9 — 'ἐκ τοῦ μαμωνᾶ τῆς ἀδικίας' — 'ek tou mamona tes adikias' — of the mammon of unrighteousness / from the mammon of unrighteousness. The preposition 'ek' indicates from where what has been received ought to lead to friendship. The parable can only be understood properly as a whole; see below. 'εἰς τὰς αἰωνίους σκηνάς' - 'eis tas aionious skenas' — into everlasting habitations / into eternal dwellings. Where to.

Here is read the parable of the steward of unjustness and the beginning of Jesus' explanation of it.
It is a difficult parable to understand.
Yet it can be grasped if one pays attention to Jesus' explanation — the steward, by forgiving a portion of the debts owed by his master's debtors, showed mercy to them. Christ desires that the faithful show mercy toward other sinful people, so that sin may become less.
And the very title steward of unrighteousness signifies that everything good and worthy that a person possesses is not their own by origin, but has its beginning in God. It is precisely God who gives a person particular gifts, and only then does the person receive something genuinely good, possessing something genuinely good. In such a case, every person stands like that steward — managing, yet not what belongs properly to themselves, not what is exclusively their own. Therefore, they manage not their own wealth, but what God gives them. And since this wealth is originally God's, a manifestation of divine generosity, they hold it as it were unrighteously — that is, undeservedly, as a free gift from God. This is a state of becoming, when a person has not yet become who they will be, when the Kingdom of God is not yet manifest and final. Yet when the Kingdom of God comes, after the Second Coming of Christ and the general resurrection of the dead, then a person will cease to be a steward, and will become what they have become through God's mercy and their own freedom.
Why then is stewardship taken away completely? After all, God does not take away freedom, and freedom presupposes governance. It is not freedom that is taken away, rather the state of the estate will change completely. In the age to come, after the resurrection and the transformation of humanity unto the absence of bodily death, all conditions of creation will change. Behold, I make all things new! says Divine Revelation. People will no longer manage anything temporal, but will receive what is eternal: eternal life, eternal love, eternal virtues, and growth in them. Yet if they have not gained even the temporal, possessing the earnest of the eternal now — if they have not cared for its multiplication, good stewardship, fruitfulness, and the like, if instead of good they did evil, were negligent, slothful, and so forth, remaining unrepentant — then they shall not receive their own (the expected blessings), having squandered the earnest.
The inability to beg (to ask, to pray) and to dig (to renew or radically alter one's state by one's own strength) also speaks to this.
