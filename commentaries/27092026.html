Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians VI, 16-VII, 1)
2 Corinthians VI, 16 — 'ἐν αὐτοῖς' — 'en autois' — in them; among them. Where, among whom. That is, God will become a Neighbour, One close to people. The quotation from the Old Testament is composite, drawn from two passages — Leviticus XXVI, 11-12, and Ezekiel XXXVII, 26-27. See also:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/19082026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/08022026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/12022023.html

2 Corinthians VI, 17 — 'ἐκ μέσου αὐτῶν' — 'ek mesou auton' — from among them. The preposition 'ek' indicates here from where the coming out ought to take place. It speaks of the faithful being called to live according to different models than sinful people (primarily — after the example of Christ). One can also compare a similar passage in the Gospel according to Matthew regarding the Kingdom of God and people within it. I quote:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/10072026.html
Matthew XIII, 49 — 'ἐν τῇ συντελείᾳ τοῦ αἰῶνος' — 'en te synteleia tou aionos' — in the end of the world; in the completion of the age. It concerns the Second Coming of Christ in glory, the resurrection of the dead, and the Judgement of God. It is also used earlier in the chapter. 'ἐκ μέσου' - 'ek mesou' - from the midst [out of the midst]. A set expression. An interesting image mathematically — if the wicked are severed from the midst of the righteous, then the set of the righteous must be such that next to a wicked person there must be a righteous one. That is, the set of the righteous in the topological space of the Kingdom of God is dense (mathematically). See more about topological spaces, in particular, at the link:
Dense set. Encyclopedia of Mathematics
http://encyclopediaofmath.org/index.php?title=Dense_set&oldid=42518
This does not mean that there are approximately as many righteous as wicked, but it means that in one's environment there is complete hope to find a righteous person, at least one (possibly more). This gives the world hope, since it does not matter what time or country it is, but these words of Christ are eternal.
End of quote.
2 Corinthians VI, 17 — 'ἀφορίσθητε' — 'aphoristhete' — be you separate; set a boundary that separates. The word is formed from 'apo' and 'horizo'. A parallel to the Old Testament understanding of God's holiness as otherness, set-apartness. The apostle here continues the Gospel parable of the leaven and three measures of meal — the leaven is called to make all the meal into leavened dough; the faithful do not necessarily have to sever relations with unbelievers, but must build them in such a way that they become leavened in the Gospel sense, if that is possible. And if not — then one can expect the second — separation.

2 Corinthians VI, 18 — 'εἰς πατέρα' — 'eis patera' — a Father unto you; as a Father. Will be Whom. 'εἰς υἱοὺς καὶ θυγατέρας' - 'eis hyious kai thygateras' — into sons and daughters; as sons and daughters. In sense, the second translation applies in both cases here; the preposition 'eis' indicates becoming them, entering into that state.

2 Corinthians VII, 1 — 'ἀπὸ παντὸς μολυσμοῦ' — 'apo pantos molysmou' — from all filthiness. The preposition 'apo' indicates complete, total cleansing. 'ἐπιτελοῦντες' - 'epitelountes' — perfecting; bringing to completion, making perfect. 'ἁγιωσύνην' - 'hagiosynen' — holiness, state of holiness, state of sanctification. The Apostle Paul implies that there is holiness among Old Testament righteous ones too, that both they and the prophets are holy, but it is not complete, for it is completed only in Jesus Christ, the Lord and Saviour. Whereas New Testament people are called to perfection, completeness, full maturity, and perfection in Christ. The Lord has accomplished and granted everything necessary for this. God's promises — for God promises. 'ἐν φόβῳ θεοῦ' - 'en phobo theou' — in the fear of God; by the fear of God. Both translation options are possible; the first indicates that the living sanctuary is as it were surrounded by the fear of God, which is a parallel to the multi-level structure of the temple, whereas the second translation option indicates that the fear of God leads to the living sanctuary within the person, to the sanctification of the person by grace. See more on the fear of God at the link:
https://www.facebook.com/groups/1539152859716654/?multi_permalinks=3554517061513547

The quotation from the Old Testament is composite; similar words are found in many places, particularly in the Law, in the prophets, and in the historical books of the Old Testament.
Here it speaks of divinisation and the holiness of people. The theme is very profound, but I will focus on only two important points.
1. I will be their God, and they shall be My people. The faithful become partakers with Jesus Christ, the Son of God, which is why God calls them His people, as if placing Himself on the same level with them, thereby immensely endowing and exalting human beings. Yet for the faithful, God is God, which shows how incomparably Higher God is than creation. One could also put it this way: the faithful humble themselves, and God exalts them.
2. I will walk in them — in the Old Testament, ways and paths are a frequent synonym for a way of life, general conduct. Therefore, one can see here that God will manifest Himself through His actions among the faithful, granting them that as grace.

(Matthew XV, 21-28 )
Matthew XV, 21 — 'εἰς τὰ μέρη Τύρου καὶ Σιδῶνος' — 'eis ta mere Tyrou kai Sidonos' — into the coasts / borders of Tyre and Sidon. Where to.

Matthew XV, 22 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ὁρίων ἐκείνων' — 'apo ton horion ekeinon' — out of the same coasts / from those borders. The preposition 'apo' indicates that spiritually she had left the traditions of those lands, perhaps repented, or that on her mother's side she originated from there.

Matthew XV, 24 — 'εἰς τὰ πρόβατα τὰ ἀπολωλότα οἴκου Ἰσραήλ' — 'eis ta probata ta apololota oikou Israel' — unto the lost sheep of the house of Israel; for the lost sheep of the house of Israel. Both translation options are possible; in the first case, the preposition 'eis' indicates direction, the orientation of Jesus' ministry at that time; in the second — the goal of the ministry.

Matthew XV, 27 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ψιχίων' — 'apo ton psikhion' — of the crumbs. The preposition 'apo' here indicates that it refers to those crumbs leaving the food on the table by falling down. It also indicates that those crumbs are a kind of gift for the little dogs from the children. A very distinct humility of the woman. 'τῶν πιπτόντων ἀπὸ τῆς τραπέζης τῶν κυρίων αὐτῶν' - 'ton piptonton apo tes trapezes ton kyrion auton' — which fall from their masters' table. The prepositions 'apo' indicate that the crumbs fall downwards from the tables. Such a detail points to the sense of Jesus' dialogue with the woman — Jesus shows that when the Lord manifests mercy, He can manifest it even where He as it were was not supposed to. As in the case of the healing of the Roman centurion's servant, Christ emphasizes the woman's faith. We would notice first of all her humility, but the Lord as the Searcher of hearts points to the cause — faith is the cause of her humility (as with the centurion), which is why He praises her faith (and implicitly — the humility stemming from faith).

Matthew XV, 28 — 'ἀπὸ τῆς ὥρας ἐκείνης' — 'apo tes horas ekeines' — from that very hour. Here the preposition 'apo' indicates the starting point of reckoning time, that is, from then on.
