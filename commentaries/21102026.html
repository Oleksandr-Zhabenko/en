Liturgy:
(Colossians I, 18-23)
Colossians I, 18 — 'αὐτός ἐστιν ἡ κεφαλὴ τοῦ σώματος, τῆς ἐκκλησίας' - ‘autos estin he kephale tou somatos, tes ekklesias’ - He is the Head of the body, the Church. Here the Apostle begins to reveal God’s mystery of salvation in a particular way, calling Christ the Head, and the Church the body. Bodily associations indicate that the head is also a part of the body (Christ has a divine and a human nature, being of one nature with us according to human nature), while at the same time the head heads the body, directs the body, and is the part of the body that has a face and therefore receives the greatest honour. Christ not only heads the Church, but is also the Only One Who truly represents it in a Single Person, a Single Hypostasis, a Single Face. The salvation, redemption and liberation of humanity accomplished by Christ, and its consequences, extend to the whole Church.
'πρωτότοκος ἐκ τῶν νεκρῶν' — ‘prototokos ek ton nekron’ - Firstborn from the dead. The preposition ‘ek’ indicates here the place from which Christ’s transition took place in the Resurrection — from the dead to the living. Firstborn indicates that the faithful, rising with Christ, become God’s, the Father’s children by grace, whereas He Himself is the Only Son by nature. 'ἐν πᾶσιν' - ‘en pasin’ - in everything. In what.

Colossians I, 19 — 'ἐν αὐτῷ' - ‘en auto’ - in Him. In Whom. It speaks of the fact that Christ, as the God-Man, unites the whole Church; in Him and through Him all God’s benefactions and promises appear, He accomplishes God’s design, He leads the faithful to salvation (and by Himself), sinners to repentance, raises the fallen, exalts the humble and so on. The Apostle testifies that it pleased God, through Christ, to accomplish everything designed, both what has already been accomplished and what is to come, in particular the Kingdom of God. See a beautiful poem about this at the links:

Colossians I, 20 — 'δι’ αὐτοῦ ἀποκαταλλάξαι τὰ πάντα εἰς αὐτόν' - ‘di autou apokatallaxai ta panta eis auton’ - through Him reconciling all into Himself; through Him reconciling all for Himself; through Him reconciling all into Him; through Him reconciling all for Him. All variants of translation are possible. It speaks of the fact that God in Christ (the Son of God, consubstantial with the Father and the Spirit) brings everything to union and unity with Himself. This union is a union in the direction of Christ, and at the same time this union is a union in the direction of God (of the Son, because He is Christ, of the Father — for whoever honours the Son honours the Father also, of the Spirit — because therein lies the common will of God — of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and the Spirit sanctifies and accomplishes this union in the Church), that is, that union is achieved by moving towards Christ and being united with Him, with God.
'εἰρηνοποιήσας διὰ τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ σταυροῦ αὐτοῦ, δι’ αὐτοῦ εἴτε τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς εἴτε τὰ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς' — ‘eirenopoiesas dia tou haimatos tou staurou autou, di autou eite ta epi tes ges eite ta en tois ouranois’ - having made peace through the blood of His cross, through Him whether that which is on earth, or that which is in the heavens. Christ’s sacrifice is peacemaking, and He Himself is also the Creator of peace, the Creator of the fullness of life. See in particular at the links:

Colossians I, 21 — 'ἐν τοῖς ἔργοις τοῖς πονηροῖς' - ‘en tois ergois tois ponerois’ - in bad (by results; evil, generally bad) deeds.

Colossians I, 22 — 'ἐν τῷ σώματι τῆς σαρκὸς αὐτοῦ διὰ τοῦ θανάτου, παραστῆσαι ὑμᾶς ἁγίους καὶ ἀμώμους καὶ ἀνεγκλήτους κατενώπιον αὐτοῦ' - ‘en to somati tes sarkos autou dia tou thanatou, parastesai hymas hagious kai amomous kai anenkletous katenopion autou’ - in the body of flesh through the death to set you beside (Himself, Him) as holy, and unblemished, and without a stain of reproach directly before Him (Himself); by the body of flesh through death to set you beside (Himself, Him) as holy, and unblemished, and without a stain of reproach directly before Him (Himself). Both variants of translation are possible. The phrase is difficult to understand, to a certain extent mysterious, since at times body and flesh act as synonyms, or mostly only one of these two words is used. But in this context God’s design becomes visible more deeply, and the explanation can be given in simple words.
The most contextual understanding is that by the body of flesh / in the body of flesh means by body the Church, and by flesh — human nature with its infirmities and the sufferings of the Incarnate Son of God Himself, the Lord Jesus Christ. Then the quotation can be paraphrased: in the Church of human infirmity, of the sufferings of Jesus Christ, through death to set you beside… / by the Church of human infirmity, of the sufferings of Jesus Christ, through death to set you beside.... In fact, then, Christ here by sufferings and death sets the faithful beside, by the Church; they become beside in this way, being the Church.
One can also think somewhat differently (but this does not deny the first, rather it supplements it), that such a combination indicates how God through the Incarnation saves human nature damaged by sin. Body is then the name for the state of nature when it is full of God’s grace, divinised, living according to God’s will, and flesh — the human damage and infirmity assumed by God. In Christ, thus, human nature is simultaneously in both senses during His earthly life — and the salvation of the flesh is accomplished through the divinised body. And in a broader sense, the salvation of humanity is accomplished through the Body of Christ — the Church. Here the verse also says literally holy, blameless and such as cannot be accused. These are certain degrees of holiness and of approach to God — saints may have shortcomings, whereas there are those who can hardly be reproached for their shortcomings, and there are also those who truly cannot be reproached for their shortcomings, for example, the Mother of God, the All-immaculate Virgin Mary.

Colossians I, 23 — 'ἀπὸ τῆς ἐλπίδος τοῦ εὐαγγελίου' - ‘apo tes elpidos tou euangeliou’ - from the hope of the Gospel. The preposition ‘apo’ indicates here that it concerns complete falling away, abandonment, forsaking, which the Apostle hopes the faithful will not do. 'ἐν πάσῃ κτίσει τῇ ὑπὸ τὸν οὐρανόν' - ‘en pase ktisei te hypo ton ouranon’ - in all creation under heaven. To whom and where. The preposition ‘hypo’ here with the accusative means precisely under.
