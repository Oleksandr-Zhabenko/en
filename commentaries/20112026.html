Liturgy:
(2 Thessalonians III, 6-18)
2 Thessalonians III, 6 — 'ἐν ὀνόματι τοῦ κυρίου' — 'en onomati tou kyriou' — in the name of the Lord; by the name of the Lord. Both translation options are possible and complement one another — the Apostle commands both as Christians and for Christ's sake. 'ἀπὸ παντὸς ἀδελφοῦ' - 'apo pantos adelphou' — from every brother; any brother. The preposition 'apo' indicates the cessation of fellowship. 'παρ’ ἡμῶν' - 'par hemon' — from beside us. The preposition 'para' is here in its form before a following vowel together with the genitive case. Such a construction is used regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father, the Eternal Council of the Most Holy Trinity, God's design, and also regarding the human nature of Jesus Christ. See the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/uk/commentaries/Pryjmennyky.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/uk/commentaries/vystup-2025-hypo-genitive-Romans-XIII_1.pdf
Here it indicates that the apostles hand down this tradition by the action of the Holy Spirit, in accordance with God's design.

2 Thessalonians III, 7, 11 — 'ἐν ὑμῖν' — 'en hymin' — among you / in you. Among whom.

2 Thessalonians III, 8 — 'παρά τινος' — 'para tinos' — from beside someone. The preposition 'para' here takes the genitive case. Such a construction is used regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father, the Eternal Council of the Most Holy Trinity, God's design, and also regarding the human nature of Jesus Christ. See the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/uk/commentaries/Pryjmennyky.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/uk/commentaries/vystup-2025-hypo-genitive-Romans-XIII_1.pdf
Here it indicates the substantial nature of such a gift from someone; that is, there is a significant difference between voluntary labour and necessary labour — the former, following the example of the apostles, can be prompted by the action of the Holy Spirit, Who encouraged it. 'ἐν κόπῳ καὶ μόχθῳ' — 'en kopo kai mokhtho' — with labour and travail / in toil and weariness.

2 Thessalonians III, 9 — 'εἰς τὸ μιμεῖσθαι' — 'eis to mimeisthai' — to follow / for imitation. The preposition 'eis' indicates the goal of the action here.

2 Thessalonians III, 12 — 'ἐν κυρίῳ Ἰησοῦ Χριστῷ' — 'en kyrio Iesou CHristo' — by our Lord Jesus Christ / in the Lord Jesus Christ; through the Lord Jesus Christ. All translation options are possible, although the second and third have the preference, as the apostles rather exhort for Christ's sake than by Him Himself.

2 Thessalonians III, 16 — 'ἐν παντὶ τρόπῳ' — 'en panti tropo' — by all means / in every way (which is genuinely fitting for this). Both translation options are possible.

2 Thessalonians III, 17 — 'ἐν πάσῃ ἐπιστολῇ' — 'en pase epistole' — in every epistle.

The Apostle Paul reminds them once more that it is improper to do nothing and wander from place to place in search of amusement. As an example, he cites the apostles themselves, and himself in particular, as a model of diligence.
The mention of authority (exousia) signifies that the Lord permitted ministers of Christ to live from their ministry (this permission itself is termed authority). At the same time, Paul, with an educational purpose, also served as an example of industriousness, even though he could have appealed to this permission.
If anyone was unwilling to accept the word of the apostles, whether spoken or written, they sought to bring such a person to reason — first and foremost by interacting with them so that they might understand it was wrong, and above all in a brotherly (or sisterly) manner. Broadly speaking, one may consider that if a person does not live according to the good example of others, there is reason to encourage them toward it among brothers and sisters, yet no such stance ought ever to become grounds for enmity.
The epistle concludes with a greeting and blessing signed by the Apostle Paul's own hand (the text of the letter was most likely written down by Silvanus, or possibly Timothy, apostles of the Seventy [2 Thessalonians I, 1]).

(Luke XIII, 31-35)
Luke XIII, 31 — 'Ἐν αὐτῇ τῇ ὥρᾳ' — 'En aute te hora' — The same day / In that very hour. Set expression.

Luke XIII, 34 — 'ὑπὸ τὰς πτέρυγας' — 'hypo tas pterygas' — under her wings / under the wings. The preposition 'hypo' here takes the accusative case and signifies that the Lord desires to gather Israel under His wings (the Lord is compared to an angel — likely the Archistrategos Michael, who is the patron and protector of the Israelite people in particular).

Luke XIII, 35 — 'ἐν ὀνόματι' — 'en onomati' — in the name; by the name. Both translation options are possible. See more at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/07042026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/15042025.html

The beginning of the reading is a special tradition of Luke. It is understood that Jesus does not fear Herod, but fulfils His ministry. He also manifests His intention to go to Jerusalem — unto voluntary sufferings, death, and Resurrection. Here Jesus also appears as a Prophet, and the words concerning the perishing of a prophet reflect the fact that prophets suffered primarily at the hands of the authorities, and authority was concentrated in the capital, which was Jerusalem (albeit subjugated to the Romans).
Fox — it is also noted that in Semitic culture this denotes primarily a predatory beast rather than a cunning one, as, for instance, in Ukrainian. Herod — who ordered the execution of John the Baptist and cared neither for the fate of individuals nor for that of the nation (recall that when Jesus was brought before Herod for trial, the latter merely wanted to see some miracle and was uninterested in Jesus' fate as such) — fully merits this figurative comparison. It is a figurative comparison: that is, Jesus might have stated more fully, Herod, who is very much like a fox…, yet Jesus' words are briefer, more decisive, and more prophetic — the words of the Lord.

(Luke IX, 37-43)
Luke IX, 37 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ ὄρους' — 'apo tou orous' — down from the hill / from the mountain. The preposition 'apo' indicates that they left the mountain or hill.

Luke IX, 38 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ ὄχλου' — 'apo tou okhlou' — of the company / from among the crowd. The preposition 'apo' indicates that the man stood out from his surroundings.

Luke IX, 39 — 'ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ' — 'ap autou' — from him. The preposition 'apo' in its form before a following vowel indicates here the departure of the evil spirit.

A miracle performed by Jesus after the Transfiguration on the mountain before His chosen disciples.
It also appears in the other synoptic evangelists, including with additional details. Luke, as a physician, pays greater attention to the symptoms of possession.
