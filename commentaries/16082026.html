(Matthew XVIII, 23-35)
Direct continuation of the reading discussed at the link:

Matthew XVIII, 24 — 'μυρίων' — 'myrion' — ten thousand; an innumerable quantity. Traditionally translated as ten thousand, although the point is not in the exact number, but in its innumerability, the impossibility of even counting it completely.

Matthew XVIII, 26, 29 — 'μακροθύμησον' — 'makrothymeson' — be patient, long-suffering. The requests of the servants differ by only one word — the first debtor says: I will pay you all, and the second — I will pay you. One can notice that the first debtor overestimates his strength, exaggerating his capabilities in words. Realistically, he cannot pay back what is impossible even to count. Nevertheless, the master of the servants does not catch on details, but manifests general mercy.

It is known that a talent was worth 6,000 denarii, and a denarius was a worker's daily wage. A myriad of talents is an astronomical sum (if considered to be 10,000, as it is often translated, then it is 60,000,000 denarii, which means if a year has 365 days (on average slightly more — almost 365.25), then it will be… 164,271-plus years without weekends and breaks), and if considered a figurative expression for innumerability, then the number cannot even be calculated at all.

Matthew XVIII, 30 — 'εἰς φυλακὴν' — 'eis phylaken' — into prison. Where to. Indicates a certain degree of the application of force here (factually violence in this case).

Matthew XVIII, 32 — 'πονηρέ' — 'ponere' — wicked (by the outcome of activity, in the summary, as a final result), evil.

Matthew XVIII, 35 — 'ἐὰν μὴ ἀφῆτε ἕκαστος τῷ ἀδελφῷ αὐτοῦ ἀπὸ τῶν καρδιῶν ὑμῶν' — 'hean me aphete hekastos to adelpho autou apo ton kardion hymon' — if you from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses; if you do not forgive (release, send away) every one his brother from your hearts. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that it refers to leaving behind or bestowing. One can forgive so that tension, the desire for punishment, retribution or revenge, bad thoughts and the like completely leave, abandon the hearts, that is, free them from all sinfulness of such a kind. Or one can forgive as a gift — that is, the emphasis then is on bestowing forgiveness of a fault or faults upon someone (in Ukrainian there is even a phrase: Даруйте!.. [Forgive/Grant me!], which conveys this meaning, though used regarding minor inconveniences or similar things). When the apostles wish the faithful God's grace, they also use the preposition 'apo', which indicates God's grace as God's gift, bestowing, endowing. All of God's gifts are not self-valuable in isolation; they witness to and create relationships with God, responsibility before Him, meaning it is impossible to receive a gift from God and not enter into a certain relationship with Him. Perhaps someone does not want to enter into a relationship with God, but it is impossible not to enter into it, because everything good one way or another is God's gift. I will also say here about the plural regarding your hearts — Christ encourages not only personal forgiveness, but also group, Church, communal forgiveness. There is also an implicit additional meaning present in one of the senses of the word release / forgive — it can refer to the heart of each person and… that brother who sinned. That is, proper forgiveness can enable not only the one who sincerely forgives to get rid of prompts to evil, but also that person who sinned can receive liberation from the inclination to sin, from repetition and passion. And this joining of two hearts in release / forgiveness can be the most valuable thing in forgiveness itself, and, of course, Christ calls to such. That is, take care that forgiveness brings release from evil to all participants in the situation. I will also say that this topic is closely connected with the topic of overcoming anger, pride, and love.

Matthew V, 23-24 are important as testimony that an obstacle to Communion is lack of peace with a person. Yes, it is good to partake of the Holy Gifts of Christ and the Sacraments of His Body and Blood, there is His commandment, but if a person is not at peace with brothers and sisters in faith, unless the lack of peace is caused by the necessity of fulfilling God's commandments and God's will (that is, when the person is not actually guilty of what the brother or sister accuses them of), then a lack of peace of this kind is a Gospel obstacle to celebrating the Eucharist for clergy, and for the faithful — an obstacle to Communion (and more literally — even to participating in the Liturgy of the Faithful). I will add a few more words about the word 'ἀφῆτε' — 'aphete' — you release, let go, lose, free; shatter (as an army in battle); forgive debts; leave, let go (it also has other meanings that do not seem relevant in this context, in general the word is very polysemous). I will dwell on the polysemy of this word. In general, its clearest meaning in the New Testament is forgive, and the form of forgiveness (how it ought to be, how it manifests itself) is release, allow to go. That is, in the most general sense, Gospel forgiveness and commandments concern the remission / release of sins and debts, the possibility for them not to touch ourselves, becoming invulnerable to them by reason of their missing past us, etc. That is, an unforgiven sin in this sense hits the person who does not forgive, and thereby causes them pain. And to forgive means to make it so that the sin misses, passes by without touching. This corresponds to dodging a strike in martial arts — sin aims at a person, and the one who forgives it in the Gospel sense dodges the impact. But this same word also carries the meaning of routing an army in battle or war — that is, in certain cases forgiveness is equivalent to a complete final victory in war or battle. Sometimes forgiveness can mean separation, that is, being in safety, not simply dodging the impact, but moving to a safe place. And so on; that is, Christian forgiveness is polysemous and has many manifestations and forms.

In general, the master in the parable implies God (most often understood as the Father, but one can speak of the Trinity in general). And the debtors are people. Debts are sins, everything that violates or does not align with, does not correspond to God's will and design.

Of Not-Made-by-Hands Image (Mandylion) of Jesus Christ:
(Colossians I, 12-18; Luke IX, 51-56; X, 22-24)
Colossians I, 12 — 'εἰς τὴν μερίδα τοῦ κλήρου τῶν ἁγίων ἐν τῷ φωτί' — 'eis ten merida tou klerou ton hagion en to photi' — for a portion of the inheritance of the saints in light. The preposition 'eis' indicates the purpose here — the Father made the faithful capable of becoming saints in light, in God's grace, capable of inheriting all of God's promises, particularly becoming children of God and inheriting blessed eternal life in love.

Colossians I, 13 — 'ὃς ἐρύσατο ἡμᾶς ἐκ τῆς ἐξουσίας τοῦ σκότους καὶ μετέστησεν εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ υἱοῦ τῆς ἀγάπης αὐτοῦ' — 'hos erysato hemas ek tes exousias tou skotous kai metestesen eis ten basileian tou hyiou tes agapes autou' — Who delivered us from the power of darkness, and transferred us into the kingdom of His beloved Son. A typical pair of prepositions 'ek'-'eis' indicates movement — from where and to where.

Colossians I, 14 — 'ἐν ᾧ ἔχομεν τὴν ἀπολύτρωσιν, τὴν ἄφεσιν τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν' — 'en ho ekhomen ten apolytrosin ten aphesin ton hamartion' — in Whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins. In Whom. See also about the translation nuances at the link:
https://share.gemini.google/6xtmSnGMiXNv

Colossians I, 15 — ‘εἰκών’ — ‘eikon’ — image (hence the word icon, as well as a direct connection with the Image Not-Made-by-Hands, the memory of the translation of which is celebrated today).

Colossians I, 16 — 'ἐν αὐτῷ ἐκτίσθη τὰ πάντα ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς καὶ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς' — 'en auto ektisthe ta panta en tois ouranois kai epi tes ges' — in Him were all things created, in heaven and on earth; by Him were all things created, in heaven and on earth. Both translation options are possible, with the first emphasizing that all creation conceived by the Father received in the Son as the Primary Source, particularly all authority, as the Archetype, as the Pattern. The second option points to the Father Who creates by the Son, the Word (it is worth adding and by the Spirit). In this, the Son acts as the same Creator as the Father and the Spirit. 'τὰ πάντα δι’ αὐτοῦ καὶ εἰς αὐτὸν ἔκτισται' - 'ta panta di autou kai eis auton ektistai' — all things were created through Him and for Him. — Consequently, the Son is uncreated, is God. The preposition 'eis' indicates the purpose here. See also at the link:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/uk/commentaries/vystup-2025-hypo-genitive-Romans-XIII_1.pdf

Colossians I, 17 — 'τὰ πάντα ἐν αὐτῷ συνέστηκεν' — 'ta panta en auto synesteken' — all things consist in Him; all things are held together by Him. Similarly to verse 16, both translation options are possible. The Son of God as God holds everything in existence, gives life, and the like. See also at the links:
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/25042026.html
https://oleksandr-zhabenko.github.io/en/commentaries/18042026.html

Colossians I, 18 — 'πρωτότοκος ἐκ τῶν νεκρῶν' — 'prototokos ek ton nekron' — Firstborn from the dead. The preposition 'ek' indicates here from where the transition of Christ occurred in the Resurrection — from the dead to the living. Firstborn indicates that the faithful, rising with Christ, become God's, the Father's children by grace, whereas He Himself is the Only Son by nature. 'ἐν πᾶσιν' - 'en pasin' — in all things. In what.

The reading from the Apostle contains thanksgiving to God the Father and an exposition of faith regarding the Lord Jesus Christ.

The word image ('εἰκών'), from which the word icon originates, also means likeness. Likeness here by NO means implies that the Son of God differs from the Father in essence while being similar to Him — no, something else is intended here. Paul means that the invisible Father became visible in the Son; the Son reveals, shows, represents the Father to people and creation, and by His likeness as Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Son of God, corresponds in everything to the Father. In Him there is nothing that would not align; moreover, there is nothing that would introduce inconsistency with the Father, but everything in both natures harmoniously reflects and reveals the Father. By reason of the human nature of the Incarnate Son of God, Paul does not use a word that would imply equality with the Father (which the Son has as Consubstantial with the Father and the Spirit in Divinity), because he writes about both natures in Christ simultaneously.

Verse 16 is also spoken of in other places in the New Testament, particularly in the Gospel of John. Angellic ranks (some of them) are listed.

And Christ is the Beginning for everything good in creation. Firstborn regarding the Resurrection — also implies that as the Son of God receives essence from the Father in Divinity (which is called begotten of the Father), so His Resurrection is testimony that He is God. In the New Testament regarding the Resurrection of Christ, forms are used in parallel such as the Father raised the Son and Christ Himself rose, which do not contradict each other, but reveal more deeply the meaning of the event in view of what has just been written here.

The Son is the image of the Father (the invisible God), begotten before all creation (uncreated), is the Creator, for through Him (the Word) and in Him (as the Goal, as the destination, that is, all creation is directed by God towards the Son of God, everything must draw closer to Him) all things were created. This confession echoes: I am Alpha and Omega, the Beginning and the End (the Goal) (Revelation I, 8, 17-18 ).

Luke IX, 51, 53 — 'ἐν τῷ συμπληροῦσθαι' — 'en to symplerousthai' — difficult to translate, literally similar to when [the days] were fully to be fulfilled. When. 'εἰς Ἱερουσαλήμ' - 'eis Hierousalem' — into Jerusalem. Where to.

Luke IX, 52 — 'εἰς πόλιν' — 'eis polin' — into a city (settlement). Where to.

Luke IX, 54 — 'ἀπὸ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ' — 'apo tou ouranou' — from the heaven. The preposition 'apo' indicates that fire would leave heaven, and this in turn indicates that the mood and desire of the disciples were far from heavenly. The words about Elijah are absent in many manuscripts. Likewise the words of Jesus' rebuke quoted further.

Luke IX, 55-56 — in many ancient manuscripts of the Gospel, Jesus' words are absent.

The lesson regarding Jesus' gentleness — if one thinks about how to help people achieve salvation, then one becomes gentle and humane.

Luke IX, 56 — 'εἰς ἑτέραν κώμην' — 'eis heteran komen' — into another village. Where to.

It is not part of the apostolic (Christ's) mission regarding preaching to condemn people, particularly to destruction and eternal torment. If the apostles witness to perishing, it is for people's awareness, and also for understanding subsequent events, particularly regarding God's righteous judgment upon unrepentant sinners who rejected God's great mercy in order to commit their sins. A similar commandment is repeated regarding the Pharisees (let them alone: they be blind leaders of the blind), as well as regarding leaving cities and settlements that completely rejected the Gospel. The apostolic response to complete rejection is not a curse or condemnation, but a complete rupture of relations.

Luke X, 22 — 'ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός' — 'hypo tou patros' — by the Father. Here the preposition 'hypo' is with the genitive case and indicates the passive voice of the participle delivered. It has two meanings here — delivery irrespective of the Incarnation, and delivery regarding the human nature of the already Incarnate Son of God. Both in Divinity the Son of God receives everything from the Father, of Whom He is begotten eternally, and according to human nature He also receives everything from the Father. The Incarnate Son of God as man possesses all God's gifts that are provided by God for people — particularly He is King, Prophet, High Priest, Christ, Savior, Deliverer, Conqueror, and the like. It must also be understood that some of these gifts of God are not properly those that God would have wished the Son of God to have when He created the world and people; for example, God did not want people to sin when He created them, but knew as Omniscient that they would sin. Then He sent the Son to save them, and thus, as Savior, Deliverer, Who suffered for us and the like, the Son of God became as man, receiving these gifts also in view of what happened to creation, which God did not want for creation.
