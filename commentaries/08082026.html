Liturgy:
(Romans XV, 30-33)
Romans XV, 30 — 'συναγωνίσασθαί μοι ἐν ταῖς προσευχαῖς ὑπὲρ ἐμοῦ πρὸς τὸν θεόν' — 'synagonisasthai moi en tais proseukhais hyper emou pros ton theon' — take part in the struggle, help, fight on the same side together with me in prayers for me to God. That is, pray together with me, although this may resemble a struggle or contest. That is, pray even in difficult circumstances.

Romans XV, 31 — 'ἵνα ῥυσθῶ ἀπὸ τῶν ἀπειθούντων ἐν τῇ Ἰουداίᾳ' — 'hina rystho apo ton apeithounton en te Ioudaia' — that I may be delivered from the disobedient in Judea. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that it is a matter of complete deliverance, that is, so that those people would cease to be disobedient to God. 'ἡ διακονία μου ἡ εἰς Ἱερουσαλήμ' - 'he diakonia mou he eis Hierousalem' — my service which is for Jerusalem. That is, it is about service for the faithful, and this means service to the spiritual Jerusalem, that is, the Kingdom of God.

Romans XV, 32 — 'ἐν χαρᾷ' — 'en khara' — in joy. How.

Here it is about prayer. Paul asks for shared prayer with him. I note that here it is clearly visible that shared prayer can also be one carried out by people who are at a great distance from one another. Distance is not an obstacle to communion in prayer, just as it is not an obstacle when people gathered in one place are nevertheless separated by a small space from one another. Nevertheless, such communion in prayer at a distance is not equivalent to praying together in one place. This is especially relevant in the time of the spread of broadcasts via technology. For the most complete prayer in common, what is important is not only a shared place of prayer, but also personal relationships, which are possible to the fullest extent only in the immediate presence of the participants. Great distance introduces certain limitations on place and relationships, which is why it does not destroy communion of prayer, but is also not equal to praying together in one place.

To be delivered from the disobedient — that is, so that the Lord would preserve him from the persecutions of non-Christians, so that everything would be peaceful.

Acceptable to the saints — that is, to the faithful Christians, well received by them.

(Matthew XVII, 24-XVIII, 4)
Matthew XVII, 24 — 'εἰς Καφαρναούμ' — 'eis Kapharnaoum' — into Capernaum. Where to.

Matthew XVII, 25 — 'εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν' — 'eis ten oikian' — into the house; home. Where to. 'ἀπὸ τίνων λαμβάνουσιν τέλη ἢ κῆνσον; ἀπὸ τῶν υἱῶν αὐτῶν ἢ ἀπὸ τῶν ἀλλοτρίων' - 'apo tinon lambanousin tele e kenson? apo ton hyion auton e apo ton allotrion' — from whom do they take taxes or tribute? from their own sons, or from strangers?. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that taxes and tribute are taken by the rulers (authorities) completely, entirely. In those times a greater portion than now was designated for the personal expenses of the rulers. Now a significantly larger portion goes to general state needs (ideally almost all revenue to the budget).

Matthew XVII, 26 — 'ἀπὸ τῶν ἀλλοτρίων' — 'apo ton allotrion' — from strangers. See just above regarding verse 25.

Matthew XVII, 27 — 'πορευθεὶς εἰς θάλασσαν βάλε ἄγκιστρον' — 'poreutheis eis thalassan bale ankistron' — go to the sea, cast a hook; go, cast a hook into the sea. Both translation variants are possible, but the combined action that Christ tells to do is more likely to be seen here (combined actions can be more effective than single ones). Where to.

Here the question and answer show that taxes are collected not from one's own children, but from others. Therefore God's children (in particular the apostles) are not obligated to pay taxes on the temple, that is, to give money as a tax to God. All the more the Son of God Himself, Who is God. Peter to a certain extent hurried to give an answer to those who asked.

In the words of the Gospel of John As the Father has life in Himself; so HAS HE GIVEN TO THE SON TO HAVE life in Himself (John V, 26) it is shown that divine, correct fatherhood connected with 'patria' — gives to have in oneself that which is passed on.

Compared to the more famous story with the question of the lawyers, much less attention is paid to this story, because in it there is much more freedom and a call to it. But precisely this story speaks directly about what Christ Himself thinks on this matter. Moreover, here one can think about that from which in this story Christ precisely has more freedom in presenting His opinion than in the story with the cunning question about paying tax to Caesar. Christ in that situation had much more limited possibilities for freedom of expressing His opinion in such a way that He could express it calmly, clearly, and distinctly. Of course, as the Lord He said everything correctly, but in the aspect of the possibility to express Himself He had less opportunity to speak openly; whereas in the situation with Peter — everything is the opposite — Peter is one of the closest disciples and, of course, Peter would support very different views of His Teacher, and he certainly would not want to accuse Christ.

Christ speaks about how people are free, and therefore it is worth thinking about how not to limit the freedom of people, and the state must work on this, it must move in the direction of increasing the freedom of people.

Another somewhat paradoxical vector of thought should be how to receive the filling of the treasury through miracles, or to put it more simply, through that which does not require huge efforts. As Peter received the stater miraculously by performing a few simple actions, so also Christians should try to embody this Gospel ideal in life. An authority that tries to do this is more Evangelical in this matter. If one compares Russian authority in this with others, the former has no advantage, but rather a lag.
