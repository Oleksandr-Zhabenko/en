Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians VIII, 7-15)
2 Corinthians VIII, 7 — 'ἐν παντί' — 'en panti' — in everything; in all things. Both translation options are possible. 'τῇ ἐξ ὑμῶν ἐν ἡμῖν ἀγάπῃ' - 'te ex hymon en hemin agape' — in your love to us; by that love which is from you in us. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following 'hy' indicates here that it refers to manifestations of love from the Corinthians to the apostles. 'ἐν ταύτῃ τῇ χάριτι' - 'en taute te khariti' — in this grace; by this grace. Again, both translation options are possible. The word grace can also be translated here as good work, good deeds, charity, benefactions.

2 Corinthians VIII, 10 — 'ἐν τούτῳ' — 'en touto' — in this. 'ἀπὸ πέρυσι' - 'apo perysi' — since last year. Set expression. The preposition 'apo' indicates here the starting point of time regarding an important change.

2 Corinthians VIII, 11-12 — 'προθυμία' — 'prothymia' — readiness to act, willingness, intention to act, zeal, motivation.

2 Corinthians VIII, 11 — 'ἐκ τοῦ ἔχειν' — 'ek tou ekhein' — out of that which you have; from what one has. The preposition 'ek' emphasizes the essential nature of the state of wealth in this matter, that this question cannot be ignored.

2 Corinthians VIII, 13 — 'ἐξ ἰσότητος' — 'ex isotetos' — by an equality. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following vowel indicates here that equality and its pursuit act as the source of that state. 'ἐν τῷ νῦν καιρῷ' - 'en to nyn kairo' — at this present time. 'εἰς τὸ ἐκείνων ὑστέρημα' — [your abundance may be a supply] for their want; for [covering] their lack. The preposition 'eis' emphasizes the dynamics of help — the richer can and are called to support the poorer, and also indicates a possible good use of surplus, the purpose of significant material success — to enrich not only the owners, but also others.

2 Corinthians VIII, 14 — 'εἰς τὸ ὑμῶν ὑστέρημα' — 'eis to hymon hysterema' — for your want; for [covering] your lack. Analogous to what has just been written above. It is clear that there is no sense in doing something literally for lack, but it refers to covering it, reducing it.

Paul tells the Corinthians to complete, to accomplish that which they showed readiness to do good.
Christ's humility prompts the faithful to manifest deeds of mercy, particularly to the needy and poor.
Verse 12 means that if someone does good for Christ's sake, what they have, when gifted to others, will be pleasing and valuable, and what they do not have will not be an obstacle. An example of so-called positive thinking — in good deeds for the Lord's sake, one needs to look at willingness, motivation, and also value what is given. If in such a case something is lacking, there is no need to emphasize this lack.
Further Paul speaks of social equality in a special sense — he that had gathered much had nothing over, and he that had gathered little had no lack. Factually, these words can be literally understood such that the surplus of the rich should be given to those who have a lack. At the same time, Paul does not obligate them to do so, but prompts, speaks of it so that those who have sincere love for others would act precisely so. It is also worth adding that here it refers mainly to foodstuffs, perhaps clothing. Food in the South then spoiled quickly; the maximum that could be stored for a long time was grain (with proper organization — for years). Refrigerators, preservation, and similar technologies simply did not exist; there was not even a real hint of them. Therefore, the presence of food surpluses all the more prompted sharing them, and unused ones could become a problem of waste rather than an asset. This shifts the apostle's words from seemingly socialist to practical, rational, good, and Christian ones.
It must also be said that in the times of the apostles there was no modern social security and taxes for social needs, so such care was entirely voluntary.

(Mark III, 6-12)
Mark III, 7 — 'εἰς τὴν θάλασσαν' — 'eis ten thalassan' — to the sea. Where to. 'ἀπὸ τῆς Γαλιλαίας καὶ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἰουداίας' - 'apo tes Galilaias kai apo tes Ioudaias' — from Galilee, and from Judea. From where. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that those people completely left their native places to meet Jesus.

Mark III, 8 — 'καὶ ἀπὸ Ἱεροσολύμων καὶ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἰδουμαίας καὶ πέραν τοῦ Ἰορδάνου καὶ περὶ Τύρον καὶ Σιδῶνα' — 'kai apo Hierosolymon kai apo tes Idoumaias kai peran tou Iordanou kai peri Tyron kai Sidona' — and from Jerusalem, and from Idumea, and from beyond Jordan; and they about Tyre and Sidon. The preposition 'apo' indicates here that people left Jerusalem and Idumea — both the centre of Old Testament spirituality and the periphery — meaning that everyone came to Christ from everywhere.

After Jesus healed the man's withered hand, the Pharisees took counsel with the Herodians to destroy Him.
