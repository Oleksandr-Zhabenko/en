Liturgy:
(1 Corinthians I, 26-29)
1 Corinthians I, 29 — 'ὅπως μὴ καυχήσηται πᾶσα σὰρξ ἐνώπιον τοῦ θεοῦ' — 'hopos me kaukhesetai pasa sarx enopion tou theou' — that no flesh should boast in His presence (before the face of God, in God's presence). Here flesh refers to that which belongs to this age, the temporary, temporal, much as Christ also says that flesh and blood cannot inherit the Kingdom of God. The apostle says that no temporal thing can be boasted of before God, for the good is from Him, and everything else is not good, and all the less should one boast of it. But true glorying is in the Lord, regarding that which is a manifestation of His presence and participation; generally speaking — that which is granted together with, through, or in accord with God's grace (God's graces in the broad sense of the word).

One can also put it this way: Paul writes that the true ground for boasting must be first of all the Lord Himself. For if the Lord is not the cause of boasting, then a person can easily fall into various sins — first of all pride and vainglory, but also any others. Whereas if the Lord is the cause of boasting, then such boasting is at the same time a glorification of God and leads to joy in God. This does not mean that one can only boast directly of God by naming His name, but one can boast of God indirectly — by acknowledging and thanking God for what He bestows, which means one must discover, remember, preserve, develop, and multiply its divine bestowed nature in praise when it is genuine. A believing person can be praised even by unbelieving people, for example, for their virtues, achievements, labour, etc., and the believing person ought not to lose connection with God, but develop and multiply God's gift, and then such praise with such an attitude will bring the person closer to God. The opposite of this is the state when a person does not value the divine bestowed nature of what they possess, and then, even having praise from people, they will not receive praise from God.
And one more thought — it is good (and can even be better) to glorify God directly, and at times this is simply necessary. See also:

(Matthew XX, 29-34)
Matthew XX, 29 — 'ἀπὸ Ἱερειχώ' — 'apo Iereikho' — from Jericho. The preposition 'apo' emphasizes that they were leaving Jericho, moving further on.

Matthew XX, 30 — 'παρὰ τὴν ὁδόν' — 'para ten hodon' — to the proximity with the way. The preposition 'para' here is used with the accusative case and indicates that those two blind men sat near the road earlier so that people would pay attention to them (their alms depended on it).

As I wrote earlier, the words and request of the blind men are effectively a Gospel recording of the Jesus Prayer, for Son of David is the Messiah, Christ; thus, in fact, the Jesus Prayer combines the request of the blind men, Peter's confession, and the publican's prayer (combining all three, encompassing very much).
