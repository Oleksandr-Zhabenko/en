Liturgy:
(2 Corinthians VII, 1-10)
2 Corinthians VII, 1 — 'ἀπὸ παντὸς μολυσμοῦ σαρκὸς καὶ πνεύματος' — 'apo pantos molysmou sarkos kai pneumatos' — from all filthiness of flesh and spirit. The preposition 'apo' emphasizes complete, utter cleansing. The pair flesh-spirit represents opposite components, meaning that it is actually about the cleansing of the entire human nature, complete and whole. 'ἐπιτελοῦντες ἁγιωσύνην ἐν φόβῳ θεοῦ' - 'epitelountes hagiosynen en phobo theou' — perfecting holiness in the fear of God. The faithful, through God's mercy, generosity, redemption, salvation, and the like, already possess a certain grace-filled holiness, but their ascetic effort of cleansing can lead them to perfected holiness, to actual full sanctity.

2 Corinthians VII, 2 — 'Χωρήσατε ἡμᾶς· οὐδένα ἠδικήσαμεν, οὐδένα ἐφθείραμεν, οὐδένα ἐπλεονεκτήσαμεν' — 'CHoReSaTe hemas oudena edikesamen, oudena ephtheiramen, oudena epleonektesamen' — Make place for us (in your hearts)! We have wronged nobody (that is, have not hurt feelings, have not prompted bad thoughts), we have corrupted nobody (have not worsened anyone's condition), we have taken advantage of nobody(without worsening state and pain, but to a certain extent as if some object or mere item, for a purpose contrary to true love and Christ's doctrine — that is, there was no so-called objectification of personality) (note: according to Strong). Here is my dialogue with Google Gemini Flash-3.6. I noticed a connection with Christ's teaching on marriage and celibacy, and Gemini confirmed this meaning. I present the dialogue in full, as it is very interesting overall.
I asked to provide a translation of the definition, origin, and usage of the three key words of this passage. Google Gemini replied:
(ἀδικέω / adikeó)
Definition: to act unjustly towards, injure, harm.
Origin: From adikos; to be unjust, i.e. (actively) do wrong (morally, socially or physically).
Usage: hurt, injure, be an offender, be unjust, (do, suffer, take) wrong.
(φθείρω / phtheiró)
Definition: to corrupt, spoil, destroy, ruin.
Origin: Probably strengthened from phthio (to pine or waste); properly, to shrivel or wither, i.e. To spoil (by any process) or (generally) to ruin (especially figuratively, by moral influences, to deprave).
Usage: corrupt (self), defile, destroy.
(πλεονεκτέω / pleonekteó)
Definition: to take advantage of, overreach, defraud.
Origin: From pleonektes; to be covetous, i.e. (by implication) to over-reach.
Usage: get an advantage, defraud, make a gain.
Next I wrote that all this needs to be especially avoided and guarded against when it comes to marriage. Google Gemini replied:
Yes, this is a very accurate and deep pastoral observation on the text of 2 Corinthians VII, 2, where the Apostle Paul uses these three verbs in succession: We have wronged no man (ἀδικέω), we have corrupted no man (φθείρω), we have exploited no man (πλεονεκτέω).
In the context of preparation for marriage and marital life itself, these three concepts unfold as main markers of dangers that should be systematically avoided:
Guarding against ἀδικέω (injustice and wrong):
In marriage this manifests through a lack of parity, contempt for the needs or feelings of the other, egocentrism, and neglect of one's promises. It is any action that violates the commandment of love and inflicts moral or physical harm on the partner.
Guarding against φθείρω (destruction, corruption, and spoiling):
This is the poisoning of relationships from within — through toxicity, manipulation, bitterness, emotional exhaustion, or moral decline (particularly corruption, betrayal, or loss of trust). The text describes a process when something precious gradually withers and shrivels, losing its original beauty and purity.
Guarding against πλεονεκτέω (exploitation, covetousness, and self-interest):
The subtlest and most insidious danger is perceiving another person as a resource or means to achieve one's own goals (comfort, status, finances, or pleasure). It is the temptation to use a partner, stepping over their boundaries for one's own gain.
A marriage built on exposing and eliminating these three destructive models becomes a truly strong space of mutual trust and safety.
I continued the thought that the introduction speaks of place. CHoreo. This word is used by Christ in the teaching on marriage and celibacy. Google Gemini replied:
Yes, this is an important word that links these thoughts. The word χωρέω (chōreō) means to make room, to contain, to accommodate, to receive.
Jesus Christ uses this verb in His fundamental teaching on marriage and celibacy (eunuchs) in the Gospel of Matthew XIX, 11-12:
But He said unto them, All men cannot receive [χωροῦσιν / chōrousin] this saying, save they to whom it is given… He that is able to receive [χωρεῖν / chōrein] it, let him receive [χωρείτω / chōreitō] it!.
In this context, a deep connection between these fragments is revealed:
A place in the heart as the ability to receive/contain: In 2 Corinthians VII, 2 Paul says χωρήσατε ἡμᾶς (receive us / make room for us in your heart). In order to accept another person in marriage (or accept God's very design of the insolubilty of marriage), an inner capacity of the heart is needed.
Accepting the holiness of marriage: Christ emphasizes that the high demands of marriage are not merely a legal law, but a spiritual gift and capacity that a person must receive (χωρέω).
Cleansing from destruction: To receive (chōreō) another person or a calling to marriage, the heart must be cleansed of what was spoken of further — from wrong (adikeō), moral spoiling (phtheirō), and extortion or using another (pleonekteō). If the heart is filled with selfishness, there is no room (chōra) for another in it.
I added that one must choose the path that gives room. Google Gemini confirmed:
This is a deep soul and spiritual intuition. To choose the path that gives room is to choose life according to the laws of love, not according to the laws of pressure, possession, or displacement.
When we choose a path that opens up space (space for another person, for God, for inner freedom), the very quality of relationships and existence changes:
From possession to receiving (χωρέω): The chain of exploitation (pleonekteō) tries to capture a person, subdue them, turn them into its resource. The path that gives room calmly says: You exist, and in my life there is space for your personality, your calling, your feelings.
From seizure to respect: To do no wrong (adikeō) and not to corrupt (phtheirō) means not to squeeze another with one's selfishness, not to push them toward degradation, but to leave them air to grow.
The path as an open gate: The ability to receive (chōreō) always requires a certain self-sacrifice from a person — one needs to diminish one's own I, push back the walls of one's private comfort, so that next to it room appears for another.
The path that gives room does not squeeze the heart into a fist, but unfolds it, making it capable of receiving the granted grace and bestowing comfort to those nearby.

2 Corinthians VII, 3 — 'ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις ἡμῶν' — 'en tais kardiais hemon' — in our hearts. Where. 'εἰς τὸ συναποθανεῖν καὶ συνζῆν' - 'eis to synapothanein kai synzen' — unto dying together and living together; to die and live together. The first translation is a loan translation, and the second is according to meaning. The preposition 'eis' indicates here the purpose of the action. The apostle speaks of determination in unity to share ascetic labours, endurance, present life, death, and eternity in the resurrection.

2 Corinthians VII, 4 — 'παρρησία' — 'parresia' — boldness, confidence, openness.

2 Corinthians VII, 5 — 'εἰς Μακεδονίαν' — 'eis Makedonian' — into Macedonia. Where to. 'ἐν παντὶ θλιβόμενοι' - 'en panti thlibomenoi' — troubled on every side. That is, in all difficult circumstances that occurred to the apostle for Christ's sake through his faith and preaching.

2 Corinthians VII, 6, 7 — 'ταπεινούς' — 'tapeinous' — those that are cast down, humble. 'ἐν τῇ παρουσίᾳ' - 'en te parousia' — in the presence; by the coming; in presence. All translation variants are possible; they mutually complement the general picture.

2 Corinthians VII, 7 — 'ἐν τῇ παρακλήσει' — 'en te paraklesei' — in the consolation; by the comfort. Both translation variants are possible.

2 Corinthians VII, 8 — 'ἐν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ' — 'en te epistole' — in the letter; by the letter. Again, both variants are possible.

2 Corinthians VII, 9 — 'εἰς μετάνοιαν' — 'eis metanoian' — unto repentance; for repentance; so that you repented. In meaning, the last translation is more precise and more transparent. 'ἐν μηδενὶ' - 'en medeni' — in nothing. 'ἐξ ἡμῶν' - 'ex hemon' — from us. The preposition 'ek' in its form before a following 'he' indicates that it is about the real cause — that is, so that the apostles would not bring damage to the faithful. Paul manifests love here, for he cares more about the faithful themselves than about desired virtues. Pastorally, this is completely justified.

2 Corinthians VII, 10 — 'εἰς σωτηρίαν' — 'eis soterian' — unto salvation; for salvation; so as to be saved. Again, the last translation is clearer and more transparent.

In verse 8 Paul mentions an earlier letter to the Corinthians. This may precisely be 1 Corinthians, for there it is written particularly about the sins of one of the Corinthians as well as of all.

Paul often uses the words 'παράκλησις' — 'paraklesis' — comfort, consolation and 'παρακαλέω' — 'parakaleo' — to comfort, console, make glad and their derivatives here. From these same words also comes the name of the Holy Spirit — Paraclete, Comforter. That is, where Paul writes about comfort from God, it refers to the action of God's grace. The event itself — the arrival of Titus — can also be called God's gift, but it also implies here that God assists Titus in comforting Paul and those with him; this is a direct action of God's grace. (Mark I, 29-35)
Mark I, 29 — 'ἐκ τῆς συναγωγῆς' — 'ek tes synagoges' — out of the synagogue. The preposition 'ek' indicates from where they went out. 'εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν Σίμωνος καὶ Ἀνδρέου' - 'eis ten oikian Simonos kai Andreou' — into the house of Simon and Andrew. The preposition 'eis' indicates where they came. A typical combination of prepositions in the case of movement.

Mark I, 35 — 'εἰς ἔρημον τόπον' — 'eis eremon topon' — into a solitary (secluded) place. Where to. 'προσηύχετο' — 'proseukheto' — prayed exaltedly; prayed spiritually.

Here, leaping ahead immediately, I will say that the more obvious thought is that the demons felt more and more that Jesus was the Son of God. Yet the question remains unclear. Moreover, the word 'ὅτι', which is used here, can mean both that (they knew Him) and because. That is, Jesus could forbid them both from revealing or showing that they knew Him (at least felt, guessed), and could forbid them because they were speaking. One can also say that whether the demons said they knew or didn't have time, the Lord forbade them to do so.

The sick are called in Greek 'κακῶς ἔχοντας' — literally — those having essential evil; those having something truly bad, that is, by this word severe illnesses are named, although in many other places of Scripture the first of these words means something morally evil. Mark writes very simply here.

When it comes to the causes of illnesses, their connection with sins, it is appropriate to distinguish between illness and evil as such (for example, this is very important when speaking of how illness is not a sin, and when a person is ill or suffers, it is not their sin, or that all illnesses are a consequence of the Fall, but not every illness is a consequence of the sins of the person themselves), but if it concerns human perception — then a person indeed perceives illness as an evil. Mark in this entirely follows human perception.

Also here it speaks of the healing of Peter's mother-in-law, from which one can also know that Peter was married.
